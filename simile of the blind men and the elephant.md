The [parable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parable "Parable") of the **blind men and an elephant** is a story of a group of blind men who have never come across an [elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant "Elephant") before and who learn and imagine what the elephant is like by touching it. Each blind man feels a different part of the elephant's body, but only one part, such as the side or the tusk. They then describe the elephant based on their limited experience and their descriptions of the elephant are different from each other. In some versions, they come to suspect that the other person is dishonest and they come to blows. The moral of the parable is that humans have a tendency to claim absolute truth based on their limited, subjective experience as they ignore other people's limited, subjective experiences which may be equally true.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant#cite_note-snyder12-2) The parable originated in the ancient [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"), from where it has been widely diffused.

The Buddhist text _[Tittha Sutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tittha_Sutta "Tittha Sutta"), Udāna 6.4, Khuddaka Nikaya_,[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant#cite_note-Titthasutta-3) contains one of the earliest versions of the story. The Tittha sutta is dated to around c. 500 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_era "Common era"), during the lifetime of [the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha "Gautama Buddha").[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant#cite_note-Ireland2007p9-4)

An alternative version of the parable describes sighted men, experiencing a large statue on a dark night, or feeling a large object while being blindfolded. They then describe what it is they have experienced. In its various versions, it is a parable that has crossed between many religious traditions and is part of [Jain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism "Jainism"), [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu "Hindu") and [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist "Buddhist") texts of 1st millennium CE or before.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant#cite_note-Griffiths2007p46-5)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant#cite_note-Ireland2007p9-4) The story also appears in 2nd millennium [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufi "Sufi") and [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith") lore. The tale later became well known in Europe, with 19th century American poet [John Godfrey Saxe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Godfrey_Saxe "John Godfrey Saxe") creating [his own version](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Blind_Men_and_the_Elephant "s:The Blind Men and the Elephant") as a poem, with a final verse that explains that the elephant is a metaphor for God, and the various blind men represent religions that disagree on something no one has fully experienced.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_men_and_an_elephant#cite_note-Gardner1995-6) The story has been published in many books for adults and children, and interpreted in a variety of ways.
