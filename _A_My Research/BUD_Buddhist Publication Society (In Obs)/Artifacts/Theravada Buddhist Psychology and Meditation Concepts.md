| Mettā | Loving-kindness / Friendliness | Four Sublime States (Brahma-vihāra) | Universal, unselfish benevolence and friendliness toward all beings. It acts as a solvent for ill-will and a non-assertive super-solvent among virtues. The Buddha likened its protective power to a mother's love for her only child, shielding the meditator from fire, poison, and weapons. It succeeds when ill-will subsides and fails when it produces selfish affection. | [1-68] |
| Dukkha | Suffering / Unsatisfactoriness | Four Noble Truths | The fundamental spiritual problem of existence, ranging from physical pain to the inherent insecurity of all conditioned phenomena. It is compared to a disease, a malady, or an illness needing diagnosis; a poisonous tree; a heavy burden; and the "hither shore" of danger. The Buddha used similes of a man shot with a poisoned arrow, a house on fire, and a blind turtle rising to the surface of a vast ocean to illustrate its prevalence and urgency. | [2, 4, 5, 7-10, 13, 15-20, 23, 27-33, 36-38, 42, 45, 49, 53, 54, 56-60, 62-66, 69-101] |
| Anicca | Impermanence | Three Characteristics (Tilakkhaṇa) | The universal law of flux and constant change where all conditioned things arise and vanish. It is compared to a swiftly flowing river, a flickering candle flame, a flash of lightning, or a bubble on water. The Buddha used the simile of a line drawn on water or a dew-drop at sunrise to show its transience. Understanding it dispels the illusion of permanency and is compared to a man gaining eyesight to see a grimy cloth clearly. | [2, 7-9, 14, 17-21, 29, 31, 32, 37, 38, 42, 45, 49, 53, 56, 60, 63, 65, 71-77, 79, 82, 85, 86, 88, 91, 93, 96, 97, 101-111] |
| Anattā | Non-self / Insubstantiality | Three Characteristics (Tilakkhaṇa) | The doctrine that all phenomena are devoid of a permanent, unchanging soul or self-essence. It is famously illustrated by the simile of a chariot, which exists only as a functional arrangement of parts but has no core essence. Other similes include a wooden puppet moved by strings, a bubble of water, and a mirage. It acts as a medicine for the cognitive error of ego-solidification. | [2, 7-11, 13, 15, 17-20, 24, 27, 29, 31, 32, 36-38, 42, 45, 46, 49, 50, 53, 56, 60, 63, 65, 71-73, 75-77, 79, 85, 86, 88, 89, 91-93, 96-98, 101, 103-105, 108, 109, 111-119] |
| Satipaṭṭhāna | Foundations of Mindfulness | Requisites of Enlightenment | The practice of fourfold awareness (body, feelings, mind, and mental objects). It is described as the "one and only way" for the purification of beings and the realization of Nibbāna. Similes include a gate-keeper at a city gate, a stone pillar steady in a storm, and a rope used by an elephant hunter to tie a wild calf to a post to tame its wandering. | [5, 11, 17-20, 24, 25, 27, 28, 31, 34, 37, 45, 50, 56, 58, 77, 78, 80, 85, 93-95, 101, 106, 113, 114, 120-129] |
| Nibbāna | Extinction / Liberation | Four Noble Truths | The ultimate goal, representing the cessation of craving, hatred, and delusion. It is compared to a lamp's flame going out when fuel is exhausted, the "Further Shore," an island no flood can overwhelm, and a state of nectar or emancipation. It is the unconditioned (asaṅkhata) beyond the cycle of birth and death (saṃsāra). | [2, 7-10, 17, 18, 21, 24, 25, 27-32, 36-38, 42, 44, 53, 54, 58, 60, 66, 68, 69, 76, 78, 82, 85, 86, 90, 94-96, 99, 100, 104, 106, 111, 114, 116, 130-134] |
| Paññā | Wisdom / Insight | Threefold Training | Direct knowledge of reality that penetrates the true nature of existence. It is likened to a flaming sword that severs fetters, a lamp in a dark room, a flash of lightning, or a reaper's sickle. It acts as the crowning virtue and the final good that leads to freedom. It must be balanced with faith to avoid cunning. | [2, 4, 5, 7-10, 13, 15, 25, 39, 52, 60, 77, 79, 103, 119, 120, 135] |
| Karuṇā | Compassion | Four Sublime States (Brahma-vihāra) | Characterized by a "quaking heart" moved by the suffering of others and the wish to alleviate it. It is the antidote to cruelty and is compared to a mother's concern for a sick child or an endless caravan of beings burdened with sorrow. It succeeds when cruelty subsides but fails if it produces sentimental sorrow or self-pity. | [1, 3, 6-12, 14-17, 19, 20, 24, 29, 31, 33, 36-38, 41, 42, 45, 48, 54-57, 59, 62-64, 66, 80, 81, 89, 136] |
| Samādhi | Concentration / Mental Training | Threefold Training | Wholesome unification of the mind on a single object. A concentrated mind is likened to a candle flame shielded from the wind, a polished mirror, or the ridge-pole of a house to which all rafters join. It functions by suppressing the five hindrances and serves as the foundation for the arising of wisdom. | [2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 15, 18, 25, 45, 46, 50, 60, 69, 70, 74, 79, 87, 96, 119, 127, 135, 137] |
| Muditā | Appreciative Joy / Gladness | Four Sublime States (Brahma-vihāra) | Joy felt at the success and prosperity of others; the specific antidote to envy and jealousy. It is compared to a village bell that everyone rings to share good fortune or a divine smile that dispels the heart's gloomy mist. It succeeds when boredom or listlessness subsides. | [1, 3, 6, 8-10, 14-17, 19, 20, 24, 29, 31, 33, 36-38, 41, 42, 46, 55, 56, 59, 62-64, 80-82] |
| Upekkhā | Equanimity | Four Sublime States (Brahma-vihāra) | A state of inner equipoise and unshakeable freedom of mind, impartial toward all beings. It is likened to a charioteer watching horses progress evenly, a solid rock unmoved by the wind, or a clear crystal that remains unstained. It is the crown of the sublime states and the antidote to both resentment and approval. | [1, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 14-17, 19, 20, 24, 29, 31, 33-35, 37-39, 41, 42, 45, 46, 52, 55, 56, 59, 62-64, 79-81, 87, 106, 131, 137-139] |
| Sīla | Morality / Virtue | Threefold Training | Conduct governed by ethical principles and purificatory virtues. It is likened to a full-length looking glass, a fence around a garden, a watertight roof, or the "coolness of sandal" that allays mental fever. It provides the foundation for the spiritual path and is the "ornament" of a sage. | [2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 18, 27, 39, 52, 54, 60, 77, 79, 80, 117, 120, 140] |
| Taṇhā | Craving / Thirst | Causes of Suffering | The drive for pleasure and existence that fuels the cycle of rebirth. It is compared to a "seamstress" sewing links of existence, an insatiable fire, or a monkey trap where one is caught by a clenched fist of desire. Similes include a ball of string unwinding or a "House Builder" that must be identified to stop the building of suffering. | [4, 8, 10, 17, 18, 21, 28-33, 36, 53, 54, 58, 73, 74, 77, 81, 90, 94, 95, 99-101, 104, 111, 141] |
| Khandha | Aggregates | Five Aggregates | The five groups constituting personality: matter (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), formations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa). The Buddha used similes of foam, bubbles, a mirage, a hollow plantain trunk, and a conjuring trick to illustrate their insubstantiality and lack of a permanent self. | [7, 25, 30-32, 35-38, 41, 49, 50, 52, 56, 60, 63, 65, 66, 69-71, 78, 81, 87, 90-92, 94, 98, 99, 101, 111, 119-121, 129, 142-146] |
| Kāmacchanda | Sensual Desire | Five Hindrances | Attachment to pleasurable sense-objects that blinds mental vision. It is likened to being in debt and harassed by creditors, or to water mixed with colored dye in which one cannot see a clear reflection. It is inhibited by one-pointedness and overcome by contemplating the repulsiveness of the body. | [4, 6, 12, 17, 20, 36-40, 56, 63, 79, 81, 94, 102, 123, 137] |
| Byāpāda | Ill-will / Aversion | Five Hindrances | A state of anger or resentment that prevents enjoying the Dhamma. It is likened to boiling water in a pot, a physical illness where sweet food tastes bitter, or a venomous snake emerging to attack. It is specificially countered by the development of loving-kindness. | [6, 12, 17, 20, 36-40, 56, 63, 79, 81, 94, 137] |
| Thīna-middha | Sloth and Torpor | Five Hindrances | Mental sluggishness and bodily lassitude. It is compared to being kept in prison during a festival, water overgrown with moss, or butter that hardens in the cold and cannot spread. It is countered by energy-inception (ārambha-dhātu) and the factor of applied thought (vitakka). | [6, 12, 17, 20, 36-40, 56, 63, 79, 81, 94, 97, 137] |
| Uddhacca-kukkucca | Restlessness and Worry | Five Hindrances | Mental agitation and remorse over past deeds. It is compared to slavery where one is at the master's call, or to water whipped into ripples by the wind. A restless mind is likened to flustered bees in a shaken hive or a pile of ashes into which a stone is tossed. | [6, 12, 17, 20, 36-40, 56, 63, 79, 81, 94, 102, 137, 147] |
| Vicikicchā | Sceptical Doubt | Five Hindrances | Indecision or wavering regarding the Teacher, Path, and Training. It is likened to being lost in a dangerous desert fearful of robbers, or turbid muddy water placed in a dark spot. It is described as a "medicine-less" disease that causes a cynical view of reality. | [6, 12, 17, 20, 25, 36-40, 56, 63, 79, 81, 94, 102, 137, 148] |
| Viriya | Energy / Effort | Five Spiritual Faculties | Sustained endeavor and mental vigor used to burn through obstructions. It is compared to the strength of a lion, the tractive power of a beast of burden, or a man whose turban is on fire acting with supreme urgency. It acts as a prop to a falling house to prevent collapse. | [9, 10, 15, 50, 52, 60, 76, 79, 127, 135-137, 144] |
| Saddhā | Faith / Confidence | Five Spiritual Faculties | Settled trust based on knowledge rather than blind belief. It is likened to a miraculous water-clearing gem that settles defilements, a seed that provides the impulse for growth, or a hero leaping across a turbulent river to inspire others. It acts as a "guide to hidden treasure." | [5, 8, 10, 15, 50, 70, 135] |
| Sati | Mindfulness | Five Spiritual Faculties | The factor of watchful observation and attention. It is likened to an experienced gatekeeper, a charioteer keeping a vehicle on track, or salt in a curry that is essential everywhere. In meditation, it is the rope that binds the "mad monkey" of the mind to the post of the object. | [8, 10, 15, 21, 22, 36, 54, 63, 64, 76, 79, 81, 87, 98, 135, 137, 144, 149] |
| Dāna | Generosity / Giving | Ten Perfections | The act of relinquishing possessions for the sake of others to erase greed. It is likened to the sun shedding rays on all, a battle against forces of greed, or a medicinal tree whose every part is available for those in need. It serves as the foundation of the Bodhisatta career. | [2, 6, 9, 10, 39, 52, 150] |
| Paṭiccasamuppāda | Dependent Origination | Core Doctrines | The principle of causality explaining how all phenomena arise in dependence on causes. It is compared to the 12-linked "Wheel of Life," a seal pressed upon wax, or a tangled ball of thread. It illustrates the continuity of individuality without an unchanging soul. | [3, 58, 70, 90, 94, 100, 101, 109, 125, 142, 144, 151-154] |
| Jhāna | Meditative Absorption | Mental Training | Progressive levels of mental unification where hindrances are suppressed. The transition is compared to a craftsman making fine turns on a lathe, or a bath attendant kneading soap-powder until it is a moisture-pervaded ball. A proficient mind is likened to a king's cloth chest full of many-colored garments. | [5, 19, 20, 24, 25, 40, 54, 81, 85, 100-102, 106, 140, 155] |
| Bojjhaṅga | Factors of Enlightenment | Requisites of Enlightenment | Seven mental qualities (Mindfulness, Investigation, Energy, Joy, Tranquility, Concentration, Equanimity) that lead to awakening. They are compared to the "Seven Jewels" of a wheel-turning emperor or a bird becoming fully fledged with wings to fly out of the "nest" of worldliness. | [2, 34, 45, 50, 80, 94, 129, 144, 146, 153, 156] |
| Lobha | Greed / Lust | Three Roots of Evil | The insatiable drive for pleasure, wealth, or power. It is compared to drinking saltwater which increases thirst, a "robber gang" stealing fruits of meditation, or a sticky substance (like tar or sap) used for trapping monkeys. It flares up like a ball of flame thrown on a haystack. | [8, 10, 15, 17, 24, 25, 46, 60, 89, 91, 97, 110, 114, 118, 119, 145] |
| Dosa | Hatred / Aversion | Three Roots of Evil | The urge to destroy obstacles to satisfaction, manifesting as rage or ill-will. It is compared to a forest fire, a sickness that ties the mind in knots, or rust in iron that corrodes the very system that holds it. It is described as "hardening" the mind like gold too rigid to be wrought. | [9, 10, 15, 17, 25, 46, 60, 62, 89, 91, 97, 110, 114, 118, 145] |
| Moha | Delusion / Ignorance | Three Roots of Evil | A primordial blindness to the true nature of things. It functions as a cloak of dull apathy or a "dense fog of torpor" shrouding the mind. It is compared to a web of illusion, a cloud obscuring the sun, or a door-bolt that keeps the truth hidden. | [8, 10, 15, 17, 24, 25, 46, 60, 89, 91, 97, 114, 118, 145] |
| Hiri & Ottappa | Moral Shame & Moral Dread | Guardians of the World (Lokapāla) | Two bright principles that protect the world from moral decay. Hiri (shame) is likened to the disgust felt when grasping an iron bar smeared with excrement; Ottappa (dread) is the fear of grasping an iron bar heated to a red glow. They act as "brakes of the mind-vehicle." | [10, 15, 16, 110, 145, 157] |
| Ānāpānasati | Mindfulness of Breathing | Ten Recollections | Concentration developed through awareness of in-breaths and out-breaths. It is compared to a smith focusing on his work or a great rain cloud that causes all dust to vanish. The mind is likened to a wild calf tied with the rope of mindfulness to the post of the breath. | [1, 19, 58-60, 97, 106, 119] |
| Kāyagatāsati | Mindfulness of the Body | Ten Recollections | Contemplation of the thirty-two parts of the body to perceive their repulsiveness and remove the idea of beauty. It is compared to a hunter catching a monkey in a palm grove or a sack with two openings filled with various grains which a man opens and examines. | [1, 4, 80, 94, 153] |
| Sammā-diṭṭhi | Right View | Noble Eightfold Path | Understanding of kamma and the Four Noble Truths. It is the "harbinger of enlightenment," similar to the reddish glow of dawn preceding sunrise. It functions like a warrior shooting from afar or a gatekeeper who distinguishes right from wrong. | [13, 110, 144, 145, 149] |
| Iddhipāda | Bases of Success | Requisites of Enlightenment | Four conditions for spiritual success: zeal, energy, consciousness, and investigation. Those possessing these are likened to the sons of an emperor who aim to rule, providing the foundation for acquiring supernormal powers. | [34, 37, 80, 158] |
| Khanti | Patience / Forbearance | Ten Perfections | The calm endurance of suffering and hostility without seeking revenge. It is described as a "shore for the ocean of hatred" and is exemplified by the "Teacher of Patience" who remained calm while his limbs were severed by a king. | [6, 24, 39, 52, 136] |
| Nekkhamma | Renunciation | Ten Perfections | The act of abandoning attachment to sensual desires. Sensual objects are compared to fleshless dry bones, a burning sheaf of grass, or a fruit-laden tree being cut down. It is described as going from "home to homelessness" like a doctor tending to health. | [6, 39, 52] |
| Sacca | Truthfulness | Ten Perfections | The fulfillment of one's promise and consistency in deeds. It is likened to the morning star which never swerves from its course. It acts as a "nursery for other virtues" and is the basis for the "Act of Truth" asseveration. | [6, 39, 52] |
| Adhiṭṭhāna | Determination / Resolve | Ten Perfections | Resolute willpower that removes all obstructions to wholesome activity. It is compared to a rocky mountain peak that stands unmoved by boisterous gales or the firmness of one's stand in meritorious deeds. | [6, 39, 52] |
| Asubha | Foulness / Impurity | Forty Meditation Subjects | Meditation on corpses or body parts to counteract lust. The counterpart sign is compared to a man lying down after a meal. It is likened to a boat steadied by a rudder in a rapid current. | [1, 2, 30, 31, 33] |
| Māra | The Evil One / Death | Personifications of Evil | Symbolizes the forces of passion and death obstructing liberation. He is depicted as a sower who sows crops of sense pleasure to trap beings. He overpowers those pursuing pleasure like a storm throws down a weak tree. | [13, 30, 66, 136, 159] |
| Sakkāya-diṭṭhi | Personality View | Ten Fetters | The belief in a permanent soul within the five aggregates. It is compared to a leash-bound dog tied to a firmly planted post, or a man wearing red-tinted glasses who sees everything through his own bias. | [13, 25, 132, 148] |
| Pāramitā | Perfections | Path of the Bodhisatta | Virtues fulfilled by a Bodhisatta to reach Buddhahood. They are compared to a "medicine-tree" that provides parts to anyone in need without discrimination, practiced in simple, secondary, and supreme degrees. | [2, 13] |
| Vitakka | Initial Application | Jhāna Factors | Directing the mind onto the object. Similes include the striking of a bell, a bee's movement toward a lotus, or a carpenter driving a piece of wood into a joint. | [31, 33, 40] |
| Vicāra | Sustained Application | Jhāna Factors | Continued pressure on or "stroking" the object. Similes include the reverberation of a bell after striking, a bee hovering over a lotus, or the pin of a compass revolving around the center. | [31, 33, 40] |
| Papañca | Conceptual Proliferation | Psychological Processes | The tendency of the mind to spin out complex mental commentary. It is described as a "mass of darkness" or a "web" that besets a person with notions of "I" and "mine," leading to disputes. | [5, 54, 140] |
| Sotāpanna | Stream-winner | Stages of Awakening | The first stage of enlightenment attained by breaking the first three fetters. It is compared to entering a "stream" that leads irreversibly to Nibbāna, like the Ganges flowing to the ocean. | [5, 23, 113, 124] |
| Kamma | Action / Volition | Universal Laws | The law of moral causation where every volitional act produces a result. It is likened to a seed sown that determines the fruit reaped, or a shadow that follows an object. It is an impersonal, amoral mechanism of the mind. | [18, 25, 27-29, 74, 76, 77, 114] |
| Āhāra | Four Nutriments | Dependent Origination | The four types of "food" that sustain existence: edible food (like eating the flesh of a beloved son to survive), sense-impression (like a skinned cow attacked by insects), mental volition (like being dragged into a pit of embers), and consciousness (like being struck by 300 spears daily). | [144, 151, 160] |
| Nīvaraṇa | Hindrances | Five Hindrances | Mental pollutions that obstruct wisdom and calm. They are compared to impurities in gold (iron, lead, etc.) that make it brittle, or "insolent flies" that return even after being whisked away. Abandoning them is like being cured of a disease or released from slavery. | [28, 29, 31, 34, 36-39, 45, 49, 50, 56, 70, 80, 81, 85, 90, 94, 96, 106, 119, 126, 127, 129, 140, 144, 146, 147, 153, 156] |
| Sammāpadhāna | Right Efforts | Requisites of Enlightenment | A fourfold effort to prevent, overcome, arouse, or maintain mental states. The Buddha used the simile of a man whose turban is on fire to describe the urgency needed to abandon evil states. | [34, 35, 80] |
| Bhavaṅga | Life-continuum | Process-Freed Consciousness | A passive state of consciousness necessary for continued subjective existence, occurring in dreamless sleep. It is likened to a stream (nadī soto) that flows uninterruptedly between thought-processes. | [25] |
| Paṭhavīkasiṇa | Earth kasiṇa | Ten Kasiṇas | The use of an earth disk to achieve jhāna. The counterpart sign appears purified like a looking-glass disk drawn from its case or the moon's disk from behind a cloud. | [1] |
| Catudhātuvavatthāna | Defining of the four elements | One Defining | Characterizing the essences of earth, water, fire, and air within the body. It is compared to a skilled butcher who sees a cow only as pieces of meat rather than a living being. | [1] |
| Pārājika | Involving disqualification / Defeat | Classes of Offences | Gravest offences involving immediate loss of monkhood. Similes include a man with his head cut off, a withered leaf detached from its twig, and a palmyra palm cut off at the crown. | [161] |
| Adhikaraṇasamatha | Settlement of legal issues | Legal Procedures | Procedures for settling disputes. One procedure, Tiṇavatthāraka, is likened to covering excrement with grass to settle a mass of minor offences collectively without further dispute. | [161] |
| Dibbacakkhu | Divine Eye | Supernormal Knowledges | The ability to see beings passing away and being reborn according to kamma. It is likened to a man standing on a high tower looking down upon a thousand farmsteads. | [5] |
| Phassa | Contact | Dependent Origination | The meeting of object, faculty, and consciousness. It is a "strong condition" like a prop bolstering up a dilapidated house, acting as the "bait of the round." | [13, 160] |
| Kalyāṇamitta | Good Friend | Requisites of Enlightenment | A teacher or associate helpful to spiritual development. They are compared to the aurora or dawn which precedes the sunrise of wisdom. | [162] |
| Passaddhi | Tranquillity / Calmness | Seven Factors of Enlightenment | The allaying of excitation and the quieting of disturbances. It is compared to the cool shade offered by a tree to weary travelers or the cooling of a hot place by rain. | [70, 79, 137] |
| Pīti | Rapture / Joy | Seven Factors of Enlightenment | Intense interest and delight in the object of meditation. Similes include a thirsty traveler seeing a forest with a lake, or waves showering a beach. True rapture comes through detachment (nekkhamma). | [40, 70, 79, 137] |
| Maraṇasati | Mindfulness of death | Ten Recollections | The remembering of the interruption of the life faculty. It is likened to a murderer with a poised sword or the ruin of success. Buddha used the simile of a mountain cow to warn against neglecting its practice. | [1, 2] |
