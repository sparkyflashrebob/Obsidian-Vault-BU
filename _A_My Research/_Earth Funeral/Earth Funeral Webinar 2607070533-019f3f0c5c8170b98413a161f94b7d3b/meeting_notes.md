### Outline
### Environmental Impact of Traditional Burial Methods

- Traditional burial creates a significant environmental burden through the use of wood, metal, concrete, and toxic embalming chemicals, while consuming land that could otherwise support life.

- By 2030, U.S. burials are projected to consume over 100 million board feet of hardwood, bury 8 million tons of concrete, and place over 300,000 tons of steel into the ground.

- Nearly 3 billion pounds of carbon dioxide will be emitted from burials, requiring approximately 35 million trees to absorb the equivalent carbon output.

- An estimated 500,000 gallons of formaldehyde, a known carcinogen, will leach into soil and groundwater from burial practices over the next four years.

### Environmental Impact of Cremation

- Cremation trades land use for energy consumption, with each cremation emitting approximately 535 pounds of carbon dioxide.

- By 2030, cremation-related emissions are projected to total roughly 1.8 million tons of carbon released into the atmosphere.

- The energy required for all cremations is equivalent to powering every home in a small city for a year, as the process relies on natural gas.

### Soil Transformation as a Sustainable Alternative

- If every U.S. death utilized soil transformation, over 1.8 billion pounds of carbon would be removed and stored, equivalent to taking 175,000 cars off the road.

- Widespread adoption of soil transformation would also generate 15 million cubic yards of living, nutrient-rich soil, turning death from environmental harm into environmental restoration.

- Unlike burial or cremation, soil transformation produces no man-made emissions and creates true living, nutrient-rich soil that families can plant, spread, or share.

### Comparison of End-of-Life Disposition Methods

- Traditional burial carries the heaviest environmental impact, combining high land use, non-degradable materials, and toxic chemicals.

- Cremation eliminates land use but burns fossil fuels, producing 535 pounds of carbon dioxide per cremation.

- Green burial is generally gentler on the planet but still requires land, is often inaccessible in urban areas, and can face seasonal limitations when the ground is frozen.

- Aquamation begins around $2,500 and up but remains a newer, specialized process offered by relatively few providers, making it difficult to access in many areas.

- Green burial averages around $5,000 and higher, with costs in some regions such as California reaching $15,000 to $20,000 or more.

- Full-service cremation starts at approximately $6,250 without ceremony or extras, while traditional burial begins around $8,300, often climbing much higher once caskets, vaults, and cemetery fees are added.

### Financial Reality of Unplanned End-of-Life Arrangements

- A 2023 report estimated that an unprepared death can add up to $34,000 in additional costs beyond the funeral itself, including last-minute travel, time off work, legal and administrative delays, and rushed decisions made under pressure.

- Grief spending is a documented phenomenon in which families, feeling urgency to do the right thing, end up spending significantly more than they otherwise would.

- When no arrangements have been made, families are suddenly responsible for dozens of important decisions within a very short period of time, often while still processing the loss itself.

- Families without a plan are left uncertain about what the deceased would have wanted, including disposition method, services, timing, transportation, and documentation, effectively turning grief into event planning.

- Wills are often read too late, as probate typically does not occur until weeks after the funeral, meaning wishes documented only in a will often cannot guide immediate decisions.

### The Soil Transformation Process Step by Step

- Upon receiving a loved one into care, Earth's licensed team gently washes the body and wraps it in a biodegradable shroud made entirely from organic materials.

- The body is then placed on a layer of organic mulch, wood chips, and dried wildflowers, all fully biodegradable and organic, creating the ideal natural environment for transformation.

- Over approximately 30 to 45 days, the body naturally transforms into nutrient-rich soil, with Earth's team carefully monitoring and maintaining the right balance of airflow, temperature, and moisture throughout.

- No artificial chemicals or additives are used at any stage, as the process simply accelerates what nature already does on the forest floor using the same biological systems that have created fertile soil for millions of years.

- Each person is cared for in their own individual stainless steel vessel, with a strict chain of custody system tracking and verifying every stage to ensure complete dignity, identity protection, and transparency.

- Vessels are reused for sustainability but undergo a thorough sanitation process between uses, and Earth's facilities are powered by renewable energy to keep the entire journey fully sustainable.

- At the end of the process, approximately 250 to 300 pounds of nutrient-rich soil remains, representing the combined result of the body and the organic materials used throughout the transformation.

- Larger bones that do not break down naturally are milled to a sand-like consistency and included in the soil, ensuring all nutrients including phosphorus and calcium become fully bioavailable.

- Implants and other inorganic materials are removed at the end of the process and recycled, with proceeds donated to conservation efforts, making the arrangement fully compatible with modern medical implants.

- Organ donation is fully compatible with soil transformation, as donation occurs immediately after death and prior to the loved one being brought into Earth's care.

### How Families Use Their Earth Soil

- Families are the true stewards of the returned soil and choose how much is returned and what to do with it, with every use being unique to their relationship with the person they love.

- Many families plant memorial gardens or trees, creating a living place to visit, reflect, and return to over time.

- Indoor plants are a popular choice as they are portable and can travel with a family through different homes and life stages.

- Soil is often divided among children, siblings, and close friends so that multiple people can carry a personal connection forward in their own meaningful way.

- Spreading soil in places of special significance, such as favorite hiking trails, forests, family homes, or beloved gardens, is another common and deeply personal choice.

- Portions of soil are sometimes shared during a celebration of life, giving family and friends the opportunity to create their own individual memorials.

- Memorial jewelry such as rings, pendants, or lockets can hold a small portion of the soil as a quiet, personal keepsake and daily reminder of a loved one.

- Families may also combine returned soil with the ashes of another loved one or a beloved pet, creating a shared memorial that reflects their life and connection.

- Any soil not retained by the family is donated to trusted conservation organizations to support ecological restoration, forest regeneration, and the planting of native species.

- Donated soil goes to conservation projects rather than cemeteries or memorial parks, ensuring it continues to give back to future generations in a tangible way.
