### What Occupation Does to the Soul
📅 26 Jun, 2026
👥 Andy McGuire, Andrea Axtell-McGuire, Dr. Samah Jabr, Dr. Gabor Maté, Dr. Jennifer Mullan, Dr. Jess Ghannam, Za, Maritzio Bentzo
### Outline
### Introduction of Speakers and Event Context

- Gathered during an ongoing crisis, the event was framed around the genocide and systemic destruction affecting the people of Gaza and Palestine.

- Marked as the launch of the book "Radiance and Pain in Resilience," the event featured essays by Palestinian psychiatrist and mental health advocate Dr. Samah Jabr.

- Joining Dr. Jabr were trauma expert Dr. Gabor Maté and decolonial clinical psychologist Dr. Jennifer Mullan, with the conversation guided by Dr. Jess Ghannam, director of the UCSF Scholars at Risk Program and chair of the UCSF Faculty Association.

- Dr. Ghannam framed the discussion as an opportunity for moral and ethical clarity at a critical historical moment, describing Dr. Jabr's work as a means to reorient the global mental health community.

### Dr. Samah Jabr's Journey and Motivation for Writing

- Writing began as a personal and professional necessity, driven by the isolation felt as a Palestinian living under occupation and the need to reclaim agency and narrative.

- Short essays were composed in response to emotionally and intellectually provocative events, typically written quickly at the end of long working days.

- Collaboration with colleagues from Australia and the United States helped collect and compile the pieces into the published book.

- The act of writing was described as both a selfish emotional outlet and a sense of responsibility toward patients who entrusted Dr. Jabr with their stories.

### Dr. Gabor Maté's Personal and Professional Connection to Palestine

- Early engagement with Palestine began through involvement in the Zionist Youth movement, which was later reconsidered upon learning about the displacement of the Palestinian people.

- A visit during the first Intifada in the early 1990s with the Middle East Children's Alliance provided direct exposure to healthcare conditions across Palestine, including Gaza and the occupied West Bank.

- A 2022 visit to the West Bank to work with women who had been tortured in Israeli prisons revealed that their deepest concerns were often about family relationships rather than the trauma of imprisonment itself.

- Writing the foreword to Dr. Jabr's book reinforced the understanding that the Palestinian experience predates October 7th and reflects a long history of occupation and its psychological consequences.

- The book was described as addressing the deliberate attempt to erase Palestinian experience from history, with memory itself framed as a form of resistance.

### Dr. Jennifer Mullan's Background and Connection to Decolonizing Therapy

- Personal history as a multiracial Black woman shaped by forced migration, loss of ancestral practices, and systemic racism informed a deep understanding of dehumanization and displacement.

- Histories of Black communities, migrant communities, and other colonized peoples were acknowledged as distinct from the Palestinian experience while sharing recognizable mechanisms of domination.

- Years of clinical work in inner-city communities revealed that clients' nervous systems had adapted to surviving environments that repeatedly communicated disposability and unsafety.

- Organizing work from the mid-20s onward, including engagement with the People's Institute of Survival and Beyond, consistently placed Palestine and Gaza at the center of conversations about liberation and solidarity.

- "Decolonizing Therapy" was described as a continuous attempt to widen the therapeutic frame by refusing to separate the psyche from the political conditions in which people live.

### Limitations of Western Trauma Theory and the PTSD Construct

- Western trauma theory, particularly the PTSD framework, was identified as best capturing the experiences of soldiers and failing to reflect the realities of Palestinians living under occupation.

- Palestinian trauma was described as colonial trauma, characterized as cumulative, cross-generational, collective, and intentional, with the specific objective of erasing agency and enforcing helplessness.

- Standard therapeutic approaches such as trauma-focused CBT and prolonged exposure were identified as inadequate responses to the forms of trauma experienced under occupation, including humiliation, sexual degradation, and starvation.

- The DSM was criticized as a document that collects and labels symptoms without explaining their origins, dealing in diagnostic categories rather than in human pain.

- Dr. Maté emphasized that mental health symptoms do not arise in isolation but are formed through interaction with the world, most often rooted in childhood experiences shaped by social and political conditions.

- A clinical example from Dr. Jabr's book illustrated the inadequacy of individualized diagnosis, describing an elderly man forced to demolish his own home with his hands and presenting with suicidal depression that could not be understood outside its political context.

- The essential message of the book was identified as the understanding that so-called mental health conditions are normal responses to abnormal circumstances, a principle described as universal.

### Colonial Trauma as Collective, Intergenerational, and Socially Destructive

- The ongoing and escalating nature of occupation in Palestine makes it difficult to isolate which generation a given symptom originates from, as all generations breathe within the same traumatogenic atmosphere.

- Colonial trauma was described as targeting not only masses of individuals across history but also the relationships between people and the social fabric of communities.

- Fragmentation of the Palestinian people across Jerusalem, the West Bank, Gaza, communities with Israeli citizenship, and the diaspora means that different political and social determinants of health affect each group differently.

- Internalized violence expressed horizontally within communities, including high rates of domestic violence and fratricide, was identified as a consequence of inaccessible oppressors and the divide-and-rule strategies of colonial systems.

- Epigenetics, silence, and broken attachment patterns were named as mechanisms through which intergenerational trauma is transmitted even when grandparents do not explicitly share their histories.

- Dr. Mullan connected these dynamics to the concept of the colonial soul wound, describing it as the injury created when systems sever people from themselves, from one another, from their ancestors, from their cultures, and from their land.

- Survival itself was framed as a deep act of resistance when systems repeatedly communicate that people are disposable and unsafe.

### Rethinking the Role of Mental Health Workers and Community-Based Healing

- Mental health interventions in Palestine were redesigned to move away from individualized clinical models toward community-based approaches that imitate organic opportunities for healing already present in Palestinian culture.

- Collective practices such as communal prayer and traditional dance were identified as models for healing circles and listening groups where people can contain, process, and support one another.

- Training was extended to general doctors, nurses, midwives, school counselors, sport coaches, and community members with lived experience, recognizing that highly specialized professionals are not required to facilitate group support.

- Data from a school-based program in Gaza showed that approximately 30% of children who participated in group support required individual therapy afterward, while 70% benefited sufficiently from the group environment alone.

- The hierarchical, colonial model of Western therapy, exemplified by classical psychoanalysis, was criticized for stripping individuals and communities of agency and positioning the expert's insight as the source of healing.

- Dr. Mullan argued that Black and brown communities show greater openness to healing when helpers bring their full humanity into the space, including their religion, spirituality, and cultural identity, rather than hiding behind professionalism.

- Decolonizing therapy was described as resting on four pillars including the past, the political, the psychological, and the people, which cannot and should not be separated from one another.

- The role of the therapist was reframed as that of a facilitator who creates conditions for communities to help themselves, receive help, and give help to others, rather than as the primary source of healing.

### Conscientization, Liberation Psychology, and Healing the Social Fabric

- Liberation psychology from Latin America, including the concept of conscientization, was identified as a critical framework for raising political awareness among Palestinians so they can understand the mechanisms that manufacture internal division.

- Fratricide and domestic violence were described not as evidence of a lesser nature but as manufactured consequences of colonial strategies, including the arming of criminal elements to fight Palestinians from within, as seen in Gaza, South Lebanon, and Algeria.

- Political awareness was presented as a necessary complement to mental health intervention, helping communities recognize that internal conflict is fabricated rather than inherent.

- Healing circles and listening spaces were described as tools that simultaneously address the psychological consequences of trauma and build the collective capacity to confront fratricide and domestic violence.

- The work of psychiatry in Palestine was framed as a contribution to liberation itself, addressing both the occupation of land and the damage to the social fabric, with the goal of liberating Palestinian minds alongside Palestinian territory.

### Institutional Suppression of Palestinian Mental Health Discourse

- The exclusion of Palestinian trauma and the Nakba from Western mental health literature was identified as a longstanding pattern, noted by anthropologist Ros Messer among others.

- Institutions have used the pretext of neutrality to silence discussion of Palestine, a hypocrisy exposed by the contrast with the permitted and encouraged support for Ukraine following the Russian invasion.

- Mental health professionals have faced suspension, rejection of publications, and loss of positions for describing events in Gaza as genocide or for naming the political and social determinants of Palestinian health.

- This pattern was described as institutional betrayal and complicity, with mental health institutions contributing to the denial of political reality in a manner consistent with the final stage of genocide theory.

- The silence around the killing of over 1,700 medical staff in Palestine was identified as part of a broader institutional denial that reduces health professionals to protocol implementers disconnected from the human condition.

- The Arabic concept of the hakim, meaning a wise person who cares for both the physical and the human condition, was offered as a model to reclaim against the institutional reduction of the doctor's role.

- Creating alternative spaces such as the event itself and the book were described as necessary acts of resistance against institutional suppression of Palestinian narratives in health and mental health fields.

### Practical Training and Adaptive Mental Health Response in Palestine and Gaza

- Mental health policy planning in Palestine involved developing national strategies for suicide prevention and child and adolescent mental health, with emergency room teams trained as gatekeepers for suicide referrals.

- The WHO MH Gap program was used to train a large majority of primary health doctors and nurses to identify and treat common mental health conditions and refer severe cases to specialists.

- The number of psychiatrists in Palestine does not exceed 30, and the mental health budget represents less than 1% of a health sector that itself receives no more than 13% of the national budget.

- When extreme violence began in Gaza, the pre-existing crisis response plan, designed for small-scale emergencies, collapsed entirely as psychiatric hospitals, community mental health centers, and staff were displaced.

- Community members responded organically by establishing community kitchens, recovering medication from rubble, relocating clinics, and creating WhatsApp groups to help people locate available psychiatrists.

- Adaptations over time included moving from clinics to rented containers to tents, and training health professionals in teletherapy when internet access became unavailable.

- Loyalty to the protocol was explicitly rejected in favor of responsiveness, flexibility, and doing what is contextually possible and culturally meaningful at any given moment.

### Next Steps

- Continued discussion about Palestine in both difficult and favorable times was encouraged, with Dr. Jabr's book positioned as a tool for bringing Palestinian mental health into broader professional and public conversations.

- The global mental health community was called upon to synthesize and export knowledge from the Palestinian reality to other parts of the world, contributing to a broader transformation of the profession.

- Dr. Maté cited Dr. Tariq Lubani's statistic that over 80,000 children have been born in Gaza alongside 20,000 reported child deaths, framing Palestinian existence itself as resistance and a testament to resilience.
