Spiritual Awakening or Psychopathology?

Rethinking mental illness through the lens of spiritual transformation.

Posted August 7, 2025 |

Reviewed by Margaret Foley

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/docs/editorial-process

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/docs/editorial-process

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/docs/editorial-process

Spiritual awakenings can mimic symptoms of mental illness.

Differentiating between pathology and transformation requires nuance.

Integration is critical to healing and growth.

Mental health models must embrace transpersonal possibilities.

Source: Vimart/Dreamstime

In today’s mental health landscape, there is a growing recognition that some psychological crises may actually be rooted in

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/spirituality

awakening—not mental illness. Yet the line between awakening and pathology is not always clear, and the stakes are high. Without proper understanding and support, a genuine spiritual emergence can spiral into distress or dysfunction, leading to misdiagnosis and mistreatment. This raises a critical question: How do we differentiate between spiritual awakening and psychopathology—and what happens when we don’t?

What Is a Spontaneous Spiritual Awakening?

Spontaneous Spiritual Awakenings (SSAs) are “subjective experiences characterized by a sudden sense of direct contact, union, or complete nondual merging (experience of oneness) with a perceived ultimate reality, the universe, ‘God,’ or the divine” (Corneille & Luke, 2021). These awakenings can occur without deliberate spiritual practice or intention and often emerge unexpectedly, dramatically altering an individual’s worldview,

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/identity

, and sense of reality. SSAs may involve intense emotional surges, feelings of unconditional love, profound insight, or a sense of being part of something vastly greater than oneself. But while they can be beautiful and life-affirming, they can also be disorienting and frightening—especially when the person lacks a supportive framework or language to understand what’s happening.

When Spiritual Experiences Look Like Mental Illness

According to recent research by Woollacott et al. (2021) and Edwards et al. (2022), individuals undergoing a spontaneous spiritual awakening often report a wide range of intense phenomena: energy surges, perceptual shifts, expanded states of consciousness, emotional volatility, and changes in identity or sense of self. While many of these experiences are transformative, they can also be terrifying, confusing, or overwhelming, especially without a supportive framework. Unfortunately, mainstream

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/psychiatry

often interprets these symptoms through a pathological lens, diagnosing them as

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/anxiety

disorders, dissociation, or even

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/psychosis

. In some cases, spiritual experiences are mistaken for schizophrenia, particularly when individuals report hearing voices, feeling connected to unseen entities, or having a sense of merging with the universe. Yet growing evidence suggests that not all altered states of consciousness are pathological. As Edwards and Woollacott argue, there may be phenomenological overlap between mystical states and psychotic symptoms, but their meaning, outcome, and integration can be vastly different.

The Role of Integration

So what separates a spiritual breakthrough from a psychological breakdown? One key factor is integration—the ability to make sense of the experience, emotionally process it, and incorporate it into everyday life in a healthy, grounded way. Integration may involve building new meaning systems, adjusting identity structures, and learning to live from a deeper sense of self without becoming destabilized. When integration is successful, people often describe their awakening as life-changing, leading to greater peace, clarity,

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/creativity

, and compassion. When integration fails, however, the person may feel fragmented, emotionally dysregulated, or unable to distinguish inner experience from external reality. In such cases, the symptoms may become indistinguishable from those seen in schizophrenia or related disorders. As theorists like Lukoff, Lu, and Turner (1998) have proposed, “spiritual emergency” is a helpful term for these crises—a state in which awakening processes become too intense, too fast, or too unsupported. This model bridges clinical and spiritual frameworks, suggesting that what begins as a genuine process of transformation can degenerate into pathology without adequate guidance, containment, or understanding.

The importance of integration has also been strongly emphasized in the field of psychedelic-assisted

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/therapy

, where individuals often undergo profound, non-ordinary states of consciousness that can resemble spiritual awakenings. In these therapies, the psychedelic experience itself is not considered the healing agent alone—it is the meaning made afterward, and how the insights are integrated into daily life, that determines long-term benefit. Without integration, even the most

awe-inspiring

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/awe

or transformative experiences can lead to confusion, emotional overwhelm, or psychological instability. This mirrors what we see with spontaneous spiritual awakenings: When individuals are supported in making sense of their experience and

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/heuristics

it into grounded practices and relationships, the result is often deep healing and personal growth. These parallels further suggest that integration is not just helpful—it may be the key to safely navigating expanded states of consciousness, whether naturally occurring or catalyzed.

Bridging the Divide: A Call for Integrative Care

The challenge is to recognize that spiritual experiences and psychological struggles can sometimes overlap. What we need is a more integrative approach to mental health—one that makes space for both spiritual awakening and psychological vulnerability. This means:

Training mental health professionals to recognize spiritual emergencies

Creating non-pathologizing spaces for people to share and process unusual experiences

Developing assessment tools that can differentiate between genuine psychosis and transpersonal states

Offering grounding practices, somatic therapies, and meaning-making tools to support integration

Above all, it requires humility—the humility to consider that we may not yet fully understand the spectrum of human consciousness, and that mental health and spiritual development are not mutually exclusive.

Spiritual awakening and mental health crises may, at times, appear nearly identical on the surface. But with a deeper understanding and a commitment to integration, we can begin to distinguish between the two—and, more importantly, support people through the process. In doing so, we not only help individuals heal; we may also expand our collective understanding of what it means to be human.
