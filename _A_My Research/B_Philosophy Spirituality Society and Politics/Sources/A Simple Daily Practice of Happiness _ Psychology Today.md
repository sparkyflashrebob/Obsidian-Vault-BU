A Simple Daily Practice of Happiness

Personal Perspective: Noticing and preserving the goodness right in front of us.

Updated December 17, 2025 |

Reviewed by Margaret Foley

The Heal framework can be helpful in developing our capacity to notice and preserve positive experiences.

Happy experiences need not be grandiose to be meaningful.

A simple daily thought experiment can be useful for noticing beauty and goodness around us.

What one thing did you notice today that was worth noticing? What did you notice that made you feel—in some small way—happy? What did you see? Or taste? Or hear, feel, or touch? Or think?

Not every day is going to offer big happy moments, like weddings, graduations, births, or promotions, but maybe every day can offer something small—like a fawn in the backyard, or a beautiful big moon, or a truly delicious first sip of coffee.

Again, the moment may be small, but that doesn't make it any less worthwhile. When I see a gorgeous sunrise or hear an incredible song, I’m connected to it—I’m no longer trapped in the story of my mind, but rather directly plugged into life as it is happening. This is what makes it special. This is what makes life come alive—it’s what makes me

alive and happy.

For many, even the smallest moments of

seem so elusive as to appear impossible. Life’s difficulties and painful experiences are like a pitch black wall that is infinitely high, making suffering the only thing that seems real.

But what if happiness were a skill that could be practiced—open to everyone, no matter how high our walls might be? What if—like learning a new language or instrument—positive experiences and memories could be intentionally cultivated and preserved?

Evidence suggests this is possible. Using a framework known as HEAL, participants in a study published in 2021 reported significant increases in positive emotions and happiness, as well as decreases in negative emotions, which persisted for two months after the study ended.

The HEAL Method

Developed by Dr. Rick Hanson and detailed in his 2013 book,

Hardwiring Happiness

, the HEAL framework proposes a method for engaging with positive emotional experiences in a way that helps the brain to not only capture them meaningfully, but also cultivate their ongoing activation and retention. The method incorporates four steps:

Have a positive experience:

Noticing or recalling a positive, enjoyable, or useful experience activates the HEAL method. In traditional

mindfulness

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/mindfulness

terms, this is the noticing of it: the awareness of that beautiful moon or delicious sip of coffee.

Purposefully staying with the experience—prolonging it, intentionally noticing surrounding detail more vividly, and exploring its benefits more deeply.

Internalizing the experience—explicitly imagining the positive experience being taken into oneself with an awareness of its impact.

Link positive and negative material:

Given the challenging nature of recalling negative aspects of past experiences, Dr. Hanson suggests this step is optional, but it involves relating the up-front positive aspects of the experience with negative emotions or sensations that may exist in the background. The Practice

Some years ago, I began experimenting with this special kind of noticing as a daily practice. Using the HEAL method as a backdrop, I started to ask myself at the end of each day—usually right after I brushed my teeth, but before I lay my head down—“What one thing did you notice today that was worth noticing?”

At first, I found that I had to search a good bit. I was working in a very typical corporate environment, interacting with people who were often very

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/stress

, distracted, or otherwise far from in touch with anything resembling peacefulness. Heck,

was often very stressed, distracted, and otherwise far from in touch with anything resembling peacefulness.

But then I started to notice that, if I allowed the moment to be small, it became much easier to do. In fact, it became quite easy. I started to notice these small, beautiful things everywhere—

A surprise rainbow on the kitchen floor

The incredible smell of the pizza shop

The tiny kid zooming by the house on a tricycle, whooping like a siren

I noticed that when I recalled these at the end of the day, the shape of my day seemed to change. What first appeared like a normal day of typical dysfunction started to become awash with tiny miracles everywhere.

Now motivated, I started to look for them—actively—like I was collecting an inventory of sparkly moments that I could report to myself each night. Like any practice, the positive results encouraged continued exploration, and I started to get good at it!
