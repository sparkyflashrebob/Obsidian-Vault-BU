Quote of the day by David Hume: 'Beauty is no quality in things themselves, it exists merely in the mind...'

David Hume believed beauty is shaped by perception, not objects.

David Hume’s quote highlights the idea that beauty does not exist as an objective quality in objects but is formed in the mind of the observer. Written in the context of his essay Of the Standard of Taste, the philosopher argued that individual experience and perception shape how people judge art and aesthetics. The quote reflects Hume’s broader belief that human understanding is rooted in experience, helping explain why opinions on beauty differ while still allowing room for thoughtful and informed judgment.

The idea of beauty has often been treated as something fixed and universal, but centuries ago, philosopher David Hume offered a sharply different view on this subject. His observation on how beauty is often experienced highlights how conversations around art, taste, and judgment are dictated by how people perceive it internally. In today’s quote of the day, Hume’s words offer a reminder that what people admire is deeply tied to how they think and feel, rather than to fixed qualities in the world itself.

“Beauty is no quality in things themselves: It exists merely in the mind which contemplates them; and each mind perceives a different beauty.”

At its core, Hume’s statement argues that beauty does not live inside an object in the way weight or shape does. Instead, it comes into being when a person observes or experiences something. According to this view, an artwork, landscape, or piece of music does not carry beauty on its own. Beauty appears only when the human mind responds to it. This explains why the same painting can move one person deeply while leaving another indifferent.

Hume also points out that no two minds react in exactly the same way. Each person brings their own experiences, emotions, habits, and expectations into the act of seeing or listening. As a result, beauty is not fixed or universal. It changes depending on who is doing the observing. What feels elegant, powerful, or pleasing to one person may feel ordinary or even unpleasant to someone else, without either reaction being wrong.

Why Subjectivity Matters in Judging Taste

Hume explored this idea in his essay Of the Standard of Taste, published in 1757. There, he examined how people make judgments about art and culture while still trying to explain why some works gain lasting respect over time. While he accepted that beauty is subjective, he did not argue that all opinions carry the same weight. Instead, he suggested that experience, careful attention, and sensitivity can shape better judgment, even in matters rooted in personal feeling.

This balance is important. Hume’s view avoids strict rules that declare something beautiful for everyone, while also avoiding the claim that taste is random. Beauty begins in the mind, but minds can be trained through exposure, reflection, and comparison.

The Philosopher Behind the Thought

According to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, David Hume (1711–1776) is widely regarded as one of the most important philosophers to write in English. His major works include A Treatise of Human Nature, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, and An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals. His thinking influenced figures such as Adam Smith and Immanuel Kant, with Kant later noting that Hume’s work challenged his earlier assumptions.

Sources such as Britannica describe Hume as a leading voice of philosophical empiricism and skepticism. He believed that human understanding grows from experience rather than abstract speculation. His reflections on beauty fit within this wider project, treating taste as part of human nature rather than as a property of objects.

Whatsapp BannerHume’s words remain relevant in modern debates around art, design, culture, and even social media trends. In a world where people argue intensely over what deserves praise, his insight helps explain why consensus is rare. Beauty, as Hume saw it, is real—but it lives in perception, not in things themselves.

David Hume’s life was marked by both intellectual brilliance and personal challenges. Born in 1711 in Edinburgh, he lost his father at a young age and was educated primarily by his mother before attending the University of Edinburgh. Though his family hoped he would pursue a legal career, Hume’s intense interest in philosophy and general learning led him to reject law entirely. His early dedication to study was so consuming that it caused a nervous breakdown in 1729. Despite facing repeated professional setbacks, including being denied academic positions due to his controversial views, Hume persisted in his writing, ultimately gaining recognition as one of the most influential thinkers of the Scottish Enlightenment.
