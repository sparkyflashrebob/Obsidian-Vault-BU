# Vision Quest Anthology
### *Poems, Quotes, Stories, and Wisdom of Nature, Solitude, and Spirit*

> *"The wilderness provides many archetypal teachers. One discovers one’s relations in the blooming cactus, the soft song of a bird, or the silence of a desert, or mountain, night."*  
> — **Tony Manyhorses**, *What is a Vision Quest?*

---

## Introduction & Sacred Intent

A **Vision Quest** is an ancient, cross-cultural threshold rite: a conscious stepping across the boundary of mundane life into the wild, sacred ground of nature. It is an intentional dying to an old identity, a period of naked contemplation under the open sky, and a search for guidance, depth, and renewal.

Whether your circle is marked among the ancient boulders of the high desert or beneath the dense canopy of an old-growth forest, the non-human world becomes mirror, elder, and sanctuary. The stones steady your bones; the trees breathe your prayers; the creatures carry signs; the wind strips away conceptual chatter until only direct presence remains.

This anthology brings together poems, quotes, parables, and sacred teachings drawn directly from across your **Flashrebob Obsidian** vault. It is organized to accompany every phase of the solitary vigil—from stepping past the threshold, to communing with the tree-people and rock-elders, to sitting through the long watches of the night, and carrying the vision back into the village.

## Part I. Crossing the Threshold: The Call to the Journey

Every quest begins when the voice of ordinary life becomes untenable, and the soul answers the pull of the untamed world. Leaving behind familiarity requires surrendering the need to control the outcome.

### The Journey
*by Mary Oliver*

> One day you finally knew  
> what you had to do, and began,  
> though the voices around you  
> kept shouting  
> their bad advice —  
> though the whole house  
> began to tremble  
> and you felt the old tug  
> at your ankles.  
> “Mend my life!”  
> each voice cried.  
> But you didn’t stop.  
>   
> You knew what you had to do,  
> though the wind pried  
> with its stiff fingers  
> at the very foundations —  
> though their melancholy  
> was terrible. It was already late  
> enough, and a wild night,  
> and the road full of fallen  
> branches and stones.  
>   
> But little by little,  
> as you left their voices behind,  
> the stars began to burn  
> through the sheets of clouds,  
> and there was a new voice,  
> which you slowly  
> recognized as your own,  
> that kept you company  
> as you strode deeper and deeper  
> into the world,  
> determined to do  
> the only thing you could do —  
> determined to save  
> the only life you could save.  

***

### The River Cannot Go Back
*by Khalil Gibran*

> It is said that before entering the sea  
> a river trembles with fear.  
> She looks back at the path she has traveled,  
> from the peaks of the mountains,  
> the long winding road crossing forests and villages.  
> And in front of her,  
> she sees an ocean so vast,  
> that to enter  
> there seems nothing more than to disappear forever.  
>   
> But there is no other way.  
> The river cannot go back.  
> Nobody can go back.  
> To go back is impossible in existence.  
>   
> The river needs to take the risk  
> of entering the ocean  
> because only then will fear disappear,  
> because that’s where the river will know  
> it’s not about disappearing into the ocean,  
> but of becoming the ocean.

## Part II. Kinship with the Land: Trees, Rocks, Desert, and Sky

In the wild circle, you are never alone. The forest and desert are inhabited by ancient beings who do not rush, who do not doubt their existence, and who teach by their effortless stillness.

### When I Am Among the Trees
*by Mary Oliver*

> When I am among the trees,  
> especially the honeys and the willow locust,  
> equally the beech, the oaks and the pines,  
> they give off such hints of gladness.  
> I would almost say that they save me, and  
> daily.  
>   
> I am so distant from the hope of myself,  
> in which I have goodness, and discernment,  
> and never hurry through the world,  
> but walk slowly, and bow often.  
>   
> Around me the trees stir in their leaves  
> and call out, "Stay awhile."  
> The light flows from their branches.  
>   
> And they call again, "It's simple," they say,  
> "and you too have come  
> into the world to do this, to go easy, to be filled  
> with light, and to shine."  

***

### The Small Plot of Ground
*by Pierre Teilhard de Chardin*

> The small plot of ground  
> on which you were born  
> cannot be expected  
> to stay forever  
> the same.  
> Earth changes,  
> and home becomes different  
> places.  
> You took flesh  
> from clay  
> but the clay  
> did not come  
> from just one  
> place.  
> To feel alive,  
> important, and safe,  
> know your own waters  
> and hills, but know  
> more.  
> You have stars in your bones  
> and oceans  
> in blood.  
> You have opposing  
> terrain in each eye.  
> You belong to the land  
> and sky of your first cry,  
> you belong to infinity.

## Part III. All My Relations: The Great Web of Creatures

On a vision quest, a passing beetle, a basking lizard, a circling hawk, or a raven croaking across the canyon are not random backdrop—they are teachers speaking the primordial dialect of existence.

### What is Life?
*by Crowfoot (Blackfoot Elder)*

> *"What is life?  
> It is the flash of a firefly in the night.  
> It is the breath of a buffalo in winter.  
> It is the little shadow that runs across the grass  
> and loses itself in the sunset."*

***

### Great Spirit Prayer (May I Walk in Beauty)
*Lakota Invocation (from [[Dream Catcher Chip Prayer]] & [[Aho Mitakuye Oyasin]])*

> Great Spirit, whose voice I hear in the wind,  
> whose great breath gives life to the world,  
> hear me.  
> I come to you as one of your many children.  
> I am small and weak.  
> I need your strength and wisdom.  
> Let me walk in beauty,  
> and make my eyes ever behold the red and purple sunset.  
> Make my hands respect the things you have made,  
> and my ears sharp to hear your voice.  
> Make me wise so that I may understand the things you have taught my people.  
> Let me learn the lessons you have hidden in every leaf and rock.  
>   
> **Aho Mitakuye Oyasin** — *To All My Relations.*

***

### The Native Precepts of Living with the Earth
*from [[Native American Ten Commandments]]*

1. **Care for the Earth**, which is our Mother.
2. **Honor all relations**—human, winged, four-legged, rooted, and stone.
3. **Open your heart and soul** to the Great Spirit.
4. **Treat all beings with respect**, as all life is sacred.
5. **Take only what is needed** from the Earth.
6. **Do what is necessary** for the good of all.
7. **Give constant thanks** to the Great Spirit for each day.
8. **Speak the truth**, but only for the good of others.
9. **Follow the rhythms of nature**—sunrise, sunset, moon cycles, and seasons.
10. **Enjoy life’s journey**, but leave no tracks.

### I Bow at the Feet of Every Creature
*by Hafiz (from [[The Gift - Hafiz]])*

> May I lay my cheek,  
> I bow my head,  
> at the feet of every creature.  
> This constant submission and homage,  
> of kissing God against the beauty of each form...  
> For when I bring my heart close to any object,  
> I always hear the Friend say:  
> *"Hafiz, I am here."*  
>   
> Because of my union with Reality,  
> whenever I hear of the holy ones having walked the earth,  
> I know I was a tree that stood near, leaned down, and took notes.  
> I know I was the earth that measured the infinite arch of their feet.  
> I know I was the water that quenched their thirst.

### The Sacred Hoop of the Four Directions
*from Doug Good Feather, [[Think Indigenous_1.pdf]]*

- **East (*Wiyóhpiyata*) — Fire & New Beginnings:** The morning light, awakening vision, gratitude, and generosity.
- **South (*Itókagata*) — Air & The Breath of Life:** Summer vitality, innocence, mental clarity, and walking with honest integrity.
- **West (*Wiyóȟpeyata*) — Water & Healing Powers:** Sunset, the emotional depths, humility, surrender, and the courage to release old grief.
- **North (*Wazíyata*) — Earth Medicine & Fortitude:** Winter stillness, the deep roots of the ancestors, compassion, endurance, and physical grounding.
- **Above (*Waŋkátakab*) & Below (*Khúta*):** The Great Mystery above and Mother Earth below, uniting within the Center of Life (*Čhaŋté* / Heart).

## Part IV. The Solitary Watch: Silence, Fasting, and the Night

In fasting and solo silence, the buffer of constant consumption dissolves. Physical hunger sharpens spiritual perception. Night turns the sky into an obsidian bowl of stars, confronting the seeker with the vastness of eternity.

### Letting Go into Deep Mind
*by Keith Kristich (from [[Poems & prayers for silence & meditation]])*

> In letting go of thoughts and thinking,  
> we sink into Deep Mind.  
>   
> In letting go of emotion and feeling,  
> we sink into Deep Heart.  
>   
> In letting go of action and doing,  
> we sink into Deep Being.  
>   
> In letting go of self and other,  
> we sink into Deep One.  
>   
> In letting go of letting go,  
> we recognize that we were never holding on.  
> We have always and only ever rested in the Beloved.  

***

### The Fullness of Silence
*from Carla A. Grosch-Miller, [[Poems & prayers for silence & meditation]]*

> O Lord, my heart is open  
> and my mind is freed  
> from the struggle to make sense  
> even of who, of how, You are.  
> *(I breathe.)*  
>   
> I come to the broad plain,  
> the fullness of silence.  
> Peace envelops me.  
> I sink into You.  
> I want for nothing.  
> *(This is the still point of the turning world.)*  
> I rest in You.

## Part V. Parables of Presence and Release

Ancient seekers preserved stories to remind the quester that spiritual freedom is found not by escaping difficult circumstances, but by leaning completely into what is right here, right now.

### 1. The Tigers and the Wild Strawberry
*(from [[The Buddhist Story That Will Change How You Spend the Next 60 Seconds of Your Life]] & [[Sweetness at the Edge of the Abyss]])*

> A seeker was walking alone through the wild forest when he heard the low, earth-shaking growl of a tiger behind him. He ran harder than he had ever run in his life until the ground abruptly ended at the lip of a precipitous cliff.  
>   
> With no choice, he caught hold of a wild vine swinging over the abyss and lowered himself over the precipice. Dangling in midair, he looked down. Waiting at the bottom of the canyon rocks was a second hungry tiger, gazing upward in patient certainty. Above him, the first tiger paced the cliff edge.  
>   
> Suspended between two deaths, the man looked closely at the vine that held him. From a cleft in the rock emerged two mice—one white, one black. Little by little, in steady alternation—day and night—they began to gnaw at the fibrous stem. He heard the fibers snapping one by one.  
>   
> In that moment of absolute stillness, where there was nowhere left to run, no future to plan, and no past to regret, he noticed something growing from a crevice within arm's reach: a single wild strawberry, crimson, glistening in the sunlight, completely alive.  
>   
> Reaching out with one free hand, he plucked the berry and placed it on his tongue.  
>   
> It was the sweetest thing he had ever tasted in his entire life.  
>   
> Not because the berry possessed magical power, but because for the first time in his life, he was unreservedly, completely present. The tigers of mortality are always there—yesterday, today, and tomorrow. The only question the quest asks is: **Are you present enough to taste the strawberry?**

***

### 2. The Muddy Road: Leaving the Burden Behind
*(from [[_Muddy Road_ - Unknown - Spiritual Story and Explanation]])*

> Two monks, Tanzan and Ekido, were walking along a rain-drenched country road. The mud was deep and thick. At an impassable crossroads stood a young woman in an exquisite silk kimono, unable to cross without ruining her clothes.  
>   
> Without a word or hesitation, Tanzan scooped the woman up into his arms, carried her across the deep mud, set her down gently on dry ground, and walked on.  
>   
> Ekido said nothing, but inwardly he seethed. Hours passed in stony silence. Night fell, and when they finally arrived at the temple lodging, Ekido could bear it no longer:  
> *"We monks are forbidden to touch women, let alone pick them up and hold them! Why did you do that? It is against our sacred vows!"*  
>   
> Tanzan looked at his companion with calm compassion and smiled:  
> *"I left the girl on the other side of the road hours ago. Why are you still carrying her?"*  
>   
> **Quest Medicine:** On the mountain, we discover how much baggage—resentments, identities, rules, grievances—we have carried up the hill. The wilderness invites us to set down what ended long ago.

***

### 3. Each Life, Its Own Mountain and River
*(from [[Each Life, Its Own Mountain and River]])*

> Every life is its own vast mountain and sea. We are born into different fires, raised under different skies. The storms we’ve witnessed vary, and the warmth and cold we carry in our hearts are never the same.  
>   
> Some are surrounded by flowers from birth — gentle and open, never knowing the bite of frost. Others grow up dancing with the wind and rain — learning to hold their own umbrella, quietly hiding their scars.  
>   
> Between travelers lie gaps of circumstance, trial, and perspective. Blooming has its brilliance; wilderness has its own quiet peace. Passion is one way to live — stillness is another worthy path.  
>   
> You have your flower-strewn path. I have my thorny trail.  
> We need not look up to each other, nor look down.  
> We simply walk our own journeys and allow the other to do the same.  
>   
> With kindness in our words, clarity in our sight, acceptance of differences, and compassion for every circumstance — this is the finest practice for the rest of our lives.

## Part VI. The Return: Bringing the Gift to the Village

A vision quest is never completed for the quester alone. Indigenous wisdom reminds us that we fast and pray on the mountain so that our people, our families, and the Earth may live with greater wisdom. When the solo ends, the vision must be translated into service, love, and courageous living.

### When Death Comes
*by Mary Oliver*

> When death comes  
> like the hungry bear in autumn;  
> when death comes and takes all the bright coins from his purse  
> to buy me, and snaps the purse shut;  
> when death comes  
> like the measle-pox;  
> when death comes  
> like an iceberg between the shoulder blades,  
>   
> I want to step through the door full of curiosity, wondering:  
> what is it going to be like, that cottage of darkness?  
>   
> And therefore I look upon everything  
> as a brotherhood and a sisterhood,  
> and I look upon time as no more than an idea,  
> and I consider eternity as another possibility,  
>   
> and I think of each life as a flower, as common  
> as a field daisy, and as singular,  
>   
> and each name a comfortable music in the mouth,  
> tending, as all music does, toward silence,  
>   
> and each body a lion of courage, and something  
> precious to the earth.  
>   
> When it's over, I want to say all my life  
> I was a bride married to amazement.  
> I was the bridegroom, taking the world into my arms.  
>   
> When it's over, I don't want to wonder  
> if I have made of my life something particular, and real.  
>   
> I don't want to find myself sighing and frightened,  
> or full of argument.  
>   
> I don't want to end up simply having visited this world.  

***

### Our Deepest Light
*by Marianne Williamson (from [[Marianne Williamson - Return To Love]])*

> "Our deepest fear is not that we are inadequate.  
> Our deepest fear is that we are powerful beyond measure.  
> It is our light, not our darkness, that most frightens us.  
>   
> We ask ourselves, who am I to be brilliant, gorgeous, talented, fabulous?  
> Actually, who are you not to be?  
> You are a child of God. Your playing small does not serve the world.  
> There is nothing enlightened about shrinking so that other people won't feel insecure around you.  
>   
> We were born to make manifest the glory of God that is within us.  
> It is not just in some of us; it is in everyone.  
> And as we let our own light shine,  
> we unconsciously give other people permission to do the same.  
> As we are liberated from our own fear,  
> our presence automatically liberates others."

***

### Reincorporation & Council
*from Tony Manyhorses, [[What is a Vision Quest - Tony Manyhorses - text]]*

> "The return from the quest can be a time of great energy and joy. Life is new; one has been reborn. But the **reincorporation** phase is a challenge. How does one bring Vision back into the world of work, relationship, and everyday life?  
> We meet in council, in which the sharing of our experience deepens the understanding of our lessons, and their integration into our lives.  
> The energy of the Vision Quest flows into us as a healing, and from us, enters the world."
