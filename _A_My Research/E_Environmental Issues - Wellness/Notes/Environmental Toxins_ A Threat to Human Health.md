Environmental factors significantly influence human health, impacting everything from physical well-being to mental and neurological function [1, 2]. These factors include physical surroundings, social conditions, and exposure to various pollutants [2-4]. Research shows a complex connection where environmental factors can alter brain structure and function, influence stress levels, and either protect or compromise mental health [5, 6].

**General Health Impacts of Environmental Factors**
Exposure to environmental pollution is a significant global health risk, contributing to increased mortality and morbidity [7, 8]. Even low levels of chemical exposure can cause disease [9]. The widespread presence of manufactured chemicals and plastics in the environment and in human bodies has been building evidence of their disease-causing potential since the 1970s [9, 10]. Pollution, in general, has been linked to an estimated 9 million deaths annually [11].

**Specific Environmental Factors and Their Health Consequences:**

1. **Chemical Pollution (General)**
   Man-made chemicals have infiltrated even the most remote parts of the planet, including the Mariana Trench, Himalayan glaciers, and the Arctic and Antarctic, where toxic chemicals and microplastic particles are found ubiquitously [10, 12]. These chemicals can enter the human body through inhalation, ingestion (oral absorption), or skin absorption [8, 12]. Some of the most harmful chemicals include "forever chemicals" (like PFAS) and "hormone-disrupting chemicals" that do not break down and can block, mimic, or disrupt natural hormones [12].
2. **Heavy Metals**
   Heavy metals, such as lead (Pb), mercury (Hg), arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), and nickel (Ni), are naturally occurring but are significantly introduced into the environment by human activities like industrial emissions, mining, agriculture, and waste disposal [13-18]. They pose serious global risks due to their persistence, bioaccumulation in ecosystems, and biomagnification through the food chain (e.g., in fish, milk, crops, and drinking water) [14, 19-23].

   * **Exposure Routes**: Humans are primarily exposed through ingestion (contaminated food, water, soil), but also through inhalation (industrial emissions, particles, fumes), and dermal contact [22, 24-27]. Children are particularly susceptible to soil contamination due to hand-to-mouth activities and a higher skin surface area to body weight ratio [22, 24, 27].
   * **Health Impacts**: Heavy metals can cause a wide range of debilitating effects, including:
     + **Neurological**: Neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)), cognitive impairments, seizures, psychosis, changes in neurotransmitter levels (acetylcholine, noradrenaline, dopamine), Parkinsonism, and neurotoxicity [13, 19, 20, 28-33]. Lead exposure in children can result in behavioral and cognitive problems, affecting memory, language, and reasoning [19, 29, 33].
     + **Cardiovascular**: Hypertension, increased heart rate, arterial stiffness, myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, and stroke [13, 19, 32, 34-37].
     + **Reproductive/Prenatal**: Damage to reproductive organs, sperm DNA damage, reduced sperm quality, infertility, hormone imbalances, mutations, decreased fertilization capacity, miscarriages, stillbirths, birth malformations, and diminished ovarian reserve [13, 19, 38-43]. Heavy metals like lead, cadmium, and mercury can cross the placenta and accumulate in fetal tissues, affecting neurodevelopment and causing adverse birth outcomes [33, 41-43].
     + **Other Organ Systems**: Damage to the liver and kidneys, anemia, proteinuria, glucosuria, osteomalacia, emphysema, and chemical pneumonitis [13, 19, 32, 42, 44, 45].
     + **Cancer**: Respiratory cancer, skin tumors, and tumorigenesis [13, 16, 19, 42, 45-48].
   * **Mitigation**: Strategies include *in situ* stabilization, phytoremediation, and chelation therapy [49, 50].
3. **Plastics and Microplastics**
   Plastic products contribute significantly to global greenhouse gas emissions, primarily from production [51]. Plastics and microplastics are found everywhere, including marine and terrestrial environments, and in humans [10, 51-55]. Microplastics have been detected in human blood, placenta, breast milk, meconium, and infant feces, with infants showing higher exposure levels [56, 57]. This suggests a potential "intergenerational influence" on developing fetuses [56].

   * **Exposure**: Primarily through oral intake, but also inhalation and skin contact [55]. Fast food and microwave popcorn can increase PFAS load, while home-cooked meals do not [58].
   * **Health Impacts**: While direct human health impacts are not fully understood, associations include cardiovascular disease and low male fertility [52]. Chemicals found in plastics are known to cause cancers, metabolic disorders, ADHD, and fertility issues [52]. Experimental models show microplastic toxicity leads to oxidative stress, DNA damage, organ dysfunction, metabolic disorders, immune responses, neurotoxicity, and reproductive/developmental toxicity [53, 59]. Specific industrial exposures to polymers have been linked to chronic respiratory diseases and interstitial lung disease in workers [60].
4. **Air Pollution**
   Ambient air pollution (AAP) from sources like automobile emissions, power plants, burning waste, chemical companies, and even volcanic eruptions, introduces contaminants such as particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10), sulfur dioxide (SO2), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOx), ozone (O3), heavy metals, and tobacco smoke [17, 61, 62].

   * **Health Impacts**:
     + **Respiratory**: Weakens lung defenses, increases epithelial permeability, reduces mucociliary clearance, and impairs macrophage function, leading to bronchitis, pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and lung and heart problems [44, 61].
     + **Cardiovascular**: Increases risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, and alters blood pressure, contributing to heart failure, myocardial infarction, and atherosclerosis [34, 35, 62, 63].
     + **Mental Health**: Strongly associated with exacerbation of anxiety, schizophrenia, and depression symptoms [1, 64-68]. Short-term exposure to certain pollutants can increase suicide risk [69]. Pregnant women are more vulnerable to increased pollutant concentrations [70].
     + **Neurodevelopmental**: Prenatal and early postnatal exposure to pollutants like PM2.5, NO2, PM10, and ozone is linked to the development of autism spectrum disorders (ASDs) [71, 72].
   * **Mechanisms**: Air pollutants induce oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and disrupt the blood-brain barrier, leading to neurotransmitter and hormonal dysregulation [1, 28, 66, 73].
5. **Soil Pollution**
   Soil contamination occurs from industrial processes, wastewater, domestic waste, livestock, pesticides, petroleum products, plastic additives, and heavy metals [24].

   * **Health Impacts**: Can cause acute (headaches, coughing, chest pain, nausea, skin irritation) and chronic health problems (CNS damage, organ damage, cancer) [24]. Crops grown in contaminated soil can be highly toxic upon consumption [24].
   * **Mental Health**: Heavy metals in soil have been linked to psychosis and general mental disorders [30]. Pesticides and solvents in soil can lead to mental disorders, including neuropsychiatric issues [1, 74, 75].
6. **Water Pollution**
   Water contamination is a significant global environmental issue, especially in areas with inadequate waste management and poverty [7, 19, 20]. Pollutants include heavy metals, chemical oxygen demand (COD), ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N), tetrachloroethylene (PCE), volatile phenol (Fn), and industrial effluents [20, 63].

   * **Health Impacts**: Ingestion of contaminated water can lead to various vulnerable diseases, including neural disorders, psychosomatic issues, mental retardation in neonates, tumorigenesis, and even death [20, 22].
   * **Mental Health**: Water pollution, particularly from heavy metals, contributes to mental health deterioration, disproportionately affecting low-income populations [31, 63]. Oil spills and water contamination incidents have been linked to increased stress, anxiety, depression, anger, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), as well as increased alcohol and substance use [76].
7. **Pesticides and Agrochemicals**
   Pesticides are designed to affect the nervous system of pests but can cause neurotoxicity in humans due to similar neurochemical reactions [29].

   * **Health Impacts**: Damage the liver, kidney, neural, and immune systems; induce birth defects; cause reproductive toxicity; inflammation of the upper respiratory tract; bronchitis; aplastic anemia; impaired child development and IQ; decreased lung function; and central nervous system tumors [13, 44]. They are linked to neurodevelopmental abnormalities like learning disabilities, ADHD, autism spectrum disorders, developmental delays, and behavioral problems [29, 77]. Occupational exposure can lead to higher risks of sperm abnormalities, infertility, spontaneous abortion, and fetal death [39].
   * **Mental Health**: Exposure has been linked to mental disorders in rural populations and neuropsychiatric disorders [73, 74].

**Broader Environmental and Social Contexts:**

1. **Climate Change and Extreme Weather Events**
   The World Health Organization identifies climate change as a major health threat [78]. It exacerbates existing hazards by causing inadvertent industrial chemical releases, particularly during extreme weather events like hurricanes, floods, and wildfires [79-81]. These events mobilize contaminants (e.g., coal ash) and increase the risk of chemical exposure in disaster-prone industrial areas [81, 82].

   * **Mental Health**: Extreme weather conditions are linked to depression, PTSD, and anxiety [1, 74]. Repeated exposure can have cumulative effects, and indirect exposure through media or knowing affected individuals also impacts mental health [74]. Rising temperatures are associated with higher rates of aggression and violent suicides [69].
2. **Light and Noise Pollution**
   Excessive artificial light at night (light pollution) and noise pollution are critical concerns, especially in urbanized areas [1, 83].

   * **Health Impacts**: Light pollution interferes with circadian rhythms and sleep, which is linked to neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease [1, 84]. Noise pollution is also correlated with neurodegenerative disorders and increases the risk of depression, anxiety, and suicide [1, 85-88].
3. **Socioeconomic Factors and Environmental Justice**
   Polluting industries are disproportionately located in low-income communities of color, increasing their vulnerability to health impacts from chemical exposures [89, 90]. These "fenceline communities" often face greater social stressors (poverty, unemployment, crime, racism) that amplify health burdens, including higher rates of chronic diseases and lack of access to healthy foods and resources [69, 89, 91-93]. Environmental racism, defined as racial discrimination in environmental policy-making, significantly affects the mental health of Black, Indigenous, and People of Color (BIPOC) communities [69]. The mental health benefits of exposure to nature, such as green spaces, vary and may not be as evident for those living in poorer social conditions or underserved communities with less access to such spaces [94, 95].
4. **Proximity to Industry and Legacy Contamination**
   Living near oil and gas development, industrial animal operations, and chemical manufacturing facilities leads to increased exposure to hazardous pollutants [96-101]. Legacy contamination from past industrial activities continues to affect current generations [79, 101]. Industrial closures or cleanups have provided compelling evidence of reduced exposures and improved health for nearby residents, demonstrating the direct link between industrial activity and health harm [79, 82, 102-104]. However, linking local industrial pollution to specific health impacts is challenging due to multiple pollution sources and limited research resources in affected communities [7, 91, 105-107].

**Underlying Mechanisms of Harm**
Environmental pollutants impact health through several biological mechanisms:

* **Oxidative Stress**: Buildup of free radicals, leading to damage to lipids, proteins, carbohydrates, and DNA in cells, and depletion of antioxidant defenses [1, 28, 34, 38, 44, 73, 108].
* **Systemic and Neuroinflammation**: Activation of inflammatory cells and mediators, which can affect the lung's defenses, disrupt the blood-brain barrier, and contribute to neurodegenerative disorders [1, 28, 34, 44, 66, 73, 109-111].
* **DNA Damage**: Cytotoxic, genotoxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic effects [13, 28, 47, 48, 112]. Pollutants can cause gene silencing, gene mutations, and epigenetic alterations [1, 34, 73, 112].
* **Hormonal/Endocrine Disruption**: Interference with natural hormones, leading to reproductive problems and metabolic disorders [12, 13, 43, 66].
* **Neurotoxicity**: Direct harm to the nervous system, affecting brain development, cognitive function, and behavior [1, 19, 24, 28, 29, 33].
* **Immune System Impairment**: Environmental stressors can impair immune function, increasing susceptibility to infections and affecting progression of diseases like cancer [111, 113, 114].

**Vulnerable Populations**
Infants and children are significantly more sensitive to toxic chemicals than adults, and early life exposure can lead to diseases decades later [115]. Pregnant women are also highly vulnerable due to their increased ventilation rate and the potential for contaminants to transfer to the fetus [41, 70]. Low-income communities, communities of color, and individuals with pre-existing health conditions or low socioeconomic status are disproportionately affected by environmental hazards and their health consequences [74, 89, 91, 93, 116].
