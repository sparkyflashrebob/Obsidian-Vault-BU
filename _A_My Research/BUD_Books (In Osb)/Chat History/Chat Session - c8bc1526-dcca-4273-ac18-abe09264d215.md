To resolve these internal conflicts, the texts propose a methodical, psychological approach that involves retraining the mind to abandon unwholesome habits and cultivate profound wisdom. Rather than simply suppressing negative emotions—which only drives them deeper into the subconscious where they continue to cause trouble—the goal is to transform them through specific practices [1].

Here is how the sources recommend resolving these internal conflicts:

**1. Applying Direct Antidotes (Thought Substitution)**
The mind cannot hold two contradictory thoughts at the same time, so one of the most practical strategies is to replace a harmful mental state with its exact opposite [2, 3].

* **To resolve greed and attachment:** Cultivate the intention of renunciation and practice generosity (*dāna*) [2, 4]. If you are consumed by sensual lust, contemplate the unattractive aspects of the body or the impermanence of the things you desire [5, 6].
* **To resolve hatred and anger:** Cultivate loving-kindness (*mettā*) and compassion (*karuṇā*) [5]. The texts offer five specific techniques to subdue hatred toward someone: develop goodwill for them, develop compassion for them, develop equanimity toward them, simply pay no attention to them, or reflect that their own actions (karma) will be their ultimate judge and inheritance [7, 8].
* **To resolve delusion:** Associate with knowledgeable people, study the teachings, and develop the wisdom to see the difference between what is wholesome and what leads to suffering [4, 6].

**2. Mindful Acceptance and "Non-Violence" Toward Yourself**
While it is important to apply antidotes, you should never treat your own negative emotions with violence or hatred [9, 10]. Anger, fear, and craving are organic parts of your mind, much like garbage that can eventually be composted to grow flowers [11, 12]. When an emotion like anger arises, do not fight or repress it. Instead, embrace it with mindfulness, perhaps even greeting it warmly by saying, "Hello, seed of anger!" or "Dear one, I am here for you, I am going to take care of you" [13, 14]. By observing your inner defilements without identifying with them or judging them, you starve them of the energy they need to survive, much like a stray cat that stops coming around when you stop feeding it [15].

**3. Deconstructing the Illusion of "Self"**
Because the root of all conflict is the dividing line between "I" and "not-I," the ultimate resolution requires dismantling the illusion of a separate self [16]. You can practice this by breaking down your experience into its parts—such as your body, feelings, perceptions, and thoughts—and repeatedly reflecting on them with the understanding: "This is not mine. This is not my self. This is not what I am" [17, 18].

When you deeply understand that there is no solid "self" to protect or defend, you realize that your suffering and happiness are not separate from the suffering and happiness of others [19]. Recognizing that your "enemy" is suffering from the exact same internal afflictions as you are destroys the boundary of the ego, causing anger and the desire to punish to naturally vanish [11, 19].

Through these practices, you gradually unburden the mind from its destructive habits and reach a state of pure, peaceful awareness [20].

MODEL:

The sources indicate that the root cause of all fighting and conflict lies within the human mind, specifically in the "three poisons" or mental defilements (*kilesas*): **greed (or passion), hatred (or aversion), and delusion (or ignorance)** [1-4]. These underlying forces drive people to act aggressively, selfishly, and destructively.

The texts elaborate on how these defilements manifest into conflict in several specific ways:

**1. Craving for Sensual Pleasures and Possessions**
The endless pursuit of sensual pleasures, wealth, and power is a primary reason why people fight [5-7]. Human desires are described as insatiable; as the Buddha noted, even if it rained gold coins, it would not be enough to fulfill our desires [8, 9]. Because everyone is seeking happiness from the same limited pool of resources, competition naturally arises [10, 11]. When we acquire things and label them as "mine," we become possessive and defensive [12]. This defensiveness breeds envy and stinginess, eventually leading to arguments, the taking up of weapons, and even wars [12-14].

**2. Attachment to Views and Ideologies**
Beyond material things, people fight because they cling tightly to their views, opinions, and beliefs (*ditthi*) [15-17]. When people become attached to the idea that their way of thinking is the absolute truth, they naturally fall into conflict with those who think differently, adopting the stance that "I am right and you are wrong" [18, 19]. This attachment to views causes people to become dogmatic and arrogant, leading to arguments, factionalism, and even "religiously" inspired violence, where individuals feel a sacred, righteous duty to eliminate those who oppose their beliefs [20-22].

**3. The Illusion of a Separate Self and "Objectification"**
At the deepest level, conflict stems from the delusion of a separate self—the notions of "I," "me," and "mine" [23, 24]. This self-view leads to a mental process called "objectification" (*papañca*), where we divide the world into "us" and "them" [25, 26]. Through this lens, we view other people merely as objects that either serve as a source of "food" to fulfill our desires, or as obstacles to our happiness [27, 28]. This mindset creates a world of division, alienation, and discrimination [29, 30].

Ultimately, fighting does not merely come from external circumstances or bad people, but from an internal failure to understand suffering. As long as human actions are fueled by the desire to protect our ego and our attachments, conflict will continue [31, 32].
