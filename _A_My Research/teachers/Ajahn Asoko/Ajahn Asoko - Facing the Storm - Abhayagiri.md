Explain the story of Ananda's grief over the Buddha's passing in more detail

---

Ananda, the Buddha's devoted attendant, was deeply grieved by the impending passing of his teacher. As the Buddha's parinirvana (final passing) approached, Ananda went into a building and wept, lamenting that he was still a learner with work left to do, and his compassionate Master was about to pass away.

The Buddha, knowing Ananda's distress, called for him. When Ananda came, the Buddha consoled him, reminding him of the fundamental teaching that all things dear and beloved must undergo change, separation, and severance. He explained that it is impossible for anything born, existent, fabricated, and subject to disintegration to not disintegrate.

The Buddha also praised Ananda for his long and devoted service, acknowledging his loving-kindness in deed, word, and thought. He encouraged Ananda to commit to exertion, assuring him that he would soon be free from mental fermentations.

Later accounts and discussions in Buddhist texts also touch upon Ananda's "fault" or "mistake" in not requesting the Buddha to remain for a longer period (an eon). According to some narratives, the Buddha had given hints that he could prolong his life if asked, but Ananda, whose mind was said to be "possessed by Mara" (a demon representing temptation and death), failed to grasp these hints and make the request. When Ananda eventually did ask, after the Buddha had already announced his impending parinirvana, the Buddha stated that the time for such an entreaty had passed, and that he would have consented if Ananda had asked earlier. This highlights the concept of impermanence and the idea that even enlightened beings operate within certain conditions and choices.

Despite this, Ananda eventually attained enlightenment (Arahantship) himself, often just before important events like the First Buddhist Council, where his prodigious memory was crucial in reciting many of the Buddha's discourses. His story serves to illustrate the human experience of attachment and grief, even for those on the path to liberation, and the Buddha's teachings on impermanence and letting go.
