Maxwell's missing millions

This article is more than 26 years old By

Derek Brown

https://www.theguardian.com/profile/derekbrown

Tue 2 Feb 1999 12.15 EST

mailto:?subject=Maxwell's missing millions&body=https://www.theguardian.com/news/1999/feb/02/derekbrown1?CMP=share\_btn\_url

The accountancy firm which failed to detect frauds committed by Robert Maxwell has been fined a record £3.5 million, including costs. Coopers & Lybrand has accepted the findings of the industry watchdog and has spoken of its "deep regret".

And that, apparently is that. The auditors who inspected the fat fraudster's books are still employed, no doubt lucratively, by the giant accountancy practice. The Maxwell pensioners who were ripped off to the tune of more than £400 million are still not fully compensated, nor do they have the satisfaction of knowing that anyone has been punished.

Maxwell, who lived like a nabob and was a boorish bully to all his underlings, used his companies' pension funds, held in investment companies which he established, as collateral for loans to other enterprises in his dodgy empire. Moreover, money from the pension funds was used to buy shares in Maxwell companies, inflating their value.

From outside the arcane business of modern accountancy, it seems remarkable that trained practitioners failed to detect the smell of books being cooked on this scale. It also seems a shade questionable that such an astonishing failure should be judged from within the profession, rather than by an independent body.

The report from the accountancy trade's Joint Disciplinary Scheme certainly pulls no punches. It says bluntly of Coopers & Lybrand: "The firm lost the plot".

More correctly, it never got wind of the plot. It failed to consider whether there was "evidence of fraud, other irregularities, defaults or unlawful acts" within the sprawling Maxwell business empire.

"The complaints reveal shortcomings in both vigilance and diligence and a failure to achieve an appropriate degree of objectivity and scepticism, which might have led to an earlier recognition and exposure of the reality of what was occurring," says the report.

That is accountant-speak for "they made a royal cobblers of it".

Coopers & Lybrand has since merged with another mega-practice to form PriceWaterhouseCoopers. Peter Smith, a senior partner, said today that the company accepted the findings of the Joint Disciplinary Tribunal, and the penalties it imposed, and that it has devised new procedures to prevent any repetition.

"We accept that we fell short of the very high standards we set ourselves. This is a matter of deep regret," he said.

The report, he said, had made it clear that the auditors concerned had been the "victims of deliberate deceit". Quite so. But, excuse us, isn't that exactly what they are supposed to detect?
