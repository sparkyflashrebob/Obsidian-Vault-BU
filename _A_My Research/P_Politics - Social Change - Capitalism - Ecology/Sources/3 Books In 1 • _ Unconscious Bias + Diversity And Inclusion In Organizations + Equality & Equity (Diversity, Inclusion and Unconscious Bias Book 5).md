Chapter 1 - A Trip to a World Devoid of Consciousness: Understanding Unconscious Bias

An open-minded person is one who is receptive to a wide variety of ideas, arguments, and information. This ability is generally considered a positive one and necessary for critical and rational thinking.

Without the ability to welcome other ideas and perspectives, it may be very easy to miss certain factors that contribute to the problems, making the discovery of a practical solution very difficult. With the world being as polarized as it is now, the ability to step out of your comfort zone to look into other's ideas and perspectives is vital for peaceful coexistence.

As much as open-mindedness can be a treasure to our relationships with others, it creates the perfect opening for prejudices to creep in, most times even unconsciously.

In the first book - Diversity and Inclusion in Organizations, we have already seen prejudice as a preconceived judgment of a certain group of people simply because they are members of that group. It also entails the opinions and attitudes we direct towards these people.

Prejudice was treated as a tendency to over-categorize and was seen to be unjustified and incorrect. It's just a set of attitudes that offer some form of support or reasons for the justification of discrimination and is done solely because of people's membership in a social group. An example is a person holding prejudiced views towards another for the sheer reason of the latter being a member of a particular race or gender.

Prejudice is not always discrimination. We know discrimination to mean behaviors or actions directed towards an individual or group, most especially based on the social group they belong to. These behaviors are mostly negative.

Prejudice can occur without discrimination. In essence, prejudice makes do of all the three components of an attitude which are affective, behavioral, and cognitive, while discrimination, on the other hand, involves only behavior.

The prejudiced views open-minded people hold are often unconscious. They have these views or mindsets without even being aware that they do. This has provoked the topic of Unconscious Bias, which we shall be looking into.

Unconscious (or implicit) bias as a term was first brought into light in 1995 by Mahzarin Banaji and Anthony Greenwald, both psychologists who argued that social behavior is influenced significantly by associations and judgments which are carried out unconsciously.

Since then, psychologists have carried out many extensive pieces of research on implicit bias, and results have proved that we all possess a form of implicit biases of our own even without knowing it.

According to a distinction by Kahneman in 2011, there are two different types of thinking – system 1 and system 2.

System 1 thinking involves the mode of the brain where its thinking is fast, emotional, and unconscious. Little effort is required for this type, but there are usually some errors here and there. Type 1 system is applied extensively in our everyday lives. It is required for activities like talking, cleaning, driving, etc.

The type 2 system, on the other hand, makes use of logical thoughts where effort and reason are vital. It is also done slowly and consciously.

These two terms are very important in understanding how the human mind works, but the first one – Type 1 or System 1 thinking-is of more importance to us, as it is the root of unconscious bias.

Implicit Bias vs. Explicit Bias

Implicit bias, as we have seen, is just the attitudes and beliefs that occur without our conscious awareness and control. It is an example of system 1 thinking, and we could not even be aware of its existence.

In implicit bias, a person can hold beliefs that are contradictory to their conscious beliefs without even realizing it. For instance, it is possible for one to publicly or openly express honest liking of a particular social group while at the same time being unconsciously biased against that same group. This shows that a single individual can hold implicit and explicit biases differently.

Also, implicit bias can become an explicit bias as well. This happens when the prejudices and beliefs you hold become known to your consciousness. At this point, you are consciously aware of them and can choose if you want to act on or against them.

Explicit biases are those biases that we are consciously aware of, and they are examples of the system 2 thinking treated earlier (in Diversity and Inclusion in the Organizations). A demonstration of explicit bias is being threatened by or scared of another group and subjecting to dropping hate speech against them as a result.

Many factors influence how implicit and explicit biases are developed. Therefore, yours may differ from your friend's or even your family members.

Our focus, unconscious or implicit bias, is how an opinion about a specific situation or a person is quickly formed by you without knowing it, which is deeply rooted in psychology. In a later stage of this book, we will see that our brain is responsible for forming these biases. It uses the knowledge and previous experiences on social situations, stereotypes, attitudes, cultures, emotional reactions, and many more. We gain this knowledge or experiences from our daily activities and exposure to media throughout our lives.

In recruitment, unconscious bias plays a very significant role in skewing your overall judgment on many levels. The norm is using your experience in the assessment of applicants, but it becomes a concern if you have your assumptions, expectations, and preferences influence your decision too much. It is still not ideal for letting your feelings result in positive bias as it will still lead to unfair favoring. For instance, if you associate with intelligence and choose a candidate who went to a high-profile university, it is still an example of harmful unconscious bias. Having a higher education or attending a better university doesn't automatically make a candidate more intelligent or more qualified than others.

With your biases reined in, recruitment would definitely become challenging, and first impressions are also significant during interviews. Nevertheless, avoiding unconscious bias is extremely important because it can lead to many negative results such as unfair and inaccurate judgments, overlooked talent, or discrimination in the worst-case scenario.

How Unconscious Bias Occur in Different Forms

Unconscious bias takes many forms, but we shall look at its common examples in the workplace. This aims at improving your awareness of the various forms so you can become more self-aware and have a better understanding of the topic at hand. In a later stage of this book, we will still treat more forms, but the 9 most common examples of unconscious bias in the workplace are; Affinity bias, attribution bias, beauty bias, conformity bias, confirmation bias, contrast bias, gender bias, halo effect, and horns effect.

Affinity Bias: This is an occasion where one unconsciously prefers people who share certain qualities with them or with someone they like. It is made possible by your brain seeing those people be familiar and relatable, and everyone wants to be around people they can relate well to.

An example here is if an applicant comes from the same school as you. Another is if they share similar hobbies with you. In these cases, there's a higher chance that you'll prefer them over others.

Recruiting people on the basis that they have similar qualities with you is not the right move in any way. If you want an identical culture in your business, you might want to hire like-minded people. But it would be best if you didn't let affinity bias cloud your judgment. If you do, your workplace might only end up lacking diverse personalities, thereby decreasing the level and quality of creative views.

Also, it is very unfair; as applicants who isn't like you can be just as talented or even more talented than those who are.

Attribution bias: This type of bias entails the perception of your actions and that of others. It is based on our brain's flawed ability to make accessions on the reasons for certain behaviors, most especially those who end up on success and failure.

In general, the human mind tends to take responsibility and praise its skills and personality for its accomplishments while blaming failures on other factors or making excuses for them. In essence, we are all less likely to find faults in ourselves most times.

However, this is reversed when it comes to our views on others. We always tend to tag their successes as luck while attributing their failures or errors to lack of skills.

In recruitment or the workplace, this type of bias can cloud your judgment on a candidate or your colleagues or workers. It makes you unconsciously focus on their faults and ignore most of their accomplishments.

Beauty Bias: It is normal for us humans to quickly notice people's appearances and choose to relate them with their personalities. Hence, the coining of the term "dress as you want to be addressed." Appearances are very important, especially in workplace settings, because they generally reflect professionalism. However, many people are guilty of judging people too harshly on their physical attractiveness.

It is never fair to conclude on the number of efforts people make based on their appearance. It is even worse when you start to judge their skill level by their physical attractiveness. You shouldn't always assume that a person is organized in an all-around manner just because they dress professionally. The reverse could be true.

In other cases, you might unconsciously dislike an individual because there are certain features about them that you don't like. An example could be that they lack height, have bad posture, or do not possess an expressive face. These are all common stereotypical views of how a serious, successful, or qualified person should look like.

Having these assumptions will only push you to make unfair decisions that favor attractive people in the workplace. Therefore, we should all make efforts to portray the saying "do not judge a book by its cover."

Conformity Bias: This form of bias occurs when your decisions are heavily influenced by other people or by a third party. It is brought into fruition by our natural tendency to seek acceptance from others by always striving to hold opinions accepted by society.

Conformity bias is quite common in the workplace. People tend to go by the views held by the majority group on certain issues and usually choose to dump their contrasting original views to go with the majority.

In interviews, people may develop strong feelings towards a candidate because they noticed something similar. However, it is not good to let these opinions prevent you from honestly accessing the candidate as you never can tell what influenced the thinking of the majority.

5 Confirmation Bias: Confirmation bias has to do with occasions where people opt to search for pieces of evidence that support their opinions instead of looking at the whole picture. It may sound like a good approach, but in reality, it will only result in selective observation where you ignore every other information that doesn't fit your view to focus on those that support you, no matter how wrong you are.

For example, having to interview a candidate that came late 10 minutes might clouds your judgment throughout the interview as you already concluded from the onset that they lack organizational skills. While conducting the interview, you keep being only focused on any information or idea that backs up your initial judgment.

This particular form of bias is found in many people, as we all usually seek confirmation that our initial judgment is right. Confirmation bias is also used to back up other forms of bias and is therefore very important that we keep it in check.

Contrast effect: The contrast effect is visible when two things are compared to each other instead of being judged based on their merits.

This type of bias is also prevalent in the workplace, especially in recruitment. Typically, if you want to narrow your options and reduce your work, it can be very useful to compare applications when you have too many CVs on your desk.

However, opting for this can make your standards too high and harsh. If your goal is to find people who can suit the role, you will only end up overlooking that goal, focusing on those who have the most excellent CV.

Gender Bias: This is simply the preference for a particular gender over the other. It is heavily influenced by the deep views and beliefs we hold about roles of genders and stereotypes.

In our workplaces, most especially in interviews, this form of bias can make you unconsciously favor a candidate based on their gender and qualities you've already associated with them just for belonging to that gender. A perfect and common example is unconsciously believing that a man is more deserving of a physically demanding job than a woman. Another is favoring a woman for a chef's job over a man simply because you believe women are to be better chefs than men.

Most notably, gender bias occurs when we relate better with colleagues because they are of the same gender or of the gender that we prefer. We can usually connect better with people of the same gender because they are more likely to have shared gender-specific experiences.

Halo Effect: This is apparent in situations where we are more focused on a desirable feature of a person. Seeing that feature makes you conclude the person is perfect, and all your views about them are positive and in a "halo" light. Just as in affinity and confirmation bias, we are made to overlook other information about the person. This cloud our judgment in other aspects, even the negative ones.

In workplaces and recruitments, if you don't want to hire or work with someone who turns out to be unfit for a role when the halo glow wears off, then you should try your best to prevent this form of unconscious bias.

Horns Effect: This is directly opposite to the halo effect. Here, you focus on a particularly negative attitude of a person, which skews your judgment on their other qualities, even the good ones.

For instance, if someone uses a phrase we dislike, we automatically ignore or dislike everything else they say.

In your workplace or recruitment, we must learn how to avoid focusing on features of a person we dislike. One mistake or flaw about them doesn't automatically define them entirely.

Is it right to hold biased opinions against people?

According to research, people instinctively classify others based on common criteria in general. These criteria or factors include; age, skin color, gender, and weight. People can also be classified based on educational level, accent, social status, job title, sexuality, and disability. We also have presumed traits that we subconsciously relate to these groups and automatically kick off whenever we classify any new person into these groups.

One might argue that this system is advantageous because the time and effort that would have been spent in processing information about people is saved. This creates the avenue for us to focus our attention on more important tasks.

However, there are many disadvantages associated with this, and the worst is that it can influence us to make unfair and wrong assumptions about people and act accordingly. This makes people rely more on stereotypes, even if we don't consciously believe in them.

No matter how unbiased we may consider ourselves to be, there is a good chance that we have a level of subconscious negative opinion about a particular group of people different from ours. The only way there is a low chance of feeling prejudice against these people is if we get more exposure.

Many people are unaware of their prejudices and always tend to try to hide them. However, these prejudices can have disastrous consequences for our management level and the response of the people being managed by us. According to various studies, the way victims of discrimination feel can affect them in many ways. In workplaces, most especially, it can affect their commitment, job satisfaction, and work tension, consequently harming their overall performance.

In another instance, if your workers or colleagues feel that you are being influenced by unconscious bias when conducting management reviews, they may feel they are just victims of the consequences of your discrimination. This can significantly affect your organization as there will be mistrust and low morale in your ranks. In the worst outcome, you can even lose highly qualified employees.

With a team member who felt discriminated against by you leaving your organization, it may not only result in you losing a possible gem but also spoil your reputation. At worst, if the victim is to take legal actions, it could cost you considerably. Therefore, make sure you understand the discrimination laws in the country you stay and work. Afterward, read further into this book to help you understand how and why you are capable of controlling unconscious bias and how to overcome it.

Chapter 2 - An Encounter with Mind Bugs

With the brain often referred to as one giant supercomputer, it's no surprise that it shares some of the strengths of these high-level performance computers and some of its weaknesses. With the software of a computer often having instances of errors or flaws, the human brain also has its fair share in the form of unconscious bias described as 'mind bugs' by Harvard professor Mahzarin Banaji.

Picture an American driving home after a long day of work to get home to bed. If you've done this, you would have automatically pictured a white man in the vehicle with no subconscious thought of assigning a race or a gender, the likelihood of picturing a different race and gender is highly unlikely though possible. The possibility of picturing a white male is aggressively high because we have automatically attributed a white person to be American even without mentioning the race in the statement. Why? Because the flooded ideas that associate these concepts together expressed by the mainstream media, books, movies, etc., all leave an imprint of that idea in your mind.

Mind bugs are perception shapeshifters (changers) that unconsciously control our mental activity without us knowing or giving full thought about them.

Mind bugs not only exist in instances of memory, but there are also social mind bugs, visual mind bugs (as in the case of the checkerboard by Ted Adelson), memory mind bugs where the brain creates false memories, sometimes in a desperate attempt to shield us from trauma or a false testimony given by a witness because of the mind. The mind sometimes automatically fills in the gap or gives a false recount or memory of what we perceive to have happen or what we saw in the past.

In an experiment held in the late 1970s by the American Symphony Orchestras, an attempt was made to show whether there were or were not biases in picking the members of their orchestras as the population consisted entirely of men. The experiment involved an audition for the position to join the orchestra, but during auditioning, they had to perform with a blind, like a curtain shielding the eyes from the performer. After the audition was carried out in this format, they noticed something interesting. The number of successful women in the audition increased by a whopping 50%. This experiment clearly showed that although the American symphony orchestra was looking for the best sound, the unconscious mind affected their decision to a large extent. The eyes sending signals to the brain because of the gender of the performer; even though their reflective mind had no bias against women and was ultimately still seeking the better sound, their unconscious (automatic) mind still rated the male higher than the women. To this day, American symphony orchestras use this method of shielding their vision during an audition to get into the program.

This experiment and many others prove how the eye unconsciously tells the mind how to react and how it can affect our decision-making.

However, this is just an example of an area where visual mind bugs are pronounced. There are several other types, and I will be explaining them later in the next part of this chapter.

TYPES OF MIND BUGS

There are diverse ranges of mind bugs. However, the major categories they are grouped into are; virtual mind bugs, memory mind bugs, availability and anchoring of mind bugs, and social mind bugs.

VISUAL MINDBUGS

Visual mind bugs (as in the case of the American symphony orchestra) are visual illusions that often produce an error in the mind's ability to perceive objects as they are inherent habits of reasoning that lead to flaws in how

we see, remember, reason, and make judgments. Unconscious interference is a term coined by nineteenth-century German physicist and physiologist Hermann von Helmholtz to explain the visual illusions that the mind ascribes automatically and without conscious thought to create visual impressions such that even after finding out that our eyes have played tricks on us; we cannot convince ourselves otherwise.

An example of unconscious interference is the Kanizsa's triangle by Gaetano Kanizsa in 1976, which is an illusory contour. Although discovered by Friedrich Schumann in the twentieth century, illusory contour gained a resurgence in interest when the Kanizsa triangle was introduced in 1976 by Gaetano, an illusory contour or subjective contour.

Looking at the Kanizsa triangle, your mind automatically creates a white triangle that is floating in the midst of the other separate fragments. The brain needs to make sense of the shape filling in the gaps like an incomplete puzzle.

This mechanism that produces the floating triangle exists to enable us to see and understand the world successfully.

Visual mind bugs are a form of unconscious interference that causes us to try to connect two or more things in relation to their shape, position, size, and other apparent qualities of those objects most times without positive or accurate results leading to a misunderstanding of what is supposed to be seen.

What unconscious interference does is that it makes you highly prone to being tricked by optical illusions. For instance, here are two tables that I want you to examine. Take your time to view it for as long as you want.

You will notice that these tables are not of the same size or shape. The table on the left is longer and will probably hold many more things than the one on the right. The one on the right is quite good, with a nice support system using sturdy legs. It can be used for a stool.

If that is what you thought, then you've fallen

prey to the visual illusion of the Shepard Tables.

The Shepard tabletop illusion, also named Shepard Tables, refers to the optical illusion invented by an award-winning psychologist, Roger Shepard, a pioneer in research dealing with spatial relations. This illusion alongside others is included in Shepard's book 'Mind Sights: ambiguities, original visual illusions, and other anomalies, with a discourse on the play of mind in perception and art' which he published in 1990. He refers to this particular illusion as "Turning the tables." When Mr. Shepard was still active, he derived pleasure from tricking students, lecturers, and even professors with this illusion of his whenever he went for lectures in other universities (well, apart from the one where his tricks are well known and he is currently honored as Professor Emeritus - Stanford University). Classifications of Implicit Association Tests

IATs are classified into several groups; some of them include:

Valence Implicit Association Tests (Valence IATs): Valence, also known as hedonic tone or emotional value, refers to a psychological term used to describe the ability of a person to react with another person or thing by attraction or the help of a function or ability. Positive valence attracts, and negative valence repels.

This type of IAT calculates links between theories and negative or positive valence. The test measures people's affinity for specific things or people related to certain concepts. Stereotype IATs: Stereotype Implicit Association Tests measure connections to which individuals hold on to familiar stereotypes. An example of this is the Gender-career IAT which has shown from general results that people associate men with being concerned about having jobs and being productive in them than having an interest in family matters and vice versa for women. Brief IATs: BIATs, Brief Implicit Association Tests employs a method analogous to the general IAT but does not have as many classifications as the original. It comprises fewer tasks and fewer reiterations. A single aspect is specified rather than the usual multiple and the central theory of each task. The abeyance rather than accurately identifying the specific concept and characteristic demanded is used to assess the candidate and formulate the result.

Self-esteem Implicit Association Tests: This category of IATs measures the subconscious self-esteem of individuals by requiring them to match 'self' with either positive or negative words. STEREOTYPING

Psychology defines "stereotype" as the generalization of belief of a particular class of people. The word stereotype is derived from French and Greek words, which translates to "Solid impressions". The word was first used in 1798 in a printing trade by Firmin Didot. It was also used outside of printing in 1850 and used in a psychological sense in 1922. It involves attributing a particular behavior, characteristics, or ability of one person as a universal identity of all group members. Stereotyping helps humans to group people into "us" and "them". Stereotyping is a learned behavior, although it is tough to explicitly determine at the first instance where an individual learned a particular stereotype. It may range from learning from neighbors, friends, and family to a learned behavior from media outlets and society. The under presentation of certain minority groups in the media also promotes stereotyping or ultimately endorses stereotyping.

Stereotyping is grouped into implicit stereotypes and explicit stereotypes. Explicit biases are conscious beliefs or stereotypes a person is aware of and can deliberately use to judge people of different groups, while implicit stereotypes lie within the subconscious and pre-reflective. Individuals might not be aware that they hold such beliefs. In explicit bias, the individual is aware of his bias but may not express them publicly to paint himself positively in society.

Stereotyping may occur due to experience by an individual from a particular group and seen as a social construct to categorize people into groups, in groups, and outgroups.

CORRESPONDENCE BIAS

Coined by Lee Ross, correspondence bias is the tendency to underestimate the structural explanation of an individual's personality and overemphasize the person's behavior to their inherent personality. A typical example is when person A is pushed past by person B in a particular space, with person B not even apologizing and continues on his way. Person A automatically labels person B as a jerk, characterizing him on his personality alone without thinking that person B may be hurriedly rushing to the hospital as person B may have been called and told that his wife is in the labor room, hence prompting person B to act in that manner based on the situation. Correspondence bias can be seen every day in society and sometimes encouraged by our unconsciousness and, in turn, favor ourselves as we rate ourselves higher, believing we are not capable of such.

Case Study 1: The Proposal That Went Unaccepted.

The Green Electric Company developed a new current regulator designed to cut the electric power consumption in aluminum plants by 35% by working day and night tirelessly. Although the competition was much, they were aware that their regulator was designed to be cheaper, more reliable, and more efficient than the competitors' products.

The owner, who was eager to capture the market, personally but somewhat in a hurry, put together a 140-page proposal to the three major aluminum manufacturers, recommending them the regulators his company made to be installed at all company plants.

She wrote the first 87 pages of the proposal about the mathematical theory and engineering design behind this new regulator and the next 52 to describe the new assembly line she planned to set up to produce regulators quickly. The test results were buried under the appendix that compared her regulator's performance with present models and a weakly drawn graph that showed how much the dollar savings would be.

Green Electric didn't get any contracts, despite having the best product. The company filed for bankruptcy just after eight months.
