**Traditional masculine norms and societal expectations act as significant barriers to men seeking help.** Across various contexts, including intimate partner violence (IPV) and mental health, the sources highlight that rigid definitions of manhood actively discourage vulnerability and the admission of distress.

**The "Masculinity Narrative" and the Stigma of Weakness**
The reluctance of men to seek help is deeply intertwined with the pervasive **"masculinity narrative,"** which dictates that men should be powerful, independent, self-sufficient, and capable of protecting themselves [1]. Because of these expectations, asking for help can challenge a man's internally and externally held beliefs about his own gender [1].

* **The Male Ego and Vulnerability:** To protect a traditionally constructed ego, men are conditioned to hide their emotions [2]. Admitting a need for help is often perceived as an **"ego bruise"** because it contradicts the ideal of the self-sufficient, invulnerable man [2].
* **Misinterpreted Stoicism:** Societal pressure often conflates emotional suppression with strength. Men are taught to suppress emotions deemed "weak," such as sadness or fear, leading to a reluctance to seek help for mental health challenges [3, 4].

**Help-Seeking for Intimate Partner Violence (IPV)**
In the context of IPV, male victims face a **"stigmatized gender"** status [1, 5]. The label of "victim" implies passivity and weakness, which contradicts societal expectations of masculinity [1].

* **Internal and External Barriers:** Men report avoiding help due to shame, embarrassment, denial of their victim status, fear of losing their children, and fear of not being taken seriously or being falsely accused of being the abuser [6, 7]. One participant noted the internalized stigma, stating, "We must man up and take all punishment. If we act or call for help, we are not a man" [8].
* **Negative Responses to Disclosure:** When male victims do attempt to seek help, their fears are often validated. Many report facing **discreditation, disbelief, and ridicule** from professional services, friends, and family [9, 10]. Men often face a cultural assumption that IPV is strictly a problem of men's violence toward women, leading to scenarios where male victims are dismissed as liars or accused of being the "real" abusers [11, 12].

**Mental Health and Suicide Prevention**
The reluctance to disclose psychological distress has severe consequences for men's mental health. The pressure to conform to traditional masculinity and avoid vulnerability contributes to untreated mental health conditions and higher rates of suicide among men [13]. Even though men are highly susceptible to distress from social isolation, unemployment, and substance misuse, they are less likely to engage with specialist mental health services [14, 15].

**Effective Interventions and Redefining Strength**
To combat these barriers, the sources suggest that interventions must be specifically tailored to address male reluctance.

* **Male-Sensitive Primary Care:** Primary care is a critical intervention point, as many men who die by suicide consult a general practitioner shortly before their death [15]. Studies show that using **male-tailored clinical tools**, such as multimedia programs designed to overcome male-specific barriers like "toughness" and the fear of stigma, can significantly increase a man's likelihood of disclosing suicidal thoughts to a doctor [16, 17].
* **Embracing Emotional Intelligence:** The sources advocate for an evolution in how masculinity is defined. Instead of emotional suppression, a healthy male psyche should embrace **emotional intelligence** [18]. Acknowledging struggles, expressing emotions, and asking for help are not signs of weakness, but rather demonstrations of immense courage, self-awareness, and human strength [19].
