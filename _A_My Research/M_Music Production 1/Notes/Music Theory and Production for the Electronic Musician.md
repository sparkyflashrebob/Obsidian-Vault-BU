## I. The Foundational Role of Music Theory

Music theory is defined as the **body of ideas that helps familiarize musicians with the aesthetic space of music** [4, 5]. It addresses the fundamental structures of sound: harmony, melody, and rhythm [5].

### Theory as a Tool for Musicians

For those involved in contemporary styles—such as rock, funk, blues, pop, country, jazz, or electronic dance music (EDM)—music theory is **vital** [6-9].

* **Enhancing Creativity:** Theory allows musicians to use their intellect to **focus and enhance their creativity** [7, 8]. It provides a framework to clarify abstract aspects of music and make the intangible tangible [10].
* **Compositional Mastery:** Composers need a deep understanding of music theory to achieve goals like creating a feeling of **stabilization or uncertainty** in a composition, or to prolong an idea effectively [11]. Studying theory provides the necessary knowledge and techniques to compose music effectively [12-14].
* **Theory Follows Practice:** Importantly, music theory is often **descriptive rather than prescriptive**; it was developed to help explain patterns in music that already existed, rather than to dictate future rules [15, 16].
* **Literacy vs. Theory:** The sources distinguish between **music theory** (the conceptual rules behind chords, scales, and harmony) and **music literacy** (the ability to read and write music) [17]. While one can exist without the other, they are closely related, and understanding notation is necessary to grasp modern music theory [18, 19].

### Theory for the Computer Musician

For the computer musician (or music producer), knowledge of music theory is essential for producing professional-sounding tracks [9, 20].

* **Filling the Knowledge Gap:** Many computer musicians who do not read conventional scores or have classical training are at a disadvantage when learning harmony, which is indispensable for professional composers [21, 22]. Books aim to break this barrier by presenting core theory knowledge through accessible mediums like the **Piano Roll view of a sequencer** [22, 23].
* **Universals of Music:** Despite the vast differences in styles of modern electronic music, there are certain fundamentals of the musical language—the **universals**—that remain the same regardless of age, culture, or style [9, 24]. These fundamentals include pitch, intensity, and tone quality (timbre) [25, 26].

## II. Core Music Theory Concepts

The sources detail fundamental concepts required for effective composition and production:

### Scales, Modes, and Harmony

All chords, no matter how complex, are essentially the result of a combination of **intervals** [27].

* **Scale Fundamentals:** Basic scales discussed include the Major Scale, Minor Scale & Relatives, Harmonic Minor, and Melodic Minor [28-30]. The relationship between Major and Minor scales, as well as their corresponding triads, are foundational elements [28].
* **Modal Usage:** Modes, often referred to as "Greek modes," "church modes," or "jazz modes," have been used for centuries as **compositional tools** and provide a source of melodic and harmonic variations [31-33].
* **Exotic and Advanced Scales:** Exotic scales are noted as a good source for inspiration, helping producers avoid a creative rut and create unusual, exotic atmospheres in electronic music [34]. Examples include the **Mixolydian flat 6** (Mixolydian #4) [35, 36] and the **Phrygian mode** [37].
* **Functional Harmony:** The principles of **functional harmony** concern the practical use of harmony, focusing on how to connect one chord to another within an overall chord progression [38, 39]. Harmony should typically **fit in between the rhythmic interplay** of the lead and bass instruments without drawing too much attention, and should be closely related to the key of the track [40].

### Rhythm and Time

Rhythm is described as the **essential heartbeat and lifeblood** of the musical language [41].

* **Rhythm vs. Tempo:** Rhythm is independent of tempo, or the speed at which music is played [42].
* **Rhythm in Production:** Rhythm and drum programming are paramount in modern computer-generated music, especially in electronic dance music (EDM) [43, 44]. The drums provide a clear sense of time and rhythm over which other parts can be laid down [45, 46].
* **Natural Rhythms:** To avoid mechanical tracks in electronic music, attention should be paid to humanizing elements like **swing** (the natural tendency to slightly delay weaker beats) and adjusting the **velocity curve** of individual drum sounds [47].

## III. Music Production Techniques and Workflows

For computer musicians, composition is often synonymously referred to as **music production** [1, 2]. Music production encompasses understanding how to write effective drum tracks, compelling basslines, and soaring melodic leads [12].

### The Producer's Workflow and Mindset

The production process is a multi-staged endeavor.

* **Workflow Subjectivity:** There is **no objective right answer** for the optimal workflow (e.g., whether to focus on sound design, composition, arranging, and mixing separately or simultaneously) [48, 49]. Producers must find their own successful workflow by **trying out various methods**, keeping the parts that work, rejecting those that do not, and synthesizing a personal approach [49].
* **Starting Point:** Many computer musicians, particularly in electronic genres, prefer to **lay down the drum track first** because it establishes a clear sense of time, rhythm, and style, providing a strong stimulus for the rest of the composition [45, 46, 50].
* **Reference Tracks:** Using a reference track—a successful, mastered piece of music in a similar style and tempo—is a valuable tool in music production [51]. Reference tracks help analyze structure and compare the loudness of elements (like the bass), which can be genre-specific [51].

### Sound Design, Synthesis, and Mixing

The electronic medium relies on an understanding of **acoustic science and basic electronics** [52].

* **Synthesis and Timbre:** The **analogue subtractive synthesizer** remains the single strongest musical component in EDM production, though virtual instruments have made more complex forms of synthesis (like FM, additive, and granular) more viable [53].
  + **Modulation** is essential for creating professional timbre, involving modulating multiple destinations from multiple sources [54].
  + The producer must be familiar with how basic oscillators (like sine, triangle, and saw waves) sound individually and when mixed together, which requires extensive practice and experimentation [55, 56].
* **Managing the Low End:** Low-end issues are crucial, and solutions go beyond traditional production techniques (EQ, compression) [57]. Compositional ideas for improving low-end clarity are recommended [57].
* **The Art of Mixing:** Mixing blends a collection of audio clips into a finished composition [58]. It is implicated in many phases of the electronic music composition process and must be viewed within the context of the whole composition [58, 59]. **Compressors** are essential in the dance musician's toolbox; their "outright abuse" has come to define part of the sound of electronic dance music [60].
* **Gaining Efficiency:** Having pre-prepared sounds (like drum kits and racks) can help producers get more into the **flow of just creating** [61].

### Specialized Component Writing

Compositional principles guide the writing of individual tracks:

* **Basslines:** Basslines can either make or break a track in electronic music [62]. While the compositional principles for basslines remain constant (supporting harmony, having melodic identity, moving the music forward), the means of production often differ [63]. **Step-basses** (arpeggiating bass patterns) are a common phenomenon in electronic music, born from using synthesizers' arpeggiators to achieve rapidity and figurative agility beyond manual playing [64].
* **Acoustic and Percussion Instruments:** Writing for acoustic instruments or samples requires research into how they are played, including characteristic techniques and their temperament (tuning) [65, 66]. Percussion instruments from other traditions enrich musical language, but the producer’s focus is on how those drums are played and the sounds they offer for incorporation into tracks [67, 68].

## IV. The Electronic Medium and Theory Divergence

The practice of electronic music has evolved from long-established acoustic traditions [69]. However, the sources note significant philosophical and practical differences between traditional theory and the electronic medium:

* **Limitations of Traditional Theory:** The **traditional canon of Western music theory** (with its emphasis on 12-note equal-tempered pitch relations) offers **limited help** in understanding the materials and organization of the electronic medium [69]. Traditional theory is "note-oriented and score-bound" [69].
* **New Aesthetic Focus:** Electronic music emphasizes elements beyond fixed pitch relations, focusing instead on sound as a **sonic, spatial, or psychoacoustic phenomenon** [69].
* **Heterogeneous Materials:** Unlike the closed, homogeneous set of notes in traditional music, the sound objects of electronic music are **extremely heterogeneous**, and sonic transformation is ubiquitous [69].
* **New Concepts:** Electronic music composition engages concepts like the **pitch-noise continuum** [70, 71], where pitch can be treated as a continuous and freely flowing substance rather than a fixed quantity [72]. Elements traditionally studied in depth include: the nature of sound, creating sound materials, sound transformation, processes of rhythm, pitch in electronic music, and articulating space [73, 74].
* **Composition Strategy vs. Analysis:** It is vital to distinguish between **music analysis** (trying to find a score in the waveforms of complex electronic music) and **composition strategy** (the ideas a composer uses to conceptualize and realize a piece) [75-77]. The analysis of electronic music is problematic, as there are often no standardized units like notes [75].
