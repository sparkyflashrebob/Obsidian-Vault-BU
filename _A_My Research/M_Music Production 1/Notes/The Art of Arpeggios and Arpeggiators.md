**Arpeggios** are defined as the notes of a chord sounded one after another, rather than simultaneously [1-4]. The term "arpeggio" is derived from the Italian word "arpeggiare," which refers to playing notes on a harp [5].

Here's a comprehensive look at arpeggios:

* **Definition and Formation**

  + An arpeggio occurs when the notes comprising a chord are played as a series, one after another, instead of all at once [5].
  + In their simplest form, arpeggios play the notes of a chord successively [6].
  + They are like "broken" chord-tones, composed of intervals of a 3rd or larger, rather than scale-tones [7].
  + Any chord can be broken down into an interesting melody by playing its chord tones with different note values, such as quarter, eighth, and sixteenth notes, or a mixture of them [2].
  + Traditionally, arpeggios are played upward unless indicated with a down arrow [7].
* **Purpose and Effect**

  + Arpeggios are a great songwriting tool when **movement is desired instead of a static chord** [3].
  + They **create a sense of motion and forward movement** within what would otherwise be a field of static harmonies [6, 8].
  + They can provide a very effective and atmospheric way of bringing a sense of motion to an otherwise static string chord texture [9].
  + Arpeggios can generate **active layers of music** in any required register, helping to push the music forward and give it a sense of motion and impetus [10].
* **Playing and Notation**

  + On a piano or harp, instruments that typically do not have much sustain, arpeggios allow a harmony to be sustained through any number of bars [6].
  + A simple example is the picking technique of guitar players, where plucking different strings in succession produces a sense of motion that affects the chord being used [11].
  + Arpeggios may appear as simple broken chords or in extended form, covering two or more octaves [12].
  + Only four kinds of chords are customarily studied as arpeggios: major and minor triads (in 3 positions) and the dominant 7th and diminished 7th chords (in 4 positions) [12].
  + The difficulties in fingering triad arpeggios successfully (major, minor, & all inversions) are slight compared to scale playing [13].
  + When writing arpeggios, a suitable pattern can be evolved for one bar and then copied and pasted; the relevant notes are then changed to follow the harmony [14].
  + For greater control, arpeggio patterns can be written directly onto a sequencer track using a pencil tool [15, 16].
  + In the piano roll view, rests are not represented in arpeggios; they appear simply as empty spaces [17].
* **Arpeggiators**

  + An **arpeggiator** is a main tool used by computer musicians to generate looped material [18, 19].
  + It produces no sound itself; it is a mechanical generator of signals that controls the tempo, pitch, velocity, gate, and other parameters of a sound produced by a synthesizer or sampler [18].
  + Arpeggiators can produce complex, intricate, and rapid patterns of sound that a live performer would often struggle to play [18].
  + A synthesizer's arpeggiator can be used to create automatic melodic sequences; one simply plays a chord, and the synthesizer does the rest [3, 20].
  + Features of an arpeggiator often include:
    - **Monophonic/Polyphonic** capabilities [21].
    - **Pitch Control**: fixed pitch mode (useful for drums, basslines) or normal mode (movable pitch, follows chord played) [22]. HALO features a 16-step arpeggiator/sequencer with scale control for random note generation within a chosen scale (e.g., minor) [23, 24].
    - **Number of Octaves**: determines the range an arpeggio pattern will have (e.g., a four-octave range) [25].
    - **Note Resolution**: sets the note value for each step (e.g., eighth, sixteenth) [25].
    - **Gate Control**: decides the length of each step relative to the note resolution, allowing for more lifelike and rhythmically articulated patterns [25].
    - **Velocity Control**: defines the relative volume of the pattern, allowing for dynamic variation [26].
    - **Tempo Control**: syncs the arpeggio with the exact speed of the track [26].
    - **Swing Control**: determines the degree of swing [26].
    - **Pattern Control**: selects, edits, and sequences preloaded or custom patterns [27]. Serum offers various shapes like Up, Down, Converge, Diverge, and random options [28].
    - **Curve Control**: generates control data curves for gating, filtering, and other modulation patterns [27].
  + Arpeggiators are used in virtually every style of electronic music to generate active layers [10].
  + They can be used to create idiomatic styles of writing characteristic of particular instruments, such as a harp accompaniment to a string passage [9].
* **Non-Chord Tones in Arpeggios**

  + Arpeggiation becomes more complex and musically interesting when referencing **non-chord tones** [29].
  + A non-chord tone is any note that does not form part of the chord [29].
  + **Passing notes** are common non-chord tones, filling the space between two chordal tones a third apart [29, 30]. Their use provides melodic interest without affecting the implied harmony [30].
  + Other types of non-harmonic tones (such as returning tones or auxiliaries) can be introduced to enhance melodic interest [31, 32].
* **Steps and Patterns in Arpeggios**

  + Arpeggios are ordinarily in four, eight, sixteen, or thirty-two steps, synchronizing with the bar [33].
  + **Odd step numbers** (e.g., five-step [34], seven-step [35], and eleven-step patterns [35]) can create amazing effects because different notes are highlighted on the strong points of the measure, generating inner melodies [34].
  + When writing a riff using an arpeggio, establishing **melodic guide tones** first provides the melodic shape and character around which the riff can be built [36, 37]. The arpeggio notes (root, third, fifth) then fill in the remaining rhythmic spaces [37].
