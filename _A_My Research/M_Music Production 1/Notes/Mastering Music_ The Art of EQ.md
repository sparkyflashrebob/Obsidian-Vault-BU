Mastering EQ is a crucial step in the final stages of music production, aimed at optimizing a mixed composition for distribution on various mediums [1-3]. It involves polishing and shaping the track to ensure it has comparable levels to commercially produced recordings and sounds its best [1].

Here's a comprehensive look at mastering EQ:

### Purpose and Goals of Mastering EQ

The primary goal of mastering EQ is to achieve a good overall tonal balance and enhance the impact and spectral balance of the music [4-6]. It helps to refine the mix, making it sound more professional and ready for different playback systems, such as club sound systems or personal MP3 collections [4, 7].

### Key Practices and Recommendations

* **Cuts vs. Boosts**: Generally, it is often recommended to **cut problematic frequencies rather than boost desirable ones** for a more natural sound and a fuller overall mix [8-11]. Our ears are more accustomed to hearing a reduction in frequencies, similar to acoustic absorption in real-world environments [8, 9]. However, a great mix can involve both cuts and subtle boosts to enhance an instrument's character and help it stand out [8, 9]. If boosting, it should be done discreetly and gently [12].
* **Q Settings**: The "Q" (Quality) setting of an EQ determines the **bandwidth of affected frequencies** [13-15].
  + **Wider Q settings** are generally preferred for shaping a large area of frequencies and for overall mastering enhancements, often producing more natural results [16-19]. A Q setting of 1.0 (or 1 1/3 octave) is often referred to as the "magic Q" for general use [16, 20].
  + **Narrower Q settings** are typically used for removing problematic frequencies [11, 16].
  + For melodic instruments or vocals, wider Q settings (e.g., two octaves) are often preferred, while drums and percussion benefit from a Q of half an octave [20].
* **Listening Environment and Monitoring**: The playback or **monitoring system used in mastering is critical** [21, 22]. Professional mastering houses invest in accurate listening environments with low distortion and linear frequency response [21].
  + It is essential to **check a master on several different playback systems** (headphones, car stereos, home loudspeakers) in addition to reference monitors, as reference monitors may not account for all listening situations [21, 23, 24].
  + Monitoring at a **fixed, moderate volume** (around conversation level, 85 dB) is advisable to avoid ear fatigue and ensure a proportionately balanced mix [24-27]. The Fletcher-Munson contour control should be kept in mind, as bass elements balanced at a low monitoring level may appear louder at higher volumes, and vice versa [24-26].
* **Ear Training and Experience**: Mastering EQ is not something that can be grasped quickly; it requires a **lot of study, research, and experimentation** [28]. The importance of experience is especially critical, as mastery derives from a combination of experience, attention to detail, and aesthetic sensitivity, bolstered by solid audio theory knowledge [7, 29]. A skilled mastering engineer has "experienced ears" and can offer an objective perspective [7, 30]. **Actively listening** to commercial mixes and identifying frequencies and techniques is a good way to train your ears [31, 32].
* **Avoiding Over-processing**: Do not assume that EQ can fix every sound; it's impossible to turn bad sounds into good ones with EQ [33]. **Less processing** involved during the mastering stage generally yields better overall results [27, 34]. Over-processing can lead to a "fried" sound that is fatiguing to the ear [35].
* **Bypassing**: **Continuously bypass the EQ** effect to confirm its impact on the mix. A good rule of thumb is that when the effect is present, it shouldn't be overly noticeable, but its absence should be immediately perceptible when bypassed [36-38].
* **Contextual Listening**: Always **EQ with the entire song playing** to hear the changes in the context of the music. Solo EQ'ing is generally not recommended unless pinpointing a problematic frequency [11, 39].
* **Professional Recommendation**: Due to the complexity and high stakes involved, it is **strongly recommended to employ a professional mastering engineer** rather than attempting to master your own music, especially if you lack extensive experience and high-end equipment [1, 7, 30, 40, 41].

### Specific Frequency Ranges in Mastering EQ

Mastering EQ can be broadly split into three sections: bass, mid-range, and highs, with specific recommendations for each [42].

* **The First Octave – 20 Hz to 80 Hz (Bass)**:
  + Frequencies below **30-40 Hz should generally be removed** with a shelving filter to free up the low-end energy of speakers and improve bass definition [42-45]. Many club systems struggle to replicate frequencies lower than this, and their bandwidth can be reduced if they attempt to [42].
  + **Boosts below 40 Hz should be avoided** [43, 46]. Instead, focus on boosting around **50 Hz to 65 Hz** if necessary [43].
  + For punch or depth, **mid-side compression** is often preferred over EQ boosts in the 40-200 Hz range [45, 47].
* **The Mid-Range (100 Hz to 5 kHz)**: This range is where many frequency-related problems originate due to human ear sensitivity and room resonances [48-50].
  + **Muddy Sound**: Often caused by the **250 Hz to 600 Hz** range. Cuts around **180 Hz to 225 Hz or 300 Hz and 400 Hz** with a wide Q can reduce muddiness [19, 45, 48, 49].
  + **Honky or Nasal Sound**: Cuts in the **600 Hz to 2 kHz** range, particularly at **800 Hz, 1 kHz, and 2 kHz**, can address these issues [50]. For nasal qualities, a wide Q around **500 Hz** may help [19].
  + **Presence and Clarity**: Small boosts around **1.5 kHz** can increase the attack and body of instruments [50]. The **800 Hz to 5 kHz** range defines clarity, and small increases here can make instruments more defined [51]. The **3 kHz to 10 kHz** range is responsible for presence and definition, with 1-2 dB increases augmenting presence, while cutting can reduce sibilance or high-frequency distortion [52].
  + **Kick Drum "Bite"**: Transients typically reside between **1 kHz and 2.5 kHz**. Notching out frequencies just below the transient (e.g., with a high Q and gain reduction) can make the transient more noticeable [53, 54].
* **The High Range (4 kHz to 20 kHz)**:
  + **Sonic Harshness/Sibilance**: Cuts at **5 kHz** can reduce harshness [55]. For sibilance, a narrow bandwidth parametric EQ in the **5 kHz to 7 kHz** range is recommended [51, 52]. Wide EQ cuts at a central frequency of **14 kHz** may also reduce bright problematic frequencies [56].
  + **Air and Sparkle**: Small shelving boosts at **12 kHz** can increase detail and sheen [55]. Boosting the **6 kHz to 15 kHz** range adds brightness and "air" [52]. A large **6 dB boost with a wide Q at 20 kHz** can simulate the natural response of our ears, introducing harmonic distortion similar to valves (the Baxandall curve), which should then be followed by a "brick wall" EQ to remove frequencies above 20 kHz [57].

### Types of EQ for Mastering

* **Graphic EQs**: Often used in professional mastering suites because their fixed bands and constant Q (where the bandwidth remains constant regardless of gain) allow for surgical precision in contouring the tonal content of a mix accurately [17, 18, 58, 59]. Some revered graphic EQs, like the API-560, feature a proportional Q where the bandwidth narrows with increased boost or attenuation [59, 60].
* **Parametric EQs**: While common in mixing, if used in mastering, the Q is typically adjusted to **0.4 to 0.9** to create broader enhancements to the spectrum [18, 61]. They offer precise control over center frequency, gain, and Q [61].
* **Shelving Filters**: Applied at the extremes of the audio range (low or high frequencies) to either boost or cut [58, 62, 63]. They are effective for subtly changing the overall tone of program material, entire mixes, or buses in a natural way [63].

### Mid/Side EQ in Mastering

Mid/Side EQ is a powerful technique for correcting specific frequency problems and widening the stereo field during mastering [5, 64]. It allows for independent processing of the "mid" (mono) and "side" (stereo difference) components of a stereo signal [5, 6]. For example, it can be used to increase bass presence by compressing the sum (mid) or difference (side) [47].

### Interaction with Dynamics Processing

After EQ, dynamic manipulation, particularly **light compression**, is often applied to add punch and clarity to the mix [4, 36]. However, a standard compressor across the entire mix is generally avoided in mastering because the loudest parts (e.g., kick drum) can cause the entire mix to "pump" [36]. Mid-side dynamic processing can be employed to compress the sum or difference and increase bass presence [47].

### Other Related Tips

* **Noise Reduction**: Before mastering EQ, any extraneous noises (clicks, pops, hiss, hum) should ideally have been removed during recording or mixing. If obvious noise remains, it's recommended to return to the original mix to fix the source rather than relying heavily on noise reduction in mastering, as it can influence desired frequencies [65, 66].
* **Harmonic Excitement**: Some engineers may introduce a harmonic exciter after compression to **brighten the upper mid and lows**, restoring "lifeless" frequencies and adding clarity and definition [35, 67, 68]. However, this is a matter of personal preference, and overuse can lead to an artificial or fatiguing sound [35, 67].
