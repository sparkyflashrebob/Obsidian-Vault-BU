# INTRODUCTION

#### This book is intended to get you thinking about music again. Remember how excited you were when you first

#### discovered what a chord symbol really meant or how to read your first few notes? When something once mysterious and scary turns out to be easily understood after all, you start to get a sense of how powerful it can be to really comprehend how music works.

#### Musicians never stop looking for new ways to look at things. We are seekers. We read countless books on theory, technique and harmony. We search for interviews with our favorite artists and hang on every word for their secrets. We slog through books of scales and exercises with the hope that this next book will be the one that provides the missing piece of the puzzle and puts everything in perfect perspective. We listen for new sounds and techniques on recordings and on the radio hoping we can gain some new insight or inspiration for our playing.

#### We are all like a bunch of musical scientists looking for the one grand unifying theory that explains everything musical in the universe. With age and experience comes a certain wisdom and a hunch that maybe a musical theory of everything lies in its very simplicity. We think that if a simple music theory exists, it sure is well hidden in that maze of chord symbols, modes, scales and harmonic progressions.

#### The knowledge and talent we seek is already within us, but it can only be unlocked if we decide we want to use the key; and that key is simply our attention - nothing more or less. It is our curiosity and willingness to play the game. This is revealed in the famous Buddhist proverb, "When the student is ready, the master appears." This statement does not refer to separate individuals. The student and the master are one and the same. The master waits patiently within each of us for the moment we become willing to acknowledge his presence.

#### The purpose of this book is to help you understand and apply three essential elements of music: literacy, creativity, and ear training. These three areas contain everything you need to be a successful musician: reading, writing, performing, transcribing, structure, memory, technique, composition, improvisation, listening skills, chords, scales, modes, intervals etc.

#### In the real world, a musician does not learn music theory in a strictly linear fashion. Like the roots of a tree growing slowly around rocks and obstacles, it is learned in stops, starts, detours, dead ends and occasional spurts of growth and insight we like to call epiphanies. But understanding complex concepts does require grasping basic concepts first. So even though it is true that we don't actually learn theory in linear way, our growth as musicians tends to follow a more linear progression, from beginner to master.

#### This is why we so often seem to hit plateaus, peaks and valleys in our careers. A rut means there is a gap in our understanding of something and so we stall. When we fill in those gaps we quickly accelerate. By keeping an open mind and working toward being well rounded we can streamline the process of bridging those gaps one by one.

#### If your understanding of theory is limited, this book will help you and inspire you to study further; if you are advanced in music theory, my hope is that you will benefit from the creative perspectives offered here. Either way, this is not just a book on theory; it is on how you can creatively and effectively apply that theory and make great music. Remember, the key to understanding is simply the light of your attention. Given this attention, the light will surely reveal the true master within.

### ON MUSICAL GROWTH

#### From the moment we pick up an instrument we begin to dream about being an accomplished musician. Lucky for us all, there are many levels of “accomplished.” One thing is for sure, learning music never ends anyway, it "ends up" at a place where we can feel satisfied that we have achieved a certain level of success either professionally or as an amateur. Like the universe itself music is an ever expanding mystery. There are many areas of music that, no matter how far you have advanced, you will continue working on the rest of your life.

#### In the beginning, there are the facts and figures, nuts and bolts of learning the basics. This includes the core of what we call music theory, the knowledge of notes, chords, scales and intervals. These things can be learned for the most part by reading and studying about music theory. They are slowly but surely assimilated into our overall understanding of how music works. These mechanical and mathematical things become the “givens” of our music education. They are things every musician is expected to know. It is up to us to integrate these things and then do something creatively with these tools. A versatile musician continually and consistently develops and applies this knowledge by applying his or her individual creativity in the routine of daily practice, performance and teaching.

#### I hope you will consider this book to be a combination road map and checklist for achieving your goal to become a well rounded musician. At the very least, I hope it will be an inspiration towards that end. The chapters that follow

#### are very different from a conventional music theory book as they examine the practicalities of being a musician in todays technological world. Our goal here is to get under the hood and explore some of the underlying logic that fuels all of this complex theory, and discover how we can use that information creatively in our roles as students, teachers, composers and performers.

# ON THEORY

### "Writing about music is like describing a tree to someone who is standing right next to you looking at the same tree." - Anonymous.

#### You may be familiar with the phrase "theory follows practice." It is usually used sarcastically when someone is pontificating on and on about this or that explanation about why something is the way it is. When the constant questioning eventually gets annoying, someone will say, "theory follows practice", as if to say, "shut up and get busy with your work!" Sometimes the person doing the pontificating is yourself. As a beginning musician, how many times have you asked yourself questions such as, "why is this called a minor chord?" or "how can the same note have two different names?" You know that you should just accept the rules and names of things and get busy learning the piece, but you wonder, "who made up all these names and rules anyway?"

#### This pretty much illustrates the role of music theory. Theory comes after the fact. It has followed along behind the actual practice of music, like a bespectacled, lab coated scientist with a clipboard jotting down observations about how this chord is put together or why this note is "wrong" or that note is "correct".

#### The first definition of the word "theory" in The New Harvard Dictionary of Music is: "Music Theory: The abstract principle embodied in music and the sounds of which it consists."

#### It goes on to explain that this was the original, scientific concept of music theory. It refers to the more physical examination of the properties of sound and vibration. Later on, the dictionary explains that music theory became:

#### "...the teaching of the fundamentals or rudiments of music...the contemplation rather than the practice of music."

#### So it seems that yes, music theory is after all just like any other theory that tries to explain whats going on after the fact. My favorite illustration on the nature of music theory comes from something I once read (and is used at the opening of this chapter) "Writing about music is like describing a tree to someone who is standing right next to you looking at the same tree." although I think this quote was aimed at music critics, it can apply to music study in a sense as well.

#### Despite the bad rap that music theory often gets for being so much jive talk, there is "gold in them thar’ hills." Studying the ins and outs of music theory can open up whole new ways of looking at things and stimulate your creativity in exciting ways.

#### In this book, I draw a distinction between music theory and music literacy. I think they are two different things and should be treated separately. Music theory involves all the conceptual rules behind chords, scales and harmony and why things are the way they are, while music literacy refers to the ability to read and write music. They are closely related, but you can easily have one without the other. You can be extremely knowledgeable about music without actually being able to sight read or even play an instrument, and you can be an accomplished sight reader on several instruments and know very little about music theory.

#### Studying music theory is a lifelong endeavor. It is the scientific and theoretical part of music that can both inform and inspire it's art. This type of study doesn't have to be void of creativity. Creativity and imagination absolutely inform the science. This is how theories come into existence in the first place. It takes just as much creativity and imagination to come up with explanations for why something works as it does to compose a symphony, a Rock song, or a Jazz tune. The analysis can in turn inspire new forms of music. In this way, analytical theory and raw creativity can be seen as being complimentary - like two sides of a coin. It is in this new understanding that we are seeing an unprecedented coming together of the arts and sciences in the world today.

#### I highly encourage you to explore with equal passion both the art and science of music. The creative combination of these two areas can exponentially increase your overall awareness, technique, creativity, and musicianship.

# 1. INTRO TO CREATIVITY

#### Creativity is the soul of the arts. It is the word we use to describe how an artist invents something new, whether it

#### is music, poetry, prose or pictures. This newness is what we call creativity or originality. It represents the fact that we have not heard or seen anything quite like the piece of art we are speaking about. An “original” piece of music stands alone as a unique expression of the composer.

#### Yet within every style of music there is also a certain sameness that we use to classify it and assign it to a genre. We determine music to be “Jazz”, “Rock”, “Blues”, or “Classical”, and then we classify even further using sub-genres such as “Punk Rock” or “Bebop” or “Country Rock.” Musicians use these genre definitions in the same way a painter uses a frame - to put a border around the creation and define its limits. Limiting creativity in this way actually frees the artists to be more original by narrowing down the number of available choices. Too many choices can be intimidating and lead to lack of focus. Limiting your options can actually assist you in your ability to be creative.

#### Though the goal of any artist is to be creative and original, most will tell you that in arriving at that originality they have really combined different existing ideas in new ways rather than coming up with something out of thin air. Just as scientists achieve new heights by standing on the shoulders of those that came before them, artists also achieve new heights by combining traditional ideas and techniques in newly creative ways.

#### This section will discuss four main areas of creativity in music: technique, composition, performance and improvisation. It will also take a look at the important role memory plays in fostering creativity.

# 2. TECHNIQUE

### “The wise musicians are those who play what they can master.” - Duke Ellington

#### Technique is a funny thing. You can create majestic music with virtuoso technique, but, at the same time, you can create majestic music with very simple technique. I suppose this is true of all the arts. The beauty doesn’t come from the complexity, it often comes from simplicity instead. This should be heartening to those of us who lack the chops of a Chopin, a Perlman, or a Van Halen. Think about the simple ballads, many of them anonymous tunes that have been passed down through the generations. Think about how they move us despite their lack of complexity, often expressly because of their lack of complexity. There is more to simplicity than meets the ear. Anyone that believes simplicity is easy to achieve is mistaken. It can take a lifetime to master simplicity and to appreciate its true power. That said, there is value in expanding your speed, agility, and prowess on your instrument. It builds confidence and enables you to compose and perform exciting and uplifting music that can bring an audience to its feet in applause. In this chapter I offer some tips on how you might go about improving your technique in certain areas. SPEED

#### More than any other area, the desire for speed seems to captivate and motivate musicians to practice and practice hard. Everyone wants to have the ability to play fast. It is exciting and you always score points of admiration among your audience and peers when you can pump out sixteenth notes with abandon. There is a common phrase used by every music teacher on the planet that applies to speed: “slow is fast”. This means quite simply that to play a passage fast you must first learn to play it slowly and perfectly. There is much truth in this yet so many of us (myself included) have great difficulty keeping the faith. We learn a passage slowly but continually practice it too fast, making mistake after mistake in the quest for the elusive goal of nailing it at the target tempo. We are taught to use a metronome and build slowly up to the desired tempo in minute increments.

#### This all makes sense and it works very well, but I have found another related method that works as well if not better than the slow incremental trek toward the target tempo. I did not invent it, I have read about it in several publications and have heard other teachers describe it. This method uses the same traditional bottom up, gradually increase your speed scenario, but adds one simple step. That step is to periodically challenge yourself at the target tempo while you are building up slowly. In other words for every three or four slower repetitions, set your metronome (if you are using one) to the target tempo and give it your best shot. What happens in many cases is, even though you may only be one half or two thirds of the way to your desired speed, when you test yourself at the target tempo you find that you can hit it. You have crossed a certain threshold. You may have come to understand the passage in a new way and you can now suddenly play it much faster. When this happens it is a wonderful feeling and I have seen it work many times for myself. Give it a try and just use this rule of thumb: for every three times slow, try it one time fast.

#### Another issue that is often overlooked when talking about speed is the concept of phrasing. Very often a fast passage is not simply straight eighth notes or sixteenth notes but a few bursts here and there of fast playing combined with some slower more melodic playing. When this is the case it becomes extremely important to have a grasp of the articulation and overall feel of the passage. To master the articulation it helps to play it, not just slowly for a lack of speed’s sake but slowly with all of the same dynamics, mechanics, tonal effects and body movements that you would use when you play it up to tempo. This is a subtle point but attention to it is very helpful in minimizing the time you spend getting a passage up to speed. ACCURACY

#### At first it may seem that accuracy might be so closely related to speed as to really be the same thing. Many times books will discuss them together in chapters with titles such as “speed and accuracy.” You might begin to think that they go together like peanut butter and jelly. But really they are separate ideas and need to considered both separately and together. (as do peanut butter and jelly come to think of it.) It is true that when playing fast you need to be accurate but you need accuracy even more when you are playing slowly. If you think about it a fudged note when playing slowly is more obvious than when playing fast. A musician playing rapid fire notes will easily be forgiven for a slighted note or two. It is even expected by an audience. A missed note in a slow passage however is another story. It can destroy the mood of the piece and embarrass a performer.

#### But what goes into developing accuracy? There are several things you can work on to build accuracy. Most of them have to do with memory and the confidence that comes from knowing a tune inside out.

#### 1. Know the material well. The more familiar you are with the material, the less likely you will miss a note or slip off the beat. This means

#### listening to recorded versions of the song often, being able to sing the melody from memory and understanding the overall structure of the piece. Many mistakes in accuracy don’t result from forgetting your part, instead the mistakes come from being unsure about what part comes next. If the bridge takes you by surprise when it comes around, even though you have played it a million times, you are going to miss that first note. Always be conscious of where you are at during a song and be ready for the next section. It goes without saying that drugs and alcohol are an obvious enemy here. They are usually an enemy everywhere else too so it’s a real good idea to avoid them completely when practicing or performing. 2. Practice the song using different techniques.

#### This means if a song calls for the sustain pedal on the piano consider practicing it without the pedal and vice versa. If you are a guitar player and a passage is to be played legato, then practice it staccato instead of using palm muting. This gives you the confidence you need to play the passage in a variety of ways in case you need or want to vary the presentation or dynamics for some reason. There are even times when you find that you have to unexpectedly modify the dynamics of a section to suit a particular situation such as the band leader calling for a more subdued version, a faster or slower tempo or a version in a different style. 3. Practice the piece in a few different keys.

#### Jazz students are often instructed in method books and by some teachers to memorize a song in all twelve keys. This would be a nice thing to do (in theory) and it would certainly help in knowing the structure of the piece well, but it is not very practical advice in my opinion; unless you have a couple lifetimes to spend practicing. It is however a good idea to transpose a song into at least one or two more keys to get a feel for how the chords are functioning in relation to their position in the key. The act of transposing into one or two new keys will certainly help if you are suddenly requested to play in another key, even if it is one you haven’t specifically practiced it in. This is because you will have already spent some quality time considering whether a particular chord is functioning as the I, the II, the IV or the V chord for example.

# 3. COMPOSITION

#### The ability to compose music comes easy for a few, comes with difficulty for many, and comes not at all to most.

#### As with any creative act, composing a worthwhile piece of music can be the easiest thing in the world when you are “with the muse” and the hardest thing in the world when the inspiration is lacking.

#### There are as many ways to compose a tune as there are composers, so this is intended to be friendly advice and not in any way gospel on how to go about writing music. In my career I have written many songs in the Rock, Jazz and Classical genres. None have been published by major labels or publishers but they have been recorded and performed publicly by myself, my bands and the bands of my friends. What I have learned I would like to share here in the hopes that you may gain from it somehow.

#### There are so many ways to go about writing music that it can almost stop you dead in your tracks when you attempt to teach it, write about it or talk about it. For that reason, the following is not a step by step method but rather a scrapbook of things you might try and things to think about. THE SEAT OF THE PANTS METHOD

#### In this method anything goes. Pick up your instrument, start to play around with whatever comes to your mind. You might use a particular scale that you like or a certain chord or arpeggio pattern. If you play something interesting repeat it over and over. Just keep repeating it over and over again until it either starts to grow on you or you determine it’s not working. It might be a chord progression or a bit of a riff or a melody. Whatever it is keep repeating it. If you have a little music theory knowledge, try to determine what key and time signature you are in. Other than that, just wing it for a while.

#### Improvise using all you know about melody and harmony and rhythm. Try to play complete measures and with consistent rhythm. Use a drum machine or a metronome if you want. Repetition is the best way to evaluate a given phrase of music. Sometimes what doesn’t seem to be working the first few times through really starts to work with enough repetitions. If it begins to work, build on it until you have four measures, or six or eight measures. Write it down, record i,t or just make sure you somehow remember it. Move on and work on the next part. Stay in the same key you started in or move to a new key and try to take the melody or chord progression in a new direction. If you have been playing a single note melody maybe find something more chordal for the next part that compliments it. Do this until you have enough ideas to build a song with three or four parts that you can consider a verse chorus or bridge. If it is a Classical or instrumental piece these constructs will be different, but just keep working it until it seems to have enough form and structure that you can consider it a composition. The important thing is that you tell a story with the music. That will ensure that it at least has a beginning, middle and end. Remember it well. Come back to it. Nurture it. Don’t give up on it. THE MAD SCIENTIST METHOD

#### This method takes everything you know about the structure and form of music and puts it to work for you. It helps to approach this method with the attitude of a school or work assignment. Start by writing out an objective statement where you consider every aspect of the song you can think of, it might look something like this: Example from a Blues Context

#### “I will write a twelve bar Blues song with a two measure turnaround in the key of E. It will be in 4/4 time, medium tempo. It will be constructed with a riff on the I and IV chords and an arpeggio on the V chord. It will have a middle eight section that changes to the key of B minor and repeats the first verse at the end. It will have a total of three verses and four choruses. The lyrics will be about a love affair gone wrong.”

#### Theoretically now you just need to go about doing what you have stated you want to do until it is complete. You could be even more detailed if you want. Write it so that you can practically hear the song before you have written it. Give yourself a deadline, and stick to it just like you would stick to a school or work assignment. Example from a Jazz Context

#### “I will write a Jazz ballad primarily in the key of C major. It will be in 3/4 time and consist of two sixteen bar sections for a total of thirty two bars. The order of the song will be AABBA. The first section will be entirely C major. The second section will modulate to Bb major for the first 8 measures and then to G major for the second eight measures. The melody will be constructed based on chord tones primarily using thirds and ninths.” Example from a Classical Context

#### “I will write a prelude for solo Classical guitar in 2/4 time that consists entirely of eighth notes. It will be in the

#### key of A minor with occasional modulations to A major. It will contain two sections of 12 measures with each section repeating twice. I will write a coda that resolves to A major. I will construct it using the I, II, V and VI chords from the key of A minor. These chords will be Am, Bm7, Em7 and Fmaj7. I will maintain the sixteenth note pattern throughout the piece using arpeggios from these chords. Example from a Progressive Rock Context

#### “I will write a Progressive Rock song in 3/4 time consisting of a slow, majestic intro, four total verses, a chorus, and a bridge or middle eight section in a new key. There will also be a thundering outro with an abrupt ending. The three verse sections will contain eight measures each and be in the key of E minor. The chorus will shift to the relative key of G major, and the bridge will serve as a solo section for the guitarist. It will modulate to A major and shift to 4/4 time only to return to 3/4 time again upon the return of the chorus.

#### The amount of detail you include in these “objective statements” could easily be even more detailed. Be as specific as you need to be in order to inspire yourself to go and compose the music. You can see how you could easily construct an entire album of songs this way without even touching your instrument. When you do pick up your instrument you will surely have an easier time of getting started toward your goal of composing a tune. The (Somewhat) Random Chord Progression Method

#### This method is similar to the scientific method. But it combines an element of randomness with a strict structural approach. It works best for traditional songs in the Rock, Country, Pop or Jazz genre. To try this you would take a couple sheets of staff paper, divide each line into four measures each until you had 24 bars. (Any number of measures would work, but I’ll use twenty four for this example). Declare the first eight measures to be your verse, the second eight measures to be your chorus and the third eight measures to be your bridge. Put repeat markers and navigational instructions in these eight measure sections. Next choose a key for your song. Take the root chord of your chosen key and write it above the first and last measures of your song. This ensures you begin and end on the song in the same key. (You could choose a key change, but I’m trying to keep this simple for now.)

#### Next you would randomly choose chords that are diatonic to the key you have chosen and place them in the remaining measures of the song. This doesn’t have to be entirely random at this point. You may have a favorite chord progression that you would like to use. If so, stick it in and use it as part of a verse or chorus. When you have filled in all the measures you have created a harmonic progression for your song. Play through the song using a strict three of four beats to the measure rhythm pattern. You can always syncopate the rhythm later if you like. If you are a singer songwriter all that is left is to play an accompaniment on guitar and piano and come up with some lyrics and a melody to fit the chords.

#### Coming up with a melody for your song just got a whole lot easier because you already have a harmonic outline to work within. You have limited your options to within a manageable range. You can simply take notes from the existing chords, add some passing tones and build your melody based on the chord progression. Limits can be very liberating. The A Capella Method: Write Without an Instrument

#### Another approach you could take would be to try writing a song without your instrument. See if you can compose a melody with only your voice. You don’t have to be a great singer to do this. Just hum to yourself until something starts to develop. If you have lyrics that you want to put to the music, great, but it is not required. Some of the best songwriters around tell stories of how they have written songs this way. I have a student who came to me to learn guitar because she was writing this way. She had come up with complete songs including verses, chorus and bridge but she couldn’t play an instrument well enough to accompany herself. She came to her lessons with only her vocal parts to the song and I helped her to understand how to take that melody and arrange chords to go along with it. Now that she can play a little guitar she can do the arranging for herself. The advantage to this method is that all of your attention is focused on what many people feel is the most important part of any musical composition - the main melody. SUMMARY

#### Composition and songwriting is a mysterious process to most people, including composers and songwriters themselves; but just because mystery and magic so often seem to play a part doesn’t mean that composing isn’t first and foremost a craft that can be expanded, honed and continually refined like any other art form.

#### See if you can come up with some more methods like the ones described above. Everyone has different ways of doing things and its fun to experiment with different ways to write music. Who knows? You may just come up with a magical formula for composing or songwriting that puts you on the charts for a very long time. At the very least you will be challenging yourself in a way that helps you grow creatively as a musician.

# 4. IMPROVISATION

#### WHAT IS IMPROVISATION? Improvisation is the art and science of creating original music spontaneously in real time, that is to say - on the

#### stage, just jamming with friends, or during a recording session. For many, improvisation skills represent the pinnacle of creativity and expression in musicianship. For Jazz players it is absolutely essential to be able to create music this way since much of what happens in a Jazz tune is expected to be spontaneously created.

#### Improvisation is the root of composition, because when we are composing we experiment with chords, rhythm and melodies. This experimentation is the essence of improvisation. It is the place in our imagination where the most triumphant symphony or the humblest ballad begins to take shape. In this way the imagination is like the soil, and our experience and creativity are like the seeds. We start with a small fragment of rhythm or melody, nourish it with rhyme and reason and it grows into what we call music. As the music is shared it takes on a life of its own and travels where it will, inspiring new musicians and influencing experienced artists.

#### We improvise because we have something to say that can only be said with music. The meaning is there but it is pointless to try and define it. It defines us instead. For anyone who has experienced it, the thrill of making a statement with a melody while playing alone or with a band is unmatched. Everyone has a different way of going about it and that’s what makes the art of improvising so individual, expressive and interesting. THREE PERFORMANCE SITUATIONS

#### When it comes to performance there are three improvisational situations that should be approached somewhat separately. The first situation concerns improvisation when there is no predetermined structure whatsoever, the second situation is when there is a general existing or agreed upon structure and the third is when you are performing by yourself in a solo situation.

### 1. IMPROVISING WITH OTHERS WHEN THERE IS NO PREDETERMINED STRUCTURE.

#### This often happens when two or more musicians find themselves either jamming together at an informal party, open mic night, or just decide to start playing for any other reason. Usually one musician will begin playing a line or a chord progression and invite the other to follow. Assuming that there is no known chord progression, sheet music or existing melody to refer to, the other musicians will simply join in. They join while listening carefully to what the others are playing, falling in line with the rhythm and attempting to provide either accompaniment or harmonizing melodies.

#### In this situation the music is created spontaneously by all the musicians involved. Everyone improvising at the same time. There may be two, three or any number of players but each responds individually to what they are hearing and feeling. They compliment the rhythms and phrases of the others with their own ideas. Musicians take this for granted, but for non-musicians who don’t have the background to understand what is happening, this can seem nothing short of miraculous.

#### For experienced musicians these types of impromptu sessions are like entering a conversation. Just as in conversation when there is no known structure you are free to “say” anything, so you will mainly rely on your ears to figure out things like what key you are in or what the time signature is. This is akin to listening to a conversation before entering it, and determining what the subject is about. Once you understand the topic, just as you do when speaking to another person you will enter a respectful cycle of listening and responding.

#### The same rules that apply to spoken conversations apply to musical conversations. If you are constantly taking the lead and playing too loudly, the others will feel uncomfortable and the session will break down. If you allow the conversation to take place naturally where each member is allowed to have input and their phrases are complimented and referenced by the others, the session will go smoothly and great music will be made. Likewise, if another member is interested in joining the conversation, the space is created to do so. A summary of the topic may be introduced, a question may be asked, and the conversation continues.

#### It is striking how the metaphor of conversation is so appropriate to improvising music. Examine the patterns in your everyday interactions with family, friends and coworkers and observe the parallels. Observe these conversations and look for insights you can use in your music. How and why do those conversations either expand or contract, succeed or fail? The same etiquette of spoken conversation applies to musical conversations. You can learn a great deal by observing speech patterns even if you are not taking part in the discussion. Listen to interview

#### shows on TV and radio and see how the members interact. See if you can apply what you have learned the next time you find yourself improvising with other musicians.

### 2. IMPROVISING WITH OTHERS USING AN EXISTING STRUCTURE

#### A more common opportunity for improvisation occurs when you are playing within an existing, structured composition. This is the usual format for songs and structured jam sessions. In this case you have much more up-front information to work with. You may have a written chart or sheet music or you may not, but you are generally aware of the key, the chord changes and how many measures you are required or invited to solo over.

#### Just as in the above impromptu situation, the success of your performance will depend on applying the entire history of your musical knowledge and experience. This is where you have a chance to really shine as a musician. If the above example can be compared to a conversation, this example can be compared to an informal speech, where you have supporters cheering you on in the form of your band-mates.

#### Here are the main things to look at when mentally preparing for your solo. You may not have much time (if any) to do this beforehand, so this often needs to happen on the fly. Know what key you are in

#### You will want to play close attention to the overall tonality of the music for the section you are soloing in. Sometimes the song may be in one key and the solo section is in another, you don’t want to be taken by surprise because you thought the same chords were being played during the solo section as were played in the verse and chorus. This can also sometimes be true of the time signature; it may change during the solo section as well, so be on the lookout for that. Know the underlying chord structure

#### Memorize the chord progression so you can take advantage of all the harmonic possibilities enabled by it. Rather than just picking a scale to use over the entire set of changes, it pays off big to really examine the underlying chords so that you can highlight chord tones with your note choices. This can be the difference between being a good soloist or a great one. Know where your solo begins and ends

#### Coming to a party too late is just as awkward as is staying too long; so it is with soloing too. Be sure you know exactly when and where you need to come in and to get out. If you are playing accompanying chords just before the solo you will want to identify which measure you can discontinue those chords. When you finish the solo you will want to know exactly what chord you will return to when you resume your accompaniment. If you are unsure of this you run the risk of ruining your solo by coming in with the wrong chord just as the audience is getting ready to applaud you for your great solo! Listen to the other instruments

#### Being aware of what the other instruments are doing is always important but especially so during your solo. For the solo to be effective you want to feed off the energy of the other instruments. Everyone has a different idea about how to do this. I have heard some artists suggest that if the rhythm section is really cooking with fast choppy rhythms that you want to play your solo using long slow lines. Likewise, some suggest that if the music is more stretched out and slower you want to pump it up a little and play faster more exciting lines. This makes sense and you can see it at work if you listen to your favorite soloists.

#### Another example of how to make the other instruments work for you is to listen to which beats they are accenting and either explicitly join them or directly oppose them. This either creates a strong driving rhythmic pulse or a more playful, funky syncopation. Either way you are adding interest to your solo by deciding where you choose to position your instrument in the beat. You can play in front of the beat, on the beat, or behind the beat. These all create a different feel to your solo. There are endless choices, but choices can only be made if you are always aware what the other instruments are doing. All Other Musical Situations

#### The following advice is suited for all types of musical situations such as composing, interpreting or improvising. It also applies more directly to solo performers than the above which focused on group situations. Keep Things Simple

#### As beginning musicians, we all start by learning the simplest melodies with the simplest rhythms. Whatever it may be, it is simple because we are just beginners. We learn to crawl before we walk, walk before we run, cry before we laugh, and laugh before we speak. This is the nature of growth and evolution. The problems only start when we

#### reach too far and try to take the stairs before we can walk on a level floor. We can't help it. We want to accelerate learning because we all have a deep desire to achieve and improve. It's not all bad to take that tumble down the stairs; the bumps and bruises provide many valuable lessons!

#### There is something about the word "melody" that implies "simple". Maybe it is because the two words are so often combined; and maybe they are so often combined because that's what melody is supposed to be. We don't have any trouble referring to the chorus of our favorite Rock ballad as a melody, but we rarely refer to a barrage of sixteenth notes in a Heavy Metal solo as a melody. Technically speaking both of these are melodies. They are a series of notes, one after another in a rhythmic pulse of time. For a musician interested in learning how to play melodies well, the differences are only a matter of speed and technique. If we reduce the speed of the Heavy Metal solo we find it has all the same properties as "Brahms' Lullaby" or "Frere' Jacques". Tell A Story

#### Music is sound organized in time. A series of notes floating on the air with no predictability in time would not be considered music. Hang those notes on a rhythm and it begins to take shape. This is easy enough to do, so why do we still struggle? It is because the chosen notes require something more. They need to tell a story.

#### Everyone likes a good story. We are raised on stories and each of our lives unfolds in a storyline. Every minute, day, month and year of our lives creates a story. Regardless of the level of drama, excitement, or adventure in our lives, our time on this planet creates a story nonetheless. The fundamental structure of a melody: how it begins, builds, ascends, climaxes, descends, resolves and ends, are the same as in telling a story. The organizing principles are the same. Stories also begin, build, ascend, descend, climax, resolve and end. This is true whether the song is an instrumental or includes lyrics. Human nature is reflected in our art. As Aristotle pointed out so long ago, “art imitates nature”.

#### Understanding this is just as important to a musician as it it is interesting. It is done consciously or subconsciously by all good musicians and composers. If a melody is constructed without these properties listeners will instinctively not like them. Listeners don't always know why they don't like a song but will choose to listen to something else saying merely that it is not to their taste. This is why we can like or dislike so many different styles of music. The structural beauty of a well constructed melody takes precedence over any stylistic tastes or moralistic values we may have. This is because the listener is responding to the underlying structure and emotion in the music, not the content of the lyrics or the appropriateness of the message. Duke Ellington is known to have said, "There are only two kinds of music, good music and bad music." Support the Main Theme

#### When you are learning a song be sure to learn how to play the main melody of the song even if your job is not to play this melody. Everything that happens in a song should happen in a way that naturally supports the main theme. This is especially true of a solo that immediately follows a verse or chorus of the song. The soloists choice of notes and rhythms should flow seamlessly from the main melody and compliment it. It might take it somewhere else, but will always return the listener to the song in a way that prepares them for next part. Doing this consistently will make you a great player and you will have the added benefit of getting along great with your bandmates! The Span of a Breath

#### Sometimes melodies are meant only to played instrumentally and other times they are written to be sung. In both cases a good melody consists of phrases that begin and end at intervals that approximate what we find in human speech. For musicians who play wind instruments, this makes perfect sense. You both want and need the phrases to be playable using a single breath. For string instruments and pianists, the need for this is less critical from a physical standpoint but just as critical when it comes to playing and composing natural sounding phrases. I have heard several musicians suggest that a non-wind player player should take note of the length of his phrases and take care to shorten them to fall within the duration of that natural breath. As we breathe so should the music breathe. I think this is excellent advice. An awareness of the organic nature of sound and it's relation to our natural rhythms can only make us more natural sounding players. The more natural we sound, the more pleasing we are likely to be to our listeners. Relate Melody to Language

#### Another thing that works well in keeping you phrases natural sounding is to make up a sentence that mimics the rhythmic pattern of the phrase you are playing. Let's say your solo has a straight eighth note passage in it that you play as "one and two and three and four and .'" You can make up some words for example that mimic the rhythm such as "get into the syncopation". When you say that phrase, it naturally come out spoken with an eighth note pattern. This is also a great way to come up with licks as you are improvising, just make up words and phrases in

#### your head and play the syllabic equivalents out in your musical phrases. Scat singers do this with their voices all the time. It works as music because we are so accustomed to these patterns in our everyday speech. Once you start doing this you will begin hearing music even when listening to talk radio! You can take the language/music comparison quite far if you like and include things in your playing like commas, periods, question marks and exclamation points. The popular musical technique of "call and response" is a good example of using question marks in musical phrasing. This is done when a melody seems to hang in the air for a moment, unresolved. The first melodic phrase is posed like question and seems to "call out" for a response to resolve it. The following phrase dose just that and the cycle is repeated. Go Outside

#### When improvising or composing a melody it is common practice to strategically include a note or two that is outside the main key signature of the song or current chord. This is done to introduce a certain tension in order to resolve it later. For example, if you are primarily composing in C major, you may choose to play the notes G Gb F Eb C to end your phrase. The Gb and the Eb are notes outside of the C major scale but any tension or dissonance that this creates is immediately resolved when you end on the C. This particular example was commonly done in the southern Unites States at the end of the nineteenth century and resulted in the birth of a whole new musical style and culture - the Blues. There are countless ways you can “get outside” and you will want to experiment on your own with it. You will soon discover that any note is fair game when playing a melody, as long as it is intentional and done to set up the expected resolution. For that matter you may want to dash all hopes of the listeners expectations and never resolve your melody at all. If this is your intention, go for it! 3. IMPROVISING BY YOURSELF IN SOLO SITUATIONS

#### In many cases, traditional solo performers choose only to play music already composed by themselves or by others. The artistic expression comes from creatively interpreting an existing piece. Each piece is worked out and rehearsed until every note is polished to perfection. But often a solo performer will choose to include room for improvisation in their performances, believing that it provides the ultimate opportunity for musical self expression.

#### When improvising in a solo situation, that is, playing by yourself with no accompanist, it is certainly true that “anything goes.” There are no other instruments to follow, no strict tempos, no time limits on the length of the piece, no predetermined arrangements of verse or chorus, no beginning middle or end, no key or time signature, nothing. You are free to play what you wish. But what do you do with all this freedom from constraint? For many musicians this is a frightening thought and they avoid solo improvisation like the plague.

#### I once met a classical guitarist, Roland Dyens who sets aside a chunk of time in every performance for pure improvisation. Somewhere between the Bach and the Sor and the Tarrega and the Mozart, he will insert a few minutes of his own improvisations. I once attended a master class by him and he explained how everything he had ever learned goes into those performances, but as he is improvising he is aware of none of it. He said it is his favorite part of the performance because he doesn’t know what is going to happen. He explained that it is in situations like this where you need to both rely on, and forget, all your knowledge about music theory, chords, scales and time signatures and just play. This is the time where all of that knowledge comes together and becomes wisdom.

#### This performer is not alone in his observation that all the machinations of music theory tend to disappear from the mind when entering that zone of pure performance. This is a common observation by musicians of all kinds. This after all is the place where musicians long to be. It is why we practice. We practice so we can forget about how hard it was to study and sweat in order to get to the stage. When we are on the stage and can experience those awesome moments of “just playing”. We know it has all been worth it.

# 5. PERFORMANCE

### “Music, in performance, is a type of sculpture. The air in the performance is sculpted into

#### something.” - Frank Zappa

#### WHY WE PERFORM Music by nature is a performance art. Every ounce of energy you put into your music is with the hope that you

#### can someday perform for someone else. All musicians dream of the stage, the lights, the applause, and the listeners that will be moved by their performance. This is not a selfish dream. Succeeding at music benefits many more besides yourself. How many times as a listener have you been moved by an artist’s masterful performance? You know what the music did for you, you understand how it inspired and moved you, and you were grateful to those musicians who were able to make you feel that way through their music. Your performances can potentially do the same to inspire countless others and to enrich and energize their lives in the same way your life was enriched and energized. Music is a communal event and a successful musical performance is cause for celebration each and every time it occurs. PERFORMANCE AS COMMUNICATION

#### Music itself is something of a miracle. What is it anyway? Because it is pure sound some might argue that you can’t physically see it, touch it or feel it. Or can you? I would argue that you can actually physically see it, touch it and feel it. You see it when you close your eyes and let the notes flow over you evoking images and memories or perhaps inspiring hopes for the future. You physically touch it by the very act of hearing. The pressure waves of the sound reach out and vibrate the tiny bones in your inner ear. You can feel the vibrations of certain low frequencies in your chest. You emotionally feel music too, when you hear a passage or a lyric that stirs emotions inside you and begin to move in time with the rhythm. Music is like the wind, you may not be able to see it but you can see it’s effects.

#### This all means that when we perform music we are in direct communication with our audience and with our fellow musicians. Understanding performance as communication enables you to look at your performance in a way that positions the listener as an essential partner in the event we call music. Even if that someone is yourself, you will always have an audience of at least one. You will also most likely be your toughest critic so you go easy on yourself as an appreciative audience would. CREATE PERFORMANCE GOALS

#### Regardless of how much experience you have as a performer, it helps to set clear performance goals for yourself. This simply means identifying situations where you will have the opportunity to play for an audience, and laying out the steps required in order for you to show up and play. The old adage that says “ninety percent of success is just showing up” applies to musicians as well. Painters paint, writers write, fisherman fish, and musicians perform. The scale and scope of your performances are not the issue. You can perform for yourself, your friends, your family, patrons at the local bar, a stadium full of people, or for a TV audience around the world via satellite. The reason you create performance goals is so you will be ready for the audience when it arrives, however big or however small it may be.

#### For our purposes we will define two types of performance goals, micro goals and macro goals. Micro goals are narrow in focus and involve the process of learning material and building a repertoire. Macro goals are larger in scope and more related to building a performing career. Micro Goals

#### Here are sample micro performance goals that you might consider when attempting to master a song. They are arranged on a sliding scale from simple to more complex.

#### The first example will assume you are a Classical player faced with learning a new piece (from a printed score) to add to your repertoire.

#### 1. Study entire piece without instrument 2. Read through entire first section with instrument 3. Read through entire first section 4. Memorize entire first section 5. Read through entire second section with instrument

#### 6. Memorize the second section 7. Memorize the entire piece 8. Master and rehearse the entire piece

#### This example will assume you are a Jazz player attempting to build your repertoire. In this case all you have is a song chart containing a sketch of the main melody and the chord changes.

#### 1. Read through chart and play accompaniment chords 2. Read through the main melody (head) 3. Memorize main melody 4. Memorize chord changes and A/B structure 5. Identify key changes and “outside” chords 6. Rehearse soloing over tune 7. Rehearse chord accompaniment from memory 8. Rehearse main melody from memory 9. Practice soloing over changes

#### This example will assume you are a Rock player attempting to build your repertoire. In this case all you have is a recorded version of the song and you need to learn to play it by ear.

#### 1. Listen to the song for difficult spots 2. Identify key, or any special tuning requirements 3. Identify chord changes 4. Rehearse chords changes along with recording 5. Learn and memorize any required solos or riffs 6. Rehearse the song along with recording 7. Memorize entire song and song structure

#### The above example of micro goals illustrates that by breaking down the goal of learning a single piece of music into smaller components, you can identify progress along the way and take encouragement from the fact that you are always moving ahead toward your goals. Macro Goals

#### As stated earlier, macro goals are broader and wider in scope and involve setting up targets for your overall career as a musician. The specific goals will depend on the instrument you play and the career path you envision for yourself. Examples may include: booking your first solo gig, auditioning for a job with a band or ensemble, recording your first album, or scheduling a nation wide tour.

#### By setting up incremental, achievable micro and macro goals you are always moving forward toward where you want to be both in your playing and in your career. This is not to say that you won’t experience periods where you seem to be going nowhere. This happens to everyone. But even if your playing or your career seem to be stalled, you at least know where it is you are trying to go and can take the necessary steps to get there. Without having any sort of goals established you will just be floating around hoping for a lucky break; and we all know that you can’t plan for luck - good or bad. BUILDING A REPERTOIRE

#### In order to perform you’ve got to have material to perform. If you are a composer or songwriter this will largely be your own music, but if you perform material written by others you will have to decide which songs you intend to learn and master them. The collection of songs you can competently perform is known as your “repertoire” and it very much defines you as a musician.

#### Many musicians play a wide variety of styles so there is no need to limit yourself with a label too soon, or for that matter ever. If you want to envision a clear career path however you might want to take a hard look at what style of player you are or want to become and focus on that style for a while in order to maximize your understanding of it.

#### At one level, the instrument you play says something about you, at another level the genre of music says something, and at another level the type of group you play with describes you. Here are some common examples of the way musicians are often described.

#### 1. Classical guitarist 2. Rock drummer 3. Jazz bass player 4. Singer songwriter 5. Contemporary Jazz pianist 6. Orchestral percussionist 7. Tenor saxophone player 8. Traditional Jazz trumpet player

#### When deciding on a repertoire there is something liberating about limiting yourself to a narrow genre of music. When you have too many options it is more difficult to choose. For example, if you decided you were going to focus on lute music from the late renaissance period you would have far fewer songs to choose from and the decision process would be easier. It would also bring along the added benefit of providing expert status in your chosen genre. This kind of focus can be helpful in marketing your performances and in finding a target audience to sell your recordings to.

#### Many musicians choose to focus on a specific genre for a while and then move on to another one. This is a great way to explore a range of styles and periods and advance your understanding of each as you go along. The risk of course is that your audience may not know where you are coming from at any point in time. In the end it is up to you. Do you want to focus on one genre or explore many different ones? Give it some thought. Exploring new or unfamiliar types of music can vastly improve your versatility and stylistic range as a performer.

#### Once you have decided on a direction it is time to begin building a list of pieces that you want to incorporate into your repertoire. Be sure to choose music that is not too far beyond your current capabilities otherwise it could take much longer than you expect to master the list. Once your list is made, see if you can find recordings of the songs (or record yourself playing them) and build a list on your computer so you can experiment with how the songs flow when they are played one after another. You can then begin rearranging the songs into an order that represents the type of performance you want to give. Doing this not only helps you create a set list or program of your potential performance, but it gives you something you can listen to when you want to practice your visualization techniques away from your instrument. (There is much more on visualization in the chapter on memory.) SUMMARY

#### Performing is one of the most enjoyable and rewarding things we do as musicians. It’s no coincidence that listening to others perform is equally enjoyable for music fans. If you clearly define your stylistic choices and set achievable goals for yourself both in your practice sessions and in your performances, you will surely succeed in your career as a performer.

# PART II EAR TRAINING

### “The empty vessel makes the loudest sound.” - William Shakespeare

# 6. INTRO TO EAR TRAINING

#### The words and phrases we use to describe the ability to learn music by listening are clumsy: "playing by ear",

#### "ear training", "learning by ear", "ear learning"... Ugh. None of these phrases seem to work. We could really use new words to describe this concept.

#### When we say somebody "plays by ear" it seems to suggest that they cannot read music and that they are not really a true musician. But this is rarely true. Most musicians that play by ear can also read music very well. There are those who cannot read music and strictly play by ear. They can be accomplished at their art and may understand music theory better than those who do read; but they have just not learned to read or write in standard musical notation.

#### What we need is a word that defines the concept of being able to listen to music and then play it back note for note. If I told you that I was going to read a sentence and that I wanted you to remember it and repeat it back to me word for word, you would have no problem doing so. If I played or sang a musical phrase on an instrument and asked you to repeat it back to me note for note, it would be a bit more difficult, depending on your musical aptitude. It is this type of musical skill that we need new words for. We need these words to better define the concept so that we can more deliberately study it and nurture it as part of our overall education. The closest we have is "ear training" or "playing by ear." We will make do for now.

#### How many times have you sat down to learn a song from a songbook, sheet music or tablature chart only to find that it didn't match up with what you were hearing on the recorded version of the song? If you are reading this book, it has probably happened more than a few times. The fact is, even the officially published sheet music can be inaccurate, in a different key than you need to play it in, or just downright wrong. Often the sheet music doesn't even exist for the instrument you need to learn. The solution to the problem lies in developing your ability to play by ear.

#### This section will help train your ears so you can more readily learn, memorize, synthesize and, perform music with emotion, style and confidence. This is not just a beneficial goal for reasons of musical growth, but for real economic reasons as well. Sheet music is very expensive, and you can save a considerable amount of hard earned money when you learn songs by ear. Enhancing your listening and memory skills will help you immeasurably in every aspect of your musical life. You will begin to hear things in the music that you previously didn't hear. You will begin to recognize, play and remember not just your instrument's part, but the parts of other instruments as well. If you are a teacher the benefits will be even greater, because you can pass ear training skills on to your students. If you are in a band or ensemble, you will be able to help others with their parts when they have questions.

#### We have all heard the stories of prodigies who could hear a song performed and immediately play it back note for note to perfection. This ability is the ultimate goal and desire of all musicians. We are not likely achieve that here with this one book, but yes, I'll admit it, that is the goal. If you work diligently you will not only improve your ear; you will more clearly understand song structure, and increase your capacity to remember music. How skilled you ultimately become will depend on how hard you work, your level of persistence, and how high you set your personal goals.

#### In today's world the ability to play by ear is essential to becoming a well rounded musician. Even those who read music well are constantly striving to be better listeners and ear learners. This is because so many nuances of the music are not adequately captured by any written form of music. As musicians we must fill in the blanks when it comes to things like expression, dynamics and phrasing that written music attempts to describe but (because it is not the music itself) cannot adequately communicate. The information between the notes is what gives ear learners an advantage over music readers when it comes to learning new tunes.

#### In everyday practice most musicians use a combination of learning by ear, sheet music and tablature. I encourage you to also continue on with your sight reading skills. Traditional forms of music literacy combined with enhanced ear training is a powerful combination. In the end however our ears must take precedence over any printed representation of music. Ink on paper is not music. Music is pure sound. To learn to play by ear means to open yourself up to the power of truly listening. By learning how to play by ear you are also forming a solid foundation for how to be an effective composer, improviser and soloist. HOW TO USE THIS SECTION

#### This section does not require that you know how to read music. Exactly the opposite: it assumes that you do not. I highly recommend working on obtaining the ability to read and write music as a separate endeavor, but for our purposes here no music reading is necessary.

#### If you do read music, this section naturally requires that you skip looking at the sheet music, tablature or chord charts of a song and work instead to develop your listening skills.

#### As you improve your ability to play songs by ear you will come to depend less and less on any form of written materials, but sight reading is also important and should be pursued just as vigorously by all musicians. Each skill inevitably reinforces the other.

#### In another chapter we will examine the basics of song form and structure. I will concentrate on songs in order to keep things to a manageable size, but this system of ear training works for any musical form. Most musicians today concentrate on writing, studying and performing the song form. For this reason I have chosen to include information on the structural components of songs and how to "deconstruct" songs by ear. This type of reverse engineering is essential to understanding the mechanics and architecture of all music genres such as Pop, Rock, Jazz, Blues, Reggae or Classical.

#### I have spent many years learning and transcribing songs by ear, first for myself and then for hundreds of guitar and bass students. This has provided me with an enormous stash of hard earned tips, tricks and advice that I would like to share in this chapter. I use the word "tricks" cautiously here because there is no magic bullet that will allow you to learn to play by ear. It takes time, practice, patience and persistence as any musical skill requires. What I offer here are ideas, advice, encouragement and access to timeless, proven approaches for playing by ear.

# 7. GEAR

#### Committing to playing by ear will be easier if you are prepared before you begin. Having access to the following

#### list of items will be very helpful to the process.

### PLAYBACK DEVICE

#### This could be any device of your choosing: CD/DVD player, MP3 player, computer, iPad, or smartphone.I t really doesn't matter as long as the device has a highly accurate method for pausing and restarting playback immediately. You don't want to pause your song at a critical point and then have to start the entire song from the beginning again because your player malfunctioned. Another important requirement would be a balance control that enables you to listen to either the right or left stereo channel separately if you need to (for reasons explained in a later chapter).

### CHROMATIC TUNER

#### An electronic, chromatic tuner is helpful in making sure that you are in standard tuning, or any other altered tuning that your chosen song may be using. (See chapter on tuning).

### HEADPHONES

#### While not absolutely necessary, headphones allow you to completely block out distractions, especially the noise canceling models. I have found that using headphones allows me to hear individual parts much more clearly than do external speakers.

### MIXING DEVICE (OPTIONAL)

#### If you can use a device that mixes output of your instrument with the song being played all the better. This is an excellent way to work because, in addition to respecting the privacy of others around you, you can adjust the relative volume of your instrument with respect to the overall volume of the song.

#### There are standalone electronic devices that will allow you to do this, or you can use your computer to do it. I currently us a computer interface box for this purpose. It allows me to mix the output of an electric instrument such as a guitar, bass, keyboard, or microphone with the sound of the song playing on the computer. This is really the ultimate situation if you want to completely concentrate on the task of learning your song by ear, and of course you do.

#### A standalone mixing device will allow you to plug in your instrument as well as a variety of input devices such as CD/DVD players, MP3 players, tape decks, etc. Your headphones plug in as well and there are separate volume controls for all of the inputs so you can balance the relative volumes. This is the same setup as using the computer interface box, except you don't need a computer.

### "SLOW DOWNER" DEVICE (OPTIONAL)

#### If you are working on extremely fast passages, these electronic units come in pretty handy. They are available as standalone devices, or as computer software programs. They enable you to slow down the tempo of a fast piece of music without effecting the pitch of the music.

### STAFF/TABLATURE PAPER (OPTIONAL)

#### If you don't want to rely on memory only, want to work on your notation skills, or are transcribing for a friend or student, you will need some staff/tab paper. You could also use a computer based notation program. I prefer paper only because I find it much easier for my hands to go back and forth from pencil to instrument than computer keyboard to instrument. The downside is that after transcribing to paper, you will need to input everything again, or electronically scan your work if you want a digital copy. Do whatever is most comfortable for you, or best suits your specific needs. Another way to approach this is to rely on your memory during your listening sessions, and then, at a later time, attempt to write down your music purely from memory.

### TIME

#### Be sure you set aside a block of time for your transcribing sessions. You want to begin your work knowing that you can relax into the process and not feel rushed. Be sure also to choose a time of day when you know you will be the most focused. This will be different for everybody. Only you know when your mind is the most alert and your ears are the most tuned for listening to music.

#### Try to avoid letting all the technology available get in the way of your real task. The last thing you want is to

#### waste your precious time messing around with cables, knobs and computer software when you should be working on the music. To maximize your sessions be sure to have a dedicated place where you can walk in, sit down and get to work before you lose your inspiration. I have more than once been all excited about working on a song and when I got to my studio, began tweaking and reconfiguring things. By the time I got everything set up I had either forgotten what I set out to do, or was so worn out and frustrated from configuring equipment that I had lost interest in the music!

### COMFORTABLE STUDIO SPACE

#### Set up in a place where you feel comfortable and can concentrate fully. You are more likely to spend more time on your project if you feel good in your surroundings.

# 8. TUNING

#### Properly tuning your instrument is the most fundamental requirement of any musician. You can be the hippest,

#### fastest, fanciest player on the planet, but if you are out of tune you will sound like the absolute worst musician on the planet. The reasons for this are obvious, music is not pleasing if the instruments are not in proper harmony with each other. Enough said. If you are reading this book, I know you appreciate this simple fact.

#### The thing about tuning that does need to be discussed here however is how to make sure you are in tune with the recording you are trying to learn. It is vital that you are in tune with the instruments on the recording. If you are not, you will never be able to find the actual notes being played, because they literally won't be on your instrument. STEP ONE: TUNE YOUR INSTRUMENT TO "STANDARD TUNING"

#### Standard tuning means to tune your instrument to "A 440", and that term simply means that the note "A" (the one below the piano's middle C) is tuned to vibrate at 440 Hertz or "cycles per second". This means for example, on a guitar, the open A string will vibrate back and forth exactly 440 times every second. By adjusting the tension of the string with the tuning pegs we can control the rate at which it vibrates.

#### There are many ways to tune to standard tuning. Currently the best way for string instruments is to use an electronic tuning device. If you have a good ear you may want to tune using a tuning fork or pitch pipe (if you don't mind being "old school"). If you are an acoustic piano player it's not so easy - just make sure you have your piano tuned by a professional once every few months. (I know the piano is technically a stringed instrument too, but the strings inside are not as easily accessible and tuneable as are the strings of a violin or guitar.)

#### Once your instrument is in standard tuning you are all set to go. Your instrument should now be in perfect tune with all of the instruments on the recording you want to learn, if your lucky...

#### "Lucky!" you say? "What's luck got to do with it? I thought you just said that as long as I was in standard tuning, I was good to go." Alas. In most cases this is true. However…

#### There are many reasons why the song you have chosen to learn by ear is not in this “standard” tuning. If this is the case, you will have to tune to the instruments on the recording. So, you may or may not be able to use a standard tuning device. "Ugh", you say. I feel your pain. Once again, hang in there! Reasons Why Standard Tuning Doesn't Always Jive With a Recording

#### The following points are critical to anyone trying to learn a song from an existing recording. I felt it important to be pretty detailed here mainly because in my early attempts to learn songs by ear I ran into these problems time after time and had to overcome them one by one. Hopefully, this will save you from the same frustrations. 1. The musicians may not have been in standard tuning when they recorded the song.

#### This is the case quite often on older recordings. Sometimes the musicians all tuned to the piano that was in the studio at the time. If the piano in the studio was not tuned properly to A440, then nobody else will be either. If there was no piano in the song, the musicians may have just tuned to the lead guitar player who had a few too many that day and didn't remember (or bother) to tune to standard pitch. 2. The speed of the original recording has been altered, effecting the pitch.

#### Sometimes, before digital technology was widespread, a record producer would decide that the tempo of the recorded song was either a bit too fast or too slow. Rather than make everyone record the song all over again, they would simply speed up or slow down the tape speed. Unfortunately when this was done on analog equipment, as all the older tape players were, the pitch of the resulting music is altered. It becomes a bit sharper (higher in pitch) if the tape is sped up, or a bit flatter (lower in pitch) if the tape has been slowed down. If you want to go really far back before audiotape when songs were recorded direct to vinyl disks, then it may have been that the motor on the turntable used to master the LP's was running either too fast or too slow. 3. The song was recorded off of a turntable that was spinning to slow or fast, again effecting the pitch. 4. The song was recorded off a radio or TV broadcast that was sped up (the pitch thing again) in order to fit within a predetermined time slot.

#### It happened all the time back in the day. Money takes precedence over standard tuning. Imagine that. 5. One or more of the musicians is using an "altered tuning."

#### This case is slightly different from those in the previous examples. In this example, the song may still be tuned to A 440, and you would still tune to standard pitch, it's just that the individual strings of the instrument (usually a

#### guitar) are not tuned normally. Usually, a guitar is tuned E, A, D, G, B, E, from low string to high string. There are endless variations on this, and it has become quite popular especially with Heavy Metal bands to lower the pitch of some or all the strings. These are called "drop tunings" because almost always, the strings are lowered in pitch by 1/2 step, a whole step, two whole steps, or more.

#### Whew! And I even left out a few other ways in which a recorded song may not be in so called standard tuning. The bottom line to all this confusion is that if your chosen song is indeed not in standard tuning you must tune your instrument to the song you wish to learn. (Learning to read music and dropping some bucks for sheet music is starting to sound pretty good right about now isn't it? )

#### The reasons are interesting, but in the end it doesn't matter too much why a song is not in standard tuning. For the purposes of this tutorial, if you find that you cannot tune your instrument to the recording, it might be wise to choose a different song.

# 9. CHOOSING A SONG

#### The first step in learning to play by ear is of course to choose a song to transcribe and memorize. I use the word

#### "transcribe" here loosely, meaning to decode the notes from the recorded version of the song and record them in your memory. You can of course use using standard music notation, tablature, or chord charts to do this (which is the traditional sense of the word "transcribe" ) but for the purposes of this chapter, I encourage you to use your memory as your recording device. If you are one who visualizes music as notes on paper, simply consider your mind to be your staff paper.

#### For your first song, choose one that you think is within your technical ability to perform. It's okay if it is slightly beyond your present skill level, but not drastically. You'll never really know until you try anyway. It should at least be something you would enjoy playing and that you feel provides a realistic challenge to your ears. If you are just starting out this may be hard to determine, because until you have some experience with playing by ear you won't know if something is difficult or easy. I have transcribed many songs that sounded impossible to play, that have turned out to be extremely easy, and I have transcribed many songs that have sounded easy to play that have turned out to be surprisingly difficult. You won't really know until you dig in, so if your first choice gives you too much trouble, simply pick something else. This is meant to be hard work, but it should be fun too.

#### Once you have chosen a song, the next step is to listen to it, really listen to it.

# 10. LISTENING

### “Listening is the key to everything good in music.” - Pat Metheny

#### Since we are about to learn a song by ear, careful listening is the primary tool in our toolbox. Any amount of time you spend simply listening in the beginning will pay off in the long run by significantly reducing the time you spend on any given song. Even if you have heard the song a million times already, you will want to listen several times more before you attempt to learn it on your instrument.

#### Below I describe how you can segment successive listens to achieve maximum learning efficiency. The best approach is to listen in a comfortable chair using headphones, and without your instrument nearby. You want to resist the impulse, however strong it may be, to jump up and start noodling during this all important listening phase.

#### As long as you are listening closely, you can always do these steps in a different order or with different things in mind if you wish. These are only suggestions. They are methods I personally have found successful. I urge you to give them a try. FIRST LISTEN: GETTING AN OVERALL IMPRESSION

#### The first time you listen to a song after deciding to learn it, don't try and accomplish too much. Just let the music play and listen with fresh ears. Chances are you have already heard it many times before; but now you have a different motivation. In the first listen, try to get an overall impression of the song itself. You want to identify with the composer or performers emotions. Is the song lighthearted, playful, angry, sad, melancholy, depressing, uplifting or hopeful? What is the song generally about? What do you like most about the song? Just let your mind absorb the music in a very general way. SECOND LISTEN: DETERMINING TEMPO AND RHYTHM

#### During the second listen we will focus on the tempo and rhythm of the song. Is it fast or slow or of medium tempo? Try to tap out the rhythm. Is it three beats to the measure or four, or six? Does it change somewhere during the song? Does it return to the original time signature? If you don't know what any of this means, don't worry, the main thing is just to be able to tap along with the song for now. (See chapter on Determining Time Signatures.)

#### Do this for the entire song, just letting the rhythm sink in and listening to what the drummer is doing. If there is no drummer you will still feel the pulse of the music. It will just be more subtle. You are listening for accents or strong beats and weak beats. They will occur at regular intervals. The distance between these intervals are called measures. This pulse is what we want to connect with during this listen. If you can determine the time signature, do so. If you aren't sure or don't know what a time signature is, don't worry. There is a separate chapter in the book on this. For now it is enough just to be able to feel the rhythm and tap along. THIRD LISTEN: HEARING THE MELODY

#### When you listen for the melody in your song, begin by identifying which instrument is playing the main line, that is, which instrument plays the theme that defines the overall tune. If it is a vocal song this will almost always be the vocal part. If it is an instrumental song then it could be one of many instruments. Consider how your instrument interacts or relates to that melody. Often times, the vocalist will sing a phrase and the guitar or piano will either echo that phrase or play a bit of a line that continues or compliments that phrase. Other times an instrument will harmonize with the vocal and play the exact same phrase at the same time.

#### It is commonly understood that in vocal songs the sole purpose of each instrument is to simply support and highlight the vocal. Likewise, during an instrumental passage, whichever instrument is taking the lead role should be supported by the other players and not interfered with or "stepped on".

#### Listen carefully for how, not just your instrument, but the other instruments compliment this main melody, whether it be the vocal line or the lead instrument. Hearing this will be essential later on as you learn your part and position it with the proper dynamics into the song. FOURTH LISTEN: SOLOS

#### Next focus on any solos that may be required of your instrument. My guess is that you are choosing a song that somehow features your instrument, but this is not always the case. Listen closely for the overall shape the melody or solo takes. Does it rise and fall gradually or rapidly? Does it steadily ascend or descend? Does it use syncopation heavily or just in spots? Are the phrases short or long? Where might you define the starting and stopping of these phrases? Listen over and over again to the solo. More than any other part, the solo is the part that you really want to

#### have in your head. You want to be able to sing it to yourself with or without the song playing and really get to know it's melody inside and out. I often tell my students that if they really want to copy a solo, they must make it a part of them. The way to do this is by frequent, careful and attentive listening to every aspect of it. FIFTH LISTEN: CONSIDERING THE LYRICS, MEANING, AND MESSAGE

#### While it's not essential that you understand the subject matter of a song in order to play it well, there is good reason to be aware of the song's overall meaning. If you can connect with the sensibility of the composer or songwriter, you are more likely to have a deeper subconscious understanding of where the song is coming from. When you perform the piece you will subtlety be adjusting your performance of the song (without really being aware of it) to be more in line with the overall intention of the songwriter.

#### Listen to the chosen song for the lyrical content. Don't necessarily attempt to find the deeper meaning at first, just knowing what the words actually are is a great first step. If you can't make them out, you can usually find the lyrics on the Internet. This is often overlooked by musicians in a band who aren't active singers. I know some musicians who don't sing and who couldn't even tell you one word of one song that their band performs. They just don't see it as important. I don't want to overstate the importance of knowing a songs subject matter and lyrics, but it really shouldn't be underestimated. You want to get your mind around a song any way you can. The deeper you relate to it as an artistic expression at all levels, the more meaningful your performance of it will be. This understanding will also deepen the experience for your listeners. SIXTH LISTEN: HEARING YOUR PART

#### By now you may be thinking, "We are on our fifth listen and only now are we listening for what our instrument is playing?" Keep in mind that this is a book, so there is a certain amount of linearity that must be followed in writing these steps down. You may do these in any order you like. I list this last because it will be the most involved. This is where you really zero in on the part that you intend to learn by ear. It might be a good idea to keep a notebook with you for this listen and jot down anything that you may notice about the part that you may want to recall later.

#### When listening for your part it's helpful to determine what role the part is playing in the song. Is it the main or featured instrument that takes center stage, or does it play more of a supporting role such as an accompaniment? Do you intend to learn the piece note for note exactly or do you just want to capture the essence of the part? (For now, the assumption is that we are attempting a note for note transcription). Does the instrument follow closely along with another part? For example if it is a rhythm guitar part, is it following right along with the rhythmic patterns of the bass player? Many times the part will serve several purposes. Perhaps your part plays an accompanying role through most of the tune, but takes a solo later on. Maybe your part is featured in the intro but is playing strictly rhythm parts for the remainder of the piece. Other times, your part may be playing loudly during the chorus but is subdued or nonexistent in the verses.

#### Knowing the role of your instrument at various points in the overall song is critical for several reasons. Your role will determines things like dynamics: how loudly or softly you play in relation to the overall mix, harmony: are you playing a line that strictly harmonizes with another instrument? In the case of harmony you will want to be careful to figure out what octave or register your part is playing in relation to your bandmates part. For example if the pianist is playing a part an octave lower than your guitar part, it can sometimes be difficult to tell which notes he is hitting and which notes you should be hitting. This is even trickier if there are two of the same instruments playing close harmonies. Isolate Your Part in the Mix

#### One method that is quite helpful in isolating your part so you can hear it more clearly is to use the balance control on your playback device. Often, two parts are recorded on separate audio channels in a stereo mix. Sometimes it is possible to listen only to the left or the right channels and completely remove one or the other instruments. Try shifting the balance control entirely to the left or right channel to see if this is the case. If the two parts were recorded in this fashion, you will now hear only the part you are trying to learn. This is extremely helpful in cases where two instruments are playing closely related lines.

#### If this is not the case, and your instrument cannot be isolated in the mix, you will have to pay a little more attention to the particular colors or timbre of your instrument. Is your chosen part using an electronic effect that you can use to differentiate it from other parts such as overdrive, distortion, reverb, flanger, or chorusing? Identify Techniques

#### Once you have zeroed in on your part, you may want to listen several times through just hearing what your chosen instrument is doing. Try to listen for any special techniques being used. Is the player using any approaches

#### that you are unfamiliar with or less than accomplished at? Will learning this song require you to work on any new or unfamiliar techniques? Is the tempo going to be easy or difficult for you? Do there seem to be large interval leaps? Are the chords being played pretty standard or are there some crazy exotic sounding chords that you may be unfamiliar with?

#### Specific techniques and effects you listen for will sometimes depend largely on the instrument you play. For example, a guitarist may identify passages with difficult tapped parts, or parts played with a bottleneck slide, while a pianist might identify large intervals or crossover fingering patterns. Similarly, a saxophone player might hone in on long phrases that would require a large amount of air in order to execute properly. Identify Electronic Effects

#### Electronic effects in modern music can often be just as important as the notes that are being played. Does your part rely on a certain type of effect to execute its role in the song or is that effect just a nice touch? For example, a Jimi Hendrix song might clearly require the use of a wah pedal for the guitar in order to get it's overall point or mood across. Another song however might use a wah pedal, but merely as a coloration and its use doesn't either make or break the part. A keyboard part might utilize the tremolo sounds of a "Leslie" cabinet but is that sound truly required for the song to work? In most cases the song can be learned and performed without the effect, but it helps to be aware and consider whether you either have the desired effect, don't have it and are willing to buy it, or don't have it and don't need/want to buy it at all. Listening Summary

#### After assessing these various aspects of your part: tempo, rhythm, meaning, role in the song, subject matter, techniques and effects, reassess whether this is the song you really want to put your energy into. You may determine that the song is too easy or too hard, or that the subject matter is offensive or not in line with your philosophy on life. You might also determine that you can't afford effects used, or that there are certain techniques you are not currently capable of. You can then decide to either choose another song, or make it a point to acquire the skills, speed, or equipment necessary to execute the song.

#### Remember, our goal here is primarily to develop the ability to learn a part by ear, not to instantly acquire virtuoso skills. Learning to play as well as Eddie Van Halen, Frederick Chopin or Herbie Hancock, is an admirable goal, so if that is your intention go for it! Just be sure not to let the challenge of playing your chosen part overshadow the fact that what you are really working on is training your ears, not just your fingers. You are learning to listen more deeply, and not just trying to develop advanced techniques or acquire more speed.

#### It may be the case that you have to learn the song, despite existing challenges, because your band is planning to do it, or you have been assigned it by your teacher. In either case congratulations! You are now ready to do the work of learning a song by ear.

# 11. FINDING THE CHORD PROGRESSION

#### One of the most helpful things you can do when preparing to learn a song by ear is to first determine the "root

#### progression" of the song. This means finding the bass notes that define the movement of the chords throughout the song. At this point you are not looking for the chords themselves, just the lowest note of the chord that we call the root. After finding the roots, finding the actual chords is quite easy. If you are a singer who just wants to accompany yourself with the chords to the song, this section may be all you need to learn any song you desire, just by listening. FINDING THE ROOTS

#### Start by listening for the very first note you hear in the bass (or the lowest pitched note in the song). As soon as you hear the first bass note, pause the song. Try to find that note on your instrument. Keep rewinding and listening just to that very first bass note until you have discovered exactly what that note is. Once you have matched it commit it to memory. Even if the bass is playing other notes in the measure, you will simply play and let that note ring for the duration of the measure. You are looking for the bass note that falls on beat one of the measure. This is important because the bass may be walking all over the place. All we care about is where it is on beat one. This note may repeat for one measure, or for several measures. Repeat this process for each measure until you have determined a bass note for every measure in the song. These will become our root notes. Remember these or write them down.

#### What you have now is called the root progression. It is a kind of outline for the song. It does not yet concern itself with qualities such as major or minor or seventh chords. It may sound like a tedious process but it it not as difficult as it seems; you will notice repeating patterns for sections of the song like verse, pre chorus and chorus. If you don't know the names of the notes yet, you can simply refer to these notes in any way you know how. For example a guitar or violin player might choose to use tablature. If your memory is good, you won't need to write this progression down but if you did it would look something like this:

#### :G, E, C, D: :A, E, A, E, C, D, G, G: In the above example the letters represent notes, the commas represent measures and the colons are repeat

#### markers that indicate the pattern should be repeated. You should now be able to play through the entire song using these bass notes and get a feel for how the song progresses. IDENTIFYING THE CHORD PROGRESSION

#### Next we will convert the root progression to a "chord progression." The chord progression is what defines the overall harmonic options for the song. In other words, it determines what notes will be played by the solo instruments or singers at any point during the song. To do this we simply take the root progression, which tells us the root note of the chords, and one by one we determine what the underlying chord type is.

#### We will start by assuming that every root note defines a major chord. Then we will simply test this theory for each instance of the chord and change the type of chord if it is not a major chord.

#### Using our example from above, you would playback the song on your machine and play a G major chord over the first measure. Listen closely to see if this chord sounds right. Let's say that it does sound good. You will then decide that the first chord of the song is G major.

#### The next root in our song is an E, so you will try to play an E major chord over that measure. In this case the E major chord sounds wrong. You know the root is an E however so all you need to do is try various chord types using E as the root. Here are some of the more common types to try:

#### E major (E) E minor (Em) E seventh (E7) E minor seventh (Em7) E major seventh (EM7)

#### When you try each of these chords over the measure with E as the root, you will find one that sounds the best. Lets say that you conclude that Em sound best. This is the one you will use. Don't worry for now if you may have made a mistake. There is always time to change your mind if you decide later that there is a better choice.

#### At this point, our chord progression is beginning to take shape, we now have determined that the first two chords

#### of the song are G and E minor. Simply repeat this process for each root note, and you will have a complete chord progression for the song!

#### If you choose to write this progression down, the final chord progression might look like this: Verse: G, Em, C, D7 Chorus: Am, Em, Am, Em, C, D, G, G: If you are a singer and just want to accompany yourself on guitar or piano, you are pretty much done at this

#### point. You can simply adhere to the time signature and strum or play the chords while you sing the melody. Congratulations, you have just learned how to play a song by ear.

#### In the interest of keeping things from getting too complex, this example is a simple verse/chorus song. The fact is that many songs are more complex than this, and many are even less complex. It is amazing to me how many hit songs actually take a two or three chord progression and simply repeat them over and over again, changing only the melody and dynamics from verse to chorus.

#### It is not enough to simply read how to go about identifying the chords to a song. The real learning comes when you sit down and do the actual work of listening and comparing the notes you hear with the notes being played on the recording. For people that become good at this, it is not work at all. It is all about discovery and the sense of accomplishment that comes from learning how to play music by ear.

# 12. DETERMINING TIME SIGNATURES

#### I can remember the first time I became aware of the concept of time signatures. The funny thing is, I had already

#### been playing guitar for several months before I even knew what a measure or a time signature even was! Being self taught at first, I just played along not caring or even knowing what the time signature was. I just felt the rhythm as it chugged along. When I was introduced to the fact that all music has "accents" and these accents fall on predetermined beats (usually but not always on every 2, 3, 4 or 6 beats) I was fascinated. From then on I started trying to figure out the time signature to every song I heard. WHAT IS A TIME SIGNATURE?

#### The simplest way to think about time signatures is that they are measuring sticks that mark where the various measures begin and end. This won't mean much if you don't know what a measure refers to yet but hang in there. The first step is to simply tap along to the beat of any song. Bobbing your head works too, or tapping your foot. Do whatever feels natural for you. Next, try to find the strongest beat; this will be the one that seems to have the most oomph. The relative strength of this beat is called the accent. It is usually the first beat of the measure. Assume this is the first beat and start counting aloud upon that beat "1, 2, 3, 4" keep going until you hear another accented or strong beat. That next strong beat will be the start of the next measure.

#### If you counted to 4 before you heard the next strong beat, then you can say your song is in "4/4" time. The first "4" stands for the number of beats in the measure and the next "4" stands for "quarter notes" (the type of note that we are assigning one beat to). When someone says a song is "in 4/4" (pronounced "four four") they mean there are 4 beats in a measure, and that a quarter note gets one beat. Likewise, when someone says a song is "in 3/4" (pronounced "three four") they mean there are 3 beats in a measure, and that a quarter note gets one beat. One more example, when someone says a song is "in 6/8" (pronounced "six eight") they mean there are 6 beats in a measure, and an eighth note gets one beat.

#### The terminology can be tricky if you don't have any background in basic music theory, and it is a difficult thing to describe in a print format, so don't let it bog you down if you don't understand just yet. The important thing is that you can feel the accented beats, anticipate where they fall, and be able to determine how many beats fall between those strong beats. EXAMPLES OF TIME SIGNATURES

#### It might be helpful at this point to cite some popular examples. The Chuck Berry song Johnny B. Goode is in 4/4 time. Amazing Grace is in 3/4 time. The Elvis Presley Song Love Me Tender is in 4/4 time. The Fats Domino song Blueberry Hill is in 6/8 time. Beethoven's Ode To Joy is in 4/4 time.

#### The above time signatures are very common examples. In fact 4/4 time is used so much that it is often referred to as "common time". Here are a couple time signatures that are much less common.

#### The Pink Floyd song "Money" is in 7/4 time. (7 beats per measure, a quarter note gets one beat.) this song also has a section in the middle (the guitar solo) that moves into 4/4 time before returning to 7/4 for the end of the song.

#### The Jethro Tull Song, "Living In The Past" is in 5/4 time (5 beats to the measure, a quarter note gets one beat). I recently read that this was the first 5/4 song to ever reach the Billboard Magazine "Top 40" charts. PRACTICING TIME SIGNATURES

#### Here is a simple way to get a feel for some of these time signatures. Simply read the following sentences aloud, the numbers in all caps are the accented or strong beats. They determine how many beats per measure there are.

#### 4/4 Time: Say: "ONE - two - three - four - ONE - two - three - four - ONE - two - three- four " 3/4 Time: Say: "ONE - two - three - ONE - two - three - ONE - two - three- " 2/4 Time: Say: "ONE - two - ONE - two - ONE - two - ONE - two-" 5/4 Time: Say: "ONE - two - three - four- five ONE - two - three - four - five -" By simply reading the above statements aloud, shouting the all-caps numbers, and saying the lowercase numbers

#### in a normal voice, you can get a really good idea what these time signatures sound like in a musical context.

#### The next step is to practice figuring out various time signatures for yourself. Do it for the songs you choose to learn of course, but also try it whenever you have a moment to concentrate on a piece of music. This might be when you are driving and listening to the radio, when you are at a concert, or maybe while you are in the supermarket just hearing music over the intercom. With a little practice this will become quite easy, and it's kind of fun too.

# 13. DETERMINING KEY SIGNATURES

#### Identifying what key a song is in can be tricky at first but, after reading this chapter you will see it is actually

#### very easy. The first thing we have to tackle is a general understanding of what exactly a "key" is. WHAT IS A "KEY SIGNATURE"

#### When we say a song is in the key of C or the key of B minor, or in any of the other twelve keys, exactly what does that mean? Furthermore, how can we tell what key a song is in if we don't have the sheet music to display the key signature? Why does it even matter what key a song is in?

#### Believe it or not, key signatures came about originally to solve what was mostly a printing problem. At first, notes were simply written down for what they were. For example a printed score of song in the key of E (which has four sharp notes) would be littered with sharp signs. Every time the note F, G, C, or D occurred in the piece, the composer would have to place a sharp symbol next to the note. You could imagine what that page would look like! To alleviate this problem (and to save some ink) composers began placing a "key signature" at the beginning of the score.

#### The key signature became like a legend of a map that indicated which notes were to be sharped or flatted by default. By printing the four sharp symbols at the very beginning of the piece, the composer indicated that each time those notes were called for in the score, they should be sharped or flatted. This cleaned up the page pretty well and the practice became standard procedure. If an unaltered note was called for in the score, the composer simply placed a "natural" symbol in the score to indicate that this or that instance of the note was an exception, and not intended to be sharped or flatted. So they key signature's job is to tell the player that, for the most part, the notes used in the song come from a particular scale.

#### If a key signature tells a musician what scale the majority of notes have been selected from. By extension, this means that the song's melodies will usually resolve to the root of that scale. This is why it is useful to think of keys as identifying the "tonal center" of a composition.

#### When we say a song is in a certain key (we'll use the key of C major as an example) we are basically saying two things about the song.

#### 1. The notes used to compose the song are mainly taken from the C major scale 2. The chords and harmonies used to compose the song eventually "resolve" to a C major chord.

#### Now, for this knowledge to be of any use, you would need to already know that the notes of the C major scale are C D E F G A B C. There are no sharp or flat notes in this key, so if our song has no sharp of flat notes you can conclude that it is in the key of C, or it's relative scale A minor. (Relative keys are discussed in the chapter on modes.)

#### Of the three things in the above list the second statement is the most important. This is because a song in the key of C major will very often contain notes that are not in the key of C. This happens all the time and when it does, these oddball notes are called "accidentals". (It's ironic that these notes are called "accidentals" because the are chosen by the composer very much on purpose.) Most composers know what key they are writing in, so when they choose to include a note or a chord that is not in that key it is done to achieve a certain effect. The effect is to go outside the key deliberately in order to introduce an element of tension in the song. This leaving of the key, however briefly it occurs it is called "modulation". The modulation is introduced by the composer with the sole purpose of resolving it later. This musical resolution not only provides the song with that magic that happens when tensions are resolved, it also provides an unmistakable clue as to what the key of the song is. FIGURE 2. THE CYCLE OF FIFTHS

#### This chart below is printed in every music theory book ever written. This book is no exception. It shows you how to determine the key signatures for all twelve keys. Going clockwise around the chart is progressing in fifth's, and going counterclockwise you are progressing in fourth's. If you are looking at a key signature on a piece of sheet music you can determine the key by the number of sharps or flats. If you are unsure of the key you can use the cycle of fifths to figure it out. Simply start at C and count from zero going clockwise for sharps and counterclockwise for flats.

#### Clockwise (5th's) Key of C: 0 sharps or flats Key of G: 1 sharp Key of D: 2 sharps Key of A: 3 sharps Key of E: 4 sharps Key of B: 5 sharps

#### Counterclockwise (4th's) Key of C: 0 sharps or flats Key of F: 1 flat Key of Bb: 2 flats Key of Eb: 3 flats Key of Ab: 4 flats Key of Db: 5 flats Key of Gb: 1 flats

#### There are several reasons to care about the key signature in a piece of music. 1. It makes sight reading easier by limiting the number of printed sharps and flats. 2. It provides clues for how to structure improvisations and resolve melodic phrases. 3. It provides clues to what chords can be used in accompaniment. 4. It suggests potential harmonies.

#### In summary, the key signature provides an overview of the tonality of the composition. It provides some insight as to how the composer has generally constructed things. If you know what key a song is, you can make informed choices about what notes, chords and harmonies should be used to greatest effect during improvisation and soloing.

# 14. LEARNING RIFFS AND SOLOS

#### Everything that you have learned up to this point will now help you tackle learning riffs and solos by ear. This is

#### a very satisfying part of learning by ear because often times when we hear a cool riff on the radio we don't necessarily want to learn the whole song, we just really love those opening power chords or single note melody. Other times you may want to learn a solo or two by your favorite musician as a way to become more familiar with his or her style or to learn some of their techniques and scale choices. Over the years, a soloist will collect ideas and incorporate them into their own licks for use in compositions and improvisations. This is how music evolves; it is how one musician influences another. Great music awakens us and challenges us to pick up our instruments and try to play those sounds that have so inspired us. RIFFS AND SOLOS

#### Riffs are a staple of Rock music, but they are employed in all types of music. They often consist of a repeating four to eight measure melody in a lower register and played with a driving rhythm. For some Rock examples, think "Smoke On The Water", "Day Tripper" or "Crazy Train." They are usually not too difficult and are a great way to inspire confidence and work on your rhythm and single note line chops.

#### The ability to play a simple melody one note at a time is often overlooked by self taught musicians who are often initially focused on accompaniment. This is why so many people will say "I don't play lead or anything" when they tell you they are guitar players. This is baffling to most other musicians like, trumpet or violin players who often play nothing but single note lines. The Progressive Addition Method

#### The method I call "progressive addition" works great when learning riffs and solos. In this method, you listen for first note of the riff and stop the recording. Find the note on your instrument. Keep listening to the note, just that one note, over and over until you have found it. Then play the first two notes and stop. Repeat the process. Then repeat the first two notes over and over until they are played perfectly. Next, the first three notes are played over and over and repeated until they are perfectly executed. This continues for the entirety of the riff. This method works like magic because by the time you get to the last note of the riff or solo you have played all of the other notes a hundred times perfectly.

#### Once you have learned a portion of the riff or solo, it is time to work on what is called "phrasing". Phrasing simply means to place a group of notes into context by playing it with the proper rhythm and dynamics (relative loudness and softness). Even the shortest solos are made up of several phrases linked together to form the larger idea. Good musicians organize their solos (consciously or not) into a series of ideas or "thoughts". In this way, a solo is constructed very much like a paragraph is written: a paragraph is a collection of sentences, made up of words, which are made up of syllables, which are made up of letters. To learn a riff or a solo by ear then we simply break these ideas down into manageable chunks and practice them separately, then together. After you have done this using progressive addition, it is up to you to decide what size these chunks are. Ultimately the chunks should represent phrases or single musical statements. They need to be small enough that they are easily remembered, but not so small that you lose your place in the context of the song. Practice each phrase separately and then together with the next and previous phrases in various combinations until you have mastered the entire riff or solo. CONCLUSION TO EAR TRAINING

#### You will notice that most of this section was devoted to the preparation of learning a song by ear and to the subject of song structure. This is because so much of learning a song by ear depends on being in tune with the recording, having the proper gear, choosing an appropriate song, understanding the time signature, finding the chord progression, knowing the structure, and really listening closely to the song. If you have done those things, the actual learning and playing of the song can be relatively easy.

#### In the end, learning to play by ear is a lifelong process of listening, educating yourself on music theory, trial and error, and synthesizing all the knowledge you can about the art and craft of music, composition and songwriting.

# 15. INTRO TO LITERACY

#### In this section we will take a look at what it means to be musically literate. What types of things do you need to

#### know as a music student to become conversant in the language of music? How can you approach these things and work them into your daily lessons so you can be sure you are making progress in each of the most important areas? MUSIC AS COMMUNICATION

#### When we learn language skills as children we learn how to read and to write at roughly the same stage of our development. Unfortunately, this is not true of our music education. Music students are taught the basic fundamentals of how to read music in school when they learn to sing or begin an instrument, but they are rarely taught how to compose or even how to write down music in the early stages.

#### Learning how to speak language, read language and write language happens simultaneously in our culture because it is all part of what we call "literacy." If we were to take the same approach to music we would immerse students in musical communication skills in the same way we do language. In this scenario, learning to be musically literate would also include reading, writing and speaking. In musical terms this would mean reading music, composing music, and performing music at the same phase of our music education. But our society doesn’t place the same degree of value on music as a communications skill as it does traditional language. This is understandable. When you need to interview for a job or yell “fire!” or call for an ambulance, there is no need to do so in a song or in the proper key. The place for music education is within the realm of the Art’s and Humanities. So sadly, at least in our current culture, it will always take somewhat of a back seat to more “practical” areas of education.

If you compose a song and need to communicate it to your friends or bandmates, you can easily record it and send it along via the web or email. This doesn’t discount the benefits of reading music however. There is much to be gained by having a solid grasp of how to read and write music notation.

#### If you are reading this book you know that it doesn’t matter what type of music education you have received up to this point in your life, if any. You have no limits for what you can achieve on your own. We are all ultimately self-taught and in charge of our own musical destiny. We can choose at any point in our lives to pursue a well rounded musical education. By incorporating the things in this chapter into your daily practice routine you will begin the process of advancing steadily toward your goal of being a more capable, aware and literate musician.

# 16. READING MUSIC

#### TWO TYPES OF READING MUSIC: STUDYING & SIGHT READING There are two very different approaches to reading a musical score. The first is when we are intending to learn a

#### piece from the printed music, and the second is when we are intending to play the piece upon first sight of the sheet music.

#### In the first case we intend to study the piece so we are slowly learning the piece note by note measure by measure. We can take our time and experiment with different fingerings. We can stop and start and go back. We can work out any tricky chords or intervals we are uncomfortable with.

#### In the second case (called “sight reading”) we are attempting to play the music all the way through at a steady tempo and actually perform the music for the very first time literally “on sight.”

#### These two approaches are often at odds when we sit down with a piece of music. Many of us have little trouble understanding the rules of music and the meaning of the various symbols printed on the score. We know “how” to read the music. The sight reading aspect, that is, the reading a piece for the first time without stopping part, is often much more challenging to us.

#### It is no wonder. The brain has to process a tremendous amount of information when reading music and then has to translate that information to the fingers immediately in order to pull off a convincing rendition of the music. Sight reading a moderately complex piece of music is one of the most demanding things the human brain will ever undertake. This is a skill that takes years of persistent and patient practice to master.

#### The best way to approach the task of improving your overall reading ability is to incorporate a little bit of each type of reading (both studying and sight reading) into your daily practice routine. Always keep on hand a more difficult piece that you intend to study, and multiple unfamiliar pieces that you can use to practice sight reading.

#### Studying and sight reading are two completely different sets of skills and you need to approach each with a different goal in mind. Always know which type of reading you intend to do before beginning a practice session or you will surely find yourself somewhere in-between the two areas which can lead to frustration. If you intend to sight read, use very simple pieces that get progressively more challenging. If you intend to study a piece, it can be as challenging as you like. This will take a huge chunk of unnecessary frustration out of your practice sessions.

#### Decide beforehand how much time you can afford to spend on each and then make sure to do it consistently each day. Reading music is not “like riding a bike” where you can get right back on after years of not doing it. The phrase “use it or lose it” is more rightly applied where reading music is concerned. TIPS ON STUDYING A NEW PIECE

#### It is not the goal of this book to teach how to read music. That is the job of the method books that you use in your classroom, private lessons or in your role as self teacher. What I hope to do here is to provide some general advice about how you can get the most out of your practice sessions. 1. Listen To a Recording of the Piece

#### When tackling a new piece of music, one of the best things you can do is to go find a recording of it and listen to it closely and often. It is likely that you have at least heard it before, so go purchase the recorded version and bring it with you wherever you go. Listen to it while driving, doing the dishes, or wherever you can. You want to be sure you are aware of how every note relates to every other note. Listening at night as you are falling asleep is another good way. It has a chance then to seep into your subconscious awareness and you will likely pick up on subtleties that you may not hear when you are more actively listening. You don’t want to do this to the point that you become sick of hearing it, but you do want to know it well in your mind’s ear. 2. Look Over the Entire Score

#### Before beginning to attempt to play the piece, take the time to really look over the music and notice anything you can about what is going on. This will be of great value down the line because you will know what you are getting yourself into before you have even played one note. What is the key signature? Does it change anywhere in the piece? What is the time signature? Does it change? Does it change back again? What kind of patterns can you perceive by looking at the printed music? Are there certain rhythmic groupings that appear again and again such as triplets or extended eighth or sixteenth note measures? Is there a consistent use of certain chords or repeated motifs in the bass section? Pay attention to the dynamic markings for crescendo and decrescendos. Pay attention to the navigational directions in the score too. For example, where are the repeats and the coda? Make up your own

#### questions based on the things that you know often challenge you or make you falter. Observing these things will be beneficial prior to sight reading a piece as well, the difference is you have way

#### more time if you are preparing to study the piece. When sight reading you will have time only to glance at the music. In this case you intend to spend lots of time learning it measure by measure, so take your time and really look it over. 3. Visualization: Practicing In Your Mind

#### I have found that visualizing yourself playing the notes as you look over the score is a highly effective technique for learning a piece of music. This is best done away from your instrument so you are not tempted to pick it up and actually play it.

#### The way to do this is to sit in a comfortable chair with the sheet music in front of you. Take however long it takes with each measure and read through the piece, playing it in your imagination. The reason this is so effective is that you do all the mental work of deciphering the notes and fingerings before you sit down to your instrument. When you finally do sit down with your instrument all of that mental work has been accomplished already and your brain can focus more on the physical requirements of playing the piece.

#### I have found that this makes a huge difference in the amount of time spent learning a piece. This also has a way of increasing your memory of the piece once it is finally learned. 4. Follow Along With the Sheet Music While Listening to a Recording

#### Put the music you are intending to learn on your stereo and follow along with the printed music as you listen. This is an extremely helpful way to become familiar with the music you are about to devote a huge amount of practice time to. You can listen for various techniques, dynamics, transitions, repetitions, anything you think may help you when you sit down to practice it.

#### It is also a good idea to subscribe to an online music subscription service. Depending on how many songs you learn in a month or a year, this can save you a good chunk of change in the long run. Having the ability to hear a piece of music before you decide to commit to actually practicing it is another advantage. If your sight reading skills aren’t so great, hearing a recorded version or having a friend play it are your only options for being able to hear the piece. 5. Learn it Measure By Measure

#### This advice depends on several factors, primarily your level of sight reading ability and the difficulty of a piece. If you are a good sight reader and the first few measures aren’t too difficult, you might breeze right through them. When you get to a measure that gives you consistent trouble however, it is time to really zero in on things. Approach a difficult measure as if it were the entirety of the piece you are trying to learn. When you do this, and then consider the entire piece is really just a bunch of separate measures, it is easy to see how you can turn a mountain climbing adventure into a series of individual steps and conquer it by sheer persistence in taking each and every next step.

#### When you practice an individual measure or two, you release yourself from the psychological burden that comes from seeing those massive pages of notes in front of you. I often suggest to my students that they play a little game and imagine that they have been asked by their favorite band to perform the measure (or measures) that are giving them trouble as an introduction to one of their songs they are performing live at Madison Square Garden. The student only needs to master that one measure in order to go on stage with the band - just that one measure. Do you think that they could master it? They could, and by simply taking this approach measure after measure they will surely succeed in learning to play the entire piece. This foolproof nature of this method is that it teaches us how, in the end, success is all about persistence. 6. Use Slow Motion Techniques

#### If you have ever watched a sporting event on TV you have seen instant replays of great plays. These plays are slowed down by the producers frame by frame so that you can see every little move. The wide receiver fakes left. then runs right, step by step in front of the defender, he dives for the ball and makes an amazing catch in the end zone for a touchdown. You can use this slow motion technique in practicing a musical phrase as well. Imagine that you have already learned the piece perfectly at the required tempo and someone has videotaped your performance of it. Your job is to play the piece back as if you were viewing the super slow motion instant replay. Each note is carefully fingered and executed one after another with all the proper dynamics and technique just as if you had played the part fast, but are now viewing it in slow motion. When you do this you are forced to reduce every motion down to a careful, conscious one. You will be surprised how much you can learn from doing this. Anything giving you trouble will immediately become apparent so you can go about correcting it.

#### TIPS ON SIGHT READING 1. Relish Your Mistakes

#### Sight reading a piece means that you attempt to play it for the first time without ever having seen the music, or in some cases heard the music before. By nature then, there is little preparation that can be done using the song itself for that preparation. You must prepare for sight reading by constantly attempting to read new material. Your practice involves the act of putting a fresh sheet of music onto your music stand day after day, and playing through once, and one time only. By doing this you learn to deal with all kinds of surprises and challenges that will cause you to make mistakes. You want to make those mistakes, because the act of smoothly recovering from them is what you are actually practicing. 2. Understand What Your Mind Is Doing When You Sight Read

#### When you stop to think about all the things that are happening in your mind when you are attempting to sight read, it is surprising that smoke doesn’t start streaming out of your ears. When sight reading we are simultaneously looking at a bunch of dots, words, symbols, lines and spaces on a piece of paper, decoding those symbols into concepts of notes to be played on our instrument, finding those notes on the instrument with our fingers, playing the note or notes with the intended dynamics, while looking ahead to the next note or group of notes, playing the notes within the indicated rhythmic emphasis and tempo, following the navigational directions of the score etc. Besides all this you are listening to your playing and continuously judging and adjusting your performance of it. You adjust the volume of your playing, the emotion and dynamics of certain passages, the quality of your interpretation, and somehow you still manage to think about what you want for lunch! It is amazing to think that the mind is capable of all this when we sight read. That is why you should go easy on yourself if you find it to be difficult. It is. 3. Sight Read Material That is New to You

#### As stated above, the very definition of sight reading means you are reading something for the first time, so be sure to have a large supply of fresh music on hand that you can pull from. It doesn’t necessarily have to be music written for your instrument, although it helps if the music is in the same general range and that it is written on the same staff. If you are a guitarist, try reading music written for violinists. If you are a pianist try reading music for guitar or cello. The advantage pianists have is that they learn to read from two clefs, the G clef and the F clef (bass and treble clefs) so the amount of music they have to choose from is quite large. As long as the material is new to you it doesn’t matter what it is. You should try to read all kinds of different styles too. If you mainly play Rock, try reading some Jazz. If you mainly play Jazz, try reading some Pop, Rock or Classical. The different styles will expose you to surprising challenges but that is exactly what you are after. Remember that the goal is to be able to respond to the challenge of reading any type of music put before you, so don’t limit yourself to just the instrument or the genre of music that you typically play. 4. Choose Material That Is Within Your Technical Abilities

#### Remember that when practicing your sight reading, you are not trying to improve your technical playing ability. You are trying to improve your ability to read music on sight. If you choose things that are too difficult to play and have too much trouble playing them at an even tempo, no matter how slow that tempo is, then you have chosen something too difficult and it won’t help your sight reading ability to use that piece. Choose music that you think you can play at a very slow but even tempo. Look into beginner and intermediate lesson books for this type of thing. With practice you will be able to gradually increase the difficulty level of the pieces. 5. Visualize Success

#### Imagine how good it will feel when you can put moderately difficult pieces on your music stand and play right through them! How much fun would it be to try out new pieces and see how they sound before you decide to commit them to further study. Imagine how cool it would be to sit in with fellow musicians even though you have never before played the songs that they know? Beyond this there is another, deeper type of imagination that you can put to work. Try laying down or sitting in a comfortable chair and imagining yourself playing through and entire piece at first sight. Imagine you are scanning the page for details, taking a deep breath and beginning to play. You place your fingers on the instrument and begin to decipher the notes as they flow measure by measure, rising and falling, softly and loudly, sweetly and angrily. You roll through sixteenth notes, chords, arpeggios with the ease at which you would read through a mystery novel. In this exercise you want to get the sensation of what it actually feels like to sight read successfully so that you can aim for that same feeling in your practice.

#### Now let’s use a sports analogy again. This type of visualization is done all the time by athletes. If you have ever played a sport you have probably spent some time imagining how you would react if for example a line drive was suddenly hit toward you at third base and you had to make a diving catch, or in a tennis game and you had to make a

#### long reach to return a serve and win the final point of the match. The trick is to do the same thing with a difficult passage of music. Look at the notes on the page then close your eyes and imagine yourself playing the notes on your instrument. At first you will need to do this as a very slow imaginary tempo. Gradually speed up the tempo you are imagining until you reach the target tempo. You will find that if you can clearly imagine yourself playing the passage at the desired tempo, you will have no problem actually playing it at that speed.

#### These types of visualizations are very effective because you set up the expectation inside yourself that what you are trying to do is possible and within your capabilities. When you know you can do something because you have experienced it, even if it was only experienced in your imagination, you are inspired a bit more to keep working toward your goal because you already know what it feels like to succeed.

# 17. TRANSCRIPTION

#### WHAT IS TRANSCRIPTION? When we talk about transcribing music we are talking about taking an existing recording and writing down the

#### notes that you hear in standard musical notation. This involves listening for one or more instrumental parts and writing down the notes played, note values (whole note, half note, quarter note etc). It also includes determining key signatures, time signatures, properly dividing measures and defining song structure such as repeated sections, intros and codas.

#### Before the advent of recording technology, the only way to communicate how to play a piece of music was by writing it down in standard musical notation or by having someone who knew the music show it to you in person. Musicians have always been able to play by ear, but without the technology of recorded music always required two humans, not one human and one machine. (I’ll bet it was tricky trying to fast forward or rewind music teachers in the old days).

#### If you have learned to play a song by ear, you have basically learned how to do the first part of the transcription process; that is, you have figured out how to find the notes being played. The next step is to be able to write down what you have learned according to the standard rules of music theory. If you complete this next step, you will know how to transcribe a song and be a truly “literate” musician. WHY SHOULD YOU LEARN TO TRANSCRIBE?

#### There are many reasons why transcribing music can help you develop as a musician and expand your options for your career. 1. Transcription Gigs

#### You can get work as a transcriptionist with a publishing company that publishes sheet music. These companies always need help transcribing music from existing recordings as most artists don’t supply a musical score for their recorded work, most don’t even have one. 2. Teaching Transcription

#### If you are a teacher, you can transcribe music for your students as a way of providing them with sheet music. At the same time, you can teach them how to do their own transcriptions. 3. Historical Context

#### You will better understand the mechanics of music including its structure, form and terminology that has been passed down through the ages. 4. Ear Training

#### You will train your ear and improve your ability to hear musical intervals. A better ear will help you in every area of your musical development. 5. Transcribing Helps Your Composing Skills

#### You will acquire the ability to write down your own compositions and have a whole new way of approaching the art of composition. Many singer songwriters for example simply write down the lyrics and place chord symbols by the words. Fewer of them actually compose a melody in the traditional way and write down the musical notation of that melody. Having this ability enables the composer to take a more analytical approach to the music and make slight changes to the chords or melody to really hone the song to perfection.

#### Before the advent of the printing press, many great composers got their start by transcribing music for other composers. This is true even in recent times. I recall a hearing of how instrumental guitarist Steve Vai credited much of his compositional skills to the work he did transcribing the music of Frank Zappa. A similar approach works in other arts such as fiction writing and painting. Many of today's most famous authors have spent countless hours retyping the works of their favorite authors so they could get a sense of how they worked and get a feel for the flow of authoring a great novel or short story. Students of the great painters do this frequently as well, copying a masterwork stroke for stroke to try and uncover the secrets behind the composition and structure of a particular style or technique. 6. Transcribing Improves Your Sight Reading

#### If you spend time transcribing music. You will greatly improve your sight reading ability. Just as learning to write a spoken language improves your ability to read it, learning to write music down will help you be a better

#### reader of it as well. You will become fluent in identifying the various rhythmic groupings, symbols and dynamic markings after spending time hearing them and clearly writing them down in your transcriptions. A NOTE ON MUSIC TRANSCRIPTION SOFTWARE

#### There are many computer software programs available today that facilitate the music transcription process. I highly recommend these if you can afford them or have access to them through your school or university. Some of these will even allow you to directly record your instrument into the computer and the software will convert what you are playing into fully transcribed music!

#### Similarly there are programs that allow you to enter notes from any MIDI device and the software prints the corresponding notes on the staff including the rhythmic values. This is wonderful technology and is highly useful. To obtain an accurate version of the sheet music however, you must be able to either play the song perfectly upon input, or be able to intelligently edit the music file later. Even if your input is close to perfect there will always be a need for editing and the only way you can competently edit it is to know the rules and conventions of writing down music.

#### If you intend on using this type of software to ultimately do your transcriptions (and you will need to if you want to do professional transcriptions in todays technologically driven world) learning to transcribe music the “old school” way is still of primary importance. I highly recommend doing a few pencil and paper transcriptions before diving in full bore and using the computer software. What you learn in the process will actually save you a vast amount of time in learning the software.

#### Another reason why traditional knowledge is so important is that in most cases we want to transcribe a single instrument from a recorded mix of many instruments. Isolating and extrapolating separate instruments from a pre-recorded mix cannot be done very well by current music transcription software (to the delight of music publishing companies). In any case, all of the information in this chapter directly applies whether you are using the pencil and paper method or you are using music transcribing software. METHODS FOR TRANSCRIBING

#### There are two things you need to be able to do before you can attempt to transcribe music. The first is to develop the ability to hear a note or series of notes on a recording and find those notes on your instrument, and the second is the ability to read standard musical notation. If you can do both of these things you can easily learn to transcribe music.

#### Since the first part of transcription involves the ability to hear and identify the notes from a recording, you may want to refer to the section in this book on ear training. It would be redundant to include all those steps again here, so this chapter will simply provide advice for the actual writing part of the transcription process. It will assume that you can actually hear and decipher what notes are being played.

#### Start by choosing a piece of music to transcribe. Be sure it is not to difficult. It is best to start with an extremely easy piece for your first transcription. The goal in the early stages is to simply get a feel for how it is done and to build your confidence that you can really do it and that it can be fun. THE TRANSCRIPTION PROCESS

#### Here is a streamlined discussion of the various steps involved in the transcription process. This assumes that you are transcribing one instrument at a time on a single stave or if it is piano music, on two staves.

#### The equipment needed to transcribe is basically the same as the equipment needed to learn how to play by ear, so refer to that section for more details. For the most part you will need your instrument, a recording of the song you wish to transcribe, some staff paper, a pencil and an eraser. Pencil is always the best choice over a pen because mistakes are going to be plentiful and you don’t want your first draft to look like it would inevitably look if you used a pen.

#### Keep a copy of a sample piece of written music nearby while you are transcribing. This should be somewhat similar to the type of music you are transcribing. If you are transcribing a Classical etude, find a published etude to use as a reference, if you are transcribing a popular song, find the sheet music of a popular song to use as a reference. You can use these as a guide for how your music should look as you go along. If you don’t know how to draw a G clef, for example or you’re not sure where the time signature goes, or how to notate a triplet, you can reference this sheet music and learn as you go. 1. Listen to the song for the instrument you are transcribing.

#### Be sure you listen several times before attempting transcription. This will alert you to all kinds of things that will help you in the long run.

#### 2. Determine the time signature. Time signatures can change throughout a song so be sure to identify where this occurs beforehand so you are not

#### surprised by it later. Write the time signature at the beginning of the staff paper. (More information on how to identify time signatures can be found in the section on ear training). 3. Divide your staff paper into three or four equal measures per line.

#### Be sure to give yourself plenty of space per measure. As you know, some fast measures may contain 16 or 32 notes! If you’ve listened to the song however you probably are already aware whether it has 32nd notes in it. If it does, you should probably choose another song as your first transcription! 4. Determine the chord progression. (Harmonic Progression)

#### (If you need help in identifying a chord progression, see the section on ear training.) Determining the chord progression will be more important for Rock, Pop and Jazz type songs than if you are transcribing a Classical piece. If you don’t think you need to do this you can skip this step. The reason you want to do this is that the chord progression will help you in identifying where the various measures begin and end. It will also help you identify the notes being played in each measure. I highly encourage you to take this step if you can. Identifying the chord progression is the best thing you can do to ensure accuracy in your transcription. This is because most melody notes come from chord tones or from tones in the diatonic scale of the chord in question. It will take some of the guesswork out of identifying notes. For example, if you have written down a C Major7 chord and you hear a note sustaining that you think might be a Bb, it is most likely not a Bb, but a B, since the note B is in the C Major7 chord and the note Bb is not. (If it is a passing tone however it might very well be a Bb.) Since your efforts at first will be based very much on trial and error this will help pare down the number of options you have for note choices. 5. Write the chord name at the top of the corresponding measure.

#### This involves identifying the root of the chord and then the type and quality of the chord. For example, major or minor plus any extension such as 6, 7, 9, sus4, etc. 6. Identify the notes one measure at a time and write them down.

#### At first, listen for just one or two notes at a time, then stop the recording, find the notes on your instrument, identify them and write them down on the staff. Do this one measure at a time. It is important that you don’t concern yourself with specific note values yet (such as whole note, half note, quarter note, eighth note etc.) just place a circle on the proper line of the staff. A circle is preferable to a dot because you can later fill these in. It is easier to fill in a circle later than to erase and redraw a dot if it turns out to be a half note or a whole note. For the same reasons, you don’t want to connect any note beams at this point either. If you are really confident about any of this however, go ahead and write them in. Sometimes it’s just easier to see your progress this way. Identifying notes and writing them down in the proper measure will obviously be the bulk of your work. 7. Listen for repeating measures and repeating sections.

#### If a measure is repeating over and over again you can save time by using the repeat measure symbol (division sign) in each identical measure. If a series of non-identical measures is repeating, you can place repeat dots at the beginning and end of the section that repeats. Be careful to identify any sections that seem to repeat but have different ending measures. It is very common for composers to put slight variations on nearly identical repeating sections. For this reason you may want to forget about repeating sections in your first draft and simply record everything measure for measure. You can always account for the repeats in the next draft. This will keep you from messing things up if you thought something repeated exactly and then realized later that it was slightly different causing you to have to erase large sections of completed work. 8. Fill in the note values

#### Once you have transcribed the song (or at least a major section of the song) you will want to go back over it and assign the proper note values to the notes if you have not already done so. This can be one of the trickiest parts of the transcription process so be prepared for some frustration when you get the measures messed up. Listening for the first beat of a measure is always the best way to define your measures. If you have done that, what comes between the bar lines is pretty much an exercise in math. Everything just has to add up to the total number of beats assigned to the measure. This is made much easier by music transcription software which does the math for you and alerts you when the measure doesn’t add up properly. It is at this point that you will also draw in any connecting beams, tied notes, dotted notes etc. 9. Verify the navigational directions and dynamic markings

#### At this stage you’ll want to listen to the song and put in the various navigational instructions such as DC, DS, AL

#### CODA, etc. You can also go through and add any special instructions and expression marks for crescendo and decrescendo. 10. Read through the piece and make corrections

#### When you think you’re finished read through the piece and make any necessary changes to your transcription. If you have done this with paper and pencil, all you need to do is break out the computer software and input the finished piece into the software. If you have input your transcription into the software from the beginning, you are pretty much done and can print out your score for another proof reading cycle. Some software programs will allow you to optically scan in your printed version to create a quick digital rendition of your score. This always requires pretty heavy editing however so be warned. 11. Have someone else review your score

#### If possible, ask a fellow musician or a music instructor review your work.This always helps in discovering things that you have overlooked as a result of being too close to the piece for so long. Be open to criticism and expect there to be errors. The more errors you make and correct the more opportunities for growth you have. SUMMARY

#### By taking the time to make a serious attempt at transcription you have made a giant leap forward in your musical education. As stated earlier, the act of transcribing music is one of the most beneficial things you can do to improve your musicianship and overall knowledge of music theory. Whatever time you put into it will surely pay you back big in the long run.

#### Congratulations, you have just transcribed your first piece of music. Now you know how Bach and Mozart spent their evenings instead of sitting around watching TV.

# 18. STRUCTURE

### “A scene has to have a rhythm of its own, a structure of its own.” - Michelangelo Antonioni

#### In this chapter we will examine the basics of song form and structure. I have chosen to concentrate on songs in order to keep things to a manageable size, but this general system of organization works much the same for any musical form. Studying structure is essential to understanding the mechanics and architecture of all music genres that use the song form such as Pop, Rock, Jazz, Blues, Reggae or Classical. THE STRUCTURE OF SONG

#### Throughout history music has taken on many overlapping shapes and forms: etudes, operas, blues progressions, arias, preludes, overtures, fugues, symphonies, improvisations, jam sessions... These forms are performed, recorded, written down and charted. They are packaged, sold, remixed, regrouped, and compiled into playlists. As musicians and as music fans we have a preference for different forms, just as we have a preference for different styles and artists. With all of this variety, the primary musical form of our day has become the song. It is our modern word for "sonata". It is easy to understand why it has remained so popular. The song is more accessible in today's fast paced world when compared to symphonies, operas, or extended improvisational jam sessions. A song averages three minutes, gets a simple musical or poetic point across quickly, and they can be grouped together into segments for commercial packaging such as radio shows, CD's, and Internet downloads.

#### In the ear training section you learned how to identify a root progression and convert that into a chord progression. We will also learn how to recognize repeating patterns. These repeating sections usually represent the various parts of a song that are commonly known as intro, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, middle eight, and coda.

#### Sometimes there is confusion about these terms because different people will use them to describe different things and because music by nature doesn't always want to follow strict rules or classifications. With that in mind, it might be helpful to look at each term and sketch out a general definition. Intro

#### The intro or "introduction" of a song is what the name implies. It is simply the beginning of the song. Some songs will have elaborate intros that last for several or many measures, some have very short one measure intros, and some songs don't really have one at all. This is not to say that some songs don't have a beginning (of course); it is to recognize that some songs will start immediately with the verse or the chorus and not lead in with anything different from the rest of the song. Verse

#### A song's verse is the part of the song where the chords and melodies are for the most part identical with each repetition, but the lyrics are different. The verse is where the details of a song's story are told by introducing the characters, setting, or subject matter of the song. Pre-Chorus

#### The pre-chorus leads the listener from the verse or series of verses to the chorus. It often uses the same lyrics with each instance but sometimes the words can be different each time. The point of the pre-chorus is usually to build up the energy level and anticipation for the musical excitement that is coming in the chorus. Some people prefer to call this section the "bridge" because it spans the space between verse and chorus. (We will define the bridge later as a different section with a different purpose.) Chorus

#### The defining aspect of the chorus is that it repeats the same chords, melody and lyrics each time it is repeated, unlike the verse which repeats the same music but with different words. It is the central part of the song and the most important part in the overall identity of the song. The title of the song is usually but not always found in the chorus. The chorus most often contains the defining melody of the song which is called the "hook". This hook is what record executives are always looking for because it is the catchiest, most memorable part of the song - the part that gets stuck in your head when you hear it on the radio and makes you go out and buy or download it. As such it is often considered the part that sells the song. The hook is a subjective thing and is not always in the chorus however. Bridge or Middle Eight

#### In many songs there is a section near the middle-end that occurs only once in the song. It functions lyrically to inject some new information or insight into the song, and functions musically to inject some melodic variety into the

#### repetitiveness of too many verse/chorus cycles. This section is called different things by different people, resulting in much confusion among band members,

#### songwriters and music fans. In this book I'll call it the Bridge and call the section before the chorus the pre-chorus. It is sometimes called the “middle eight” because often it consists of eight measures and is found near the middle. The problem is that it just as often contains four measures, or six, or twelve, or sixteen. Coda

#### The Coda section of the song simply refers to the ending. Like the intro, the coda can be long or short, or not exist at all. Sometimes a song will just end at the end of a verse or chorus (rarely will a song end with a bridge or pre-chorus, but it does happen). Songs that utilize a coda generally do so as a way of winding the song down and resolving the melodies to the tonic chord or key center of the song. This provides an overall feeling of resolution to all that has come before in the tune.

#### Once you understand why each of these song parts exist and get a feel for how they function in the overall context of the song, identifying them will greatly enhance your ability to remember how to play the song. For example, you will be able to summarize a song by saying, "this song goes: intro, verse, verse, chorus, then repeats until the coda. A/B Structure

#### Another popular way of summarizing a song's structure is to assign each part of a song a letter name. This is usually referred to as the "A/B Structure". This is very common in Jazz to the point where Jazz charts will actually print the letter names above each section. A publishing practice less rarely used in Classical or Popular music. To do this you simply assign a letter name to the various sections as they first appear in the song. If we use the example in the above paragraph we would assign A to intro, B to verse, C to chorus, and D to the bridge. We could then summarize the structure of the song by saying, "this song goes: ABBC repeat, D. To cite another example, if a song goes verse, chorus, verse, chorus, verse, chorus, we would identify it's structure as ABACAB. (Now you know where the Progressive Rock band "Genesis" got the name for the title track of one of their hit albums called ABACAB!)

#### In music theory all kinds of names have been assigned to various configurations of the A/B structure, for example: songs that have just one part AAAA are called “strophic” forms, songs that consist of just A and B parts such as AABBAA are called “binary” forms and songs that have a series of unrelated parts are called “medleys”. There are too many of these to concern ourselves with them in this book. The fact that they have names is important in a larger sense but for our purposes here it is enough to understand that songs sections are often assigned letter names such as these, and that some common patterns are considered to by types of songs.

### THE NASHVILLE NUMBERING SYSTEM

#### The Nashville Numbering System is a way for musicians to communicate the chord progression of a song separately from any particular key. Referring to chord progressions this way facilitates a quick and easy transposition from one key to another. It works like this:

#### All keys have 7 potential diatonic chords based on the 7 notes of the corresponding scale. Each chord in the key is assigned a roman numeral, I through VII (one through seven). So instead of saying a song goes "C Dm7 G7" you would say it goes I IIm7 V7 in the key of C. Roman numerals are used for chord numbers to avoid confusion with the numbers used for chord types. If the key is changed, the chords symbols don't need to change because they contain no letter names that refer to a specific key.

#### You can see that to account for the different types of chords, the same abbreviations are used for the various chord types and qualities. For example, in the key of C, if a Major 7 chord was called for, the first chord would not be written as CM7 but instead would be written IM7. If the second chord in the key was called for it would be referred to not as Dm7 but as IIm7. If the fifth chord in the song was called for, it would not be referred to as G7, but V7. This method simply replaces the letter name of the chord with a number that represent that chords placement in the key.

#### Using this system serves two main purposes. First it allows you to change keys quickly without rewriting or re-memorizing all the chord names, and secondly it indicates how the chord is functioning in the given key. In other words when you see a chord "CM7" you don't know if that is functioning as the I chord in the key of C or the IV chord in the key of G. With the Nashville Numbering system you know exactly how the chord is functioning in the song. Sample "standard" chord progression

#### C, Am, Dm7, G7 The same chord progression using the Nashville Numbering System:

#### I, VIm IIm7, V7 Advanced musicians would not necessarily need the chord types defined since they are determined by the degree

#### of the chord within the major scale as long as the chords are truly diatonic to the key (meaning they use no notes from outside the key signature). So they could simplify even further.

#### I, VI II, V, I In most cases however, the chord types are added to the numbers for the sake of clarity. There as many variations on this as there are musicians who chart songs, especially with regard to the symbols

#### used to specify minor and major. It is enough for our purposes here to simply understand that this system exists, is used by professional musicians and is a very useful way of describing a song's chord progression. More in-depth study requires a deeper understanding of the major scale and the way individual chords are constructed. SUMMARY

#### The importance of understanding song structure should not be underestimated if your goal is to become a versatile musician. All professional musicians refer to these structural components in their day to day work. Knowing the names of song parts and being able to identify a songs overall structure will help you as a musician in many ways. It will help you compose a song, improvise on a song, memorize a song, and most importantly it will help you communicate effectively with other musicians.

# 19. CHORDS, SCALES AND INTERVALS

#### For many students of music, scales, modes, chords and intervals can weave a complex and sticky web of

#### meaningless, tangled up letters and numbers. The fact is that each of these simply describe different aspects the same thing. With a little explanation the mess can easily be untangled.

#### First, let's get a brief definition of each term. You will notice that one definition often includes terms from the other. Hence, the entanglement. If you read over the following section a few times, everything should eventually come together and fall into place like so many pieces of a jigsaw puzzle.

### SCALE

#### A series of notes with a predetermined set of intervals between the notes. Each successive note is called a "degree". The first note of the scale functions to name the scale and is called the "root".

### MODE

#### Identical to a scale, but often described as a separate concept. Just like a scale, a mode is a simply a series of notes with a particular set of intervals between the notes. There are seven modes of the major scale. Ionian, Dorian, Phyrgian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian and Locrian. The familiar “major” and “minor” scales are actually two of these seven modes: “Ionian” is simply another name for the major scale, and Aeolian is another name for the minor scale. (See separate chapter on modes.)

### CHORD

#### A chord is a group of notes sounded at the same time. It takes at least three notes to make a chord. These three notes are called “triads”. (Some argue that two notes together can be called a chord. More on that later). A chord can be thought of as selected notes pulled from a particular scale and played simultaneously. To form a basic triad you take the first, third and fifth notes from the scale. The root note of the scale from which the notes are taken names the chord.

### ARPEGGIO

#### The notes of a chord sounded one after another and not at the same time.

### INTERVAL

#### The distance between any two notes of a chord or scale. Intervals are measured by the number of whole steps and half steps between the two notes. Whole step is the equivalent of moving two piano keys away in either direction. A half step is the equivalent of moving one key away in either direction. HOW SCALES GET THEIR NAMES

#### Scales get their names by how the are constructed. They are constructed by linking together a series of whole steps and half steps until the root note is reached again. The resulting span from root note to root note is called an "octave." On most instruments it is possible to play at least two or more complete octaves of a scale. The number of octaves that can be played on an instrument is known as an instrument's "range". WHAT MAKES A MAJOR SCALE "MAJOR"

#### "DO RE MI FA SO LA TI DO" is an example of a major scale. Musicians way back when decided this was the most pleasing scale to the ear, so they made this the "King of Scales." No problem. Someone has to be king, so we'll just go with it.

#### The following series of whole and half steps define the sound of the major scale. DO (WHOLE) RE (WHOLE) MI (HALF) FA (WHOLE ) SO (WHOLE) LA (WHOLE) TI (HALF) DO USING C Major as an example. C Major Scale C (WHOLE) D (WHOLE) E (HALF) F (WHOLE ). G (WHOLE) A (WHOLE) B (HALF) C

#### It is the pattern of whole and half steps in a scale that determines how we perceive the "happy" sound of the major scale. The notes themselves have nothing to do with it. They are chosen by the demand that the next interval be either a whole or a half step. We now know that a major scale is a major scale because it has this pattern. There is no other reason to call this a major scale. The definition of a major scale then is: A series of notes consisting of the following step pattern.

#### W W H W W W H (Where W = Whole and H = Half)

#### It doesn't matter that the major scale was arbitrarily selected, the fact is that every other type of chord, interval, scale or mode acquires it's name by being directly compared to, and measured against it. WHAT MAKES A MINOR SCALE MINOR?

#### The C minor scale is the C MAJOR SCALE with a lowered third degree, sixth degree and seventh degree. This means the step pattern has changed in relation to the major scale. Three notes have been lowered or "flatted" so the pattern is now:

#### C (WHOLE) D (HALF) \*Eb (WHOLE) F (WHOLE ). G (HALF) Ab (WHOLE) \* Bb (WHOLE) C \* flatted notes The flatting of the notes has changed, so the whole and half step pattern of the scale has changed. The scale is

#### now considered a "C minor" scale. The pattern of whole and half steps is what gives the scale its distinctive sound. The minor scale is often said to sound “sadder” or more serious than the happier sounding major scale. This mood is subjective but determined solely by the arrangement of the intervals in the scale.

#### As you know, there are many different types of scales. All scales and modes are determined by just two things. The root note, and the subsequent whole/half step pattern. Our purpose has been to describe how the major scale is the "King of Scales" and why intervals also take their names from the king! Next we will look at the various intervals. Intervals are simply names for the distance between notes. NAMING INTERVALS

#### Intervals have names too. Here are the names and the distance between notes they represent. Minor Second: one half step Major Second: one whole step Minor Third: one whole step plus one half step Major Third: two whole steps Perfect Fourth: two whole steps plus one half step Augmented Fourth: three whole steps Augmented Fifth: five whole steps Diminished Fifth: three whole steps Minor Sixth: five whole steps Major Sixth: five whole steps, plus one half step Minor Seventh: six whole steps Major Seventh: six whole steps, plus one half step Diminished Seventh: five whole steps Octave: seven whole steps

#### All of these various intervals get their names by comparing them to the intervals of the major scale. "Major" means the interval is the same as the major scale interval. "Minor" means the interval has been flatted by one half step as compared to the major scale interval. "Perfect" means the same thing as "Major". "Augmented" means the interval has been increased by one half step. "Diminished" means the interval has been reduced a half step from the original major scale interval. "Octave" means the span from root to root. "Second" indicates the second note of the scale. "Third" indicates the third note of the scale. "Fourth" indicates the fourth note of the scale. "Fifth" indicates the fifth note of the scale. "Sixth" indicates the sixth note of the scale.

#### "Seventh" indicates the seventh note of the scale. OTHER SCALE TERMS TO KNOW Diatonic

#### A group of notes or chords are said to be "diatonic" if they all belong to the same key. For example, the notes C D E F G A and B are all notes of the C major scale so we say they are diatonic to the key of C major. Similarly the chords C, Dm7, Em7, F, G7, Am7 and Bm7b5 are all made up of notes that are within the key of C major so these chords are also described as diatonic. Pentatonic

#### Pentatonic is most often used to refer to "pentatonic scales." These scales are five note scales (hence the prefix "penta" which is a Latin term meaning five). There are major pentatonic scales and minor pentatonic scales.

#### The major pentatonic scale takes the first, second, third, fifth and sixth notes from it's parent major scale. The minor pentatonic takes the first, flat third, fourth, fifth and flat seventh notes from it's parent major scale. Example: C major pentatonic scale: C D E G A Example: C minor pentatonic scale: C Eb F G Bb

#### So you can see that, in the first case,we used the major third interval in our pentatonic scale - so we must call that scale "major" pentatonic;and in the second case, we used a minor third and a minor 7 interval in our scale, so we must call that scale "minor" pentatonic.

#### The terminology can seem intimidating, but you can see that everything simply relates to the original major scales pattern of half steps and whole steps. All the terminology surrounding chord construction modify these intervals in some way.

# 20. UNDERSTANDING CHORD SYMBOLS

#### To beginning students of polyphonic (capable of playing more than one note at a time) instruments like piano

#### and guitar chords are first understood as just blocks of notes that need to be played together to accompany a singer or soloist. We are often not aware of the wealth of information stored in that seemingly trivial chord symbol. Players of instruments that rarely play chords hardly need to concern themselves with chords at all. That is, until they have to understand them in order to compose or improvise. At that point chords become something altogether different.

#### A chord symbol contains a world of the information within it. This information contained in a chord symbol sets up everything each player needs to know to make every decision that needs to be made whether it be a structural, artistic, stylistic, harmonic or even rhythmic decision. The unfortunate thing is that many soloists don't pay attention to the underlying chords during their solo other than to determine the key center. By contrast, a great soloist knows what chord represents the harmony being suggested at any given measure in the song. CONSIDERING CHORDS WHEN SOLOING, COMPOSING OR TRANSCRIBING

#### Chords are the sonic equivalent to the pallets that painters paint on. The choices an artist makes about type, color, and texture of the paints he will use depend on the qualities of the canvas. The type, color and texture of notes that a soloist plays likewise depend on the qualities of the chord being played at a given time in music.

#### What this means is that whether you are learning an existing part or improvising, it helps to have a solid understanding of what the underlying chords in the music are. This is often the difference between a bad solo, a good solo or a great solo. Just as a painter will choose certain colors to create or release tension in the viewers mind, so does a musician choose notes that will create similar moods tensions and resolutions in the listeners mind.

#### But just how does this work? Why do certain notes played against certain chords create these moods, colors and tensions? Answering that question requires a thorough understanding of the way chords are constructed; the secret lies in the complex fabric that can be woven among chords, scales and intervals. THE SECRETS OF CHORD SYMBOLS

#### Let's look at the information that is coded into chord symbols, and how to properly decode them. We'll do this by a series of examples. Reading and re-reading this section should make all kinds of light bulbs go on.

### C

#### C = C E G

#### If a letter stands alone with no other letter or numbers, this indicates a major chord, so "C" simply means "C Major".

#### All chords are built on a foundation of triads (meaning simply “three notes"). The major chord takes the first, the third, and the fifth note out of the major scale to form the triad. (From now on, we will use roman numerals for scale degrees. which is standard practice. It eliminates confusion between degrees of a scale and chord names). The C major scale is spelled C D E F G A B C, so we take the first, third and fifth notes (C E G) out of the scale to form the chord.

#### The notes can be stacked in any order but usually the root note is on the bottom, then the third note, then the fifth note. If you stack them differently you have what is called an inversion.

### C7 (C seventh)(also called C dominant seventh)

### C7 = C E G Bb

#### The capital C is the root, the 7 means to add the flatted seventh note of the major scale to the triad, We take the I, III, IV, and bVII notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, E, G, and Bb.

### Cm (C minor)

### Cm = C Eb G

#### The lowercase "m" stands for "minor". This means the third and the sixth notes from the C major scale are to be flatted by one half step. So we take notes I, bIII, and V from the scale to form the chord. We get C, Eb and G. These three notes form a C minor triad that we write as "Cm."

### Cm7 (C minor seventh)

### Cm7 = C Eb G Bb

#### The capital C is the root, the lowercase "m" stands for minor, and the 7 means to add the flatted seventh note of the major scale to the triad. We take the I, bIII, V, and bVII notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, Eb, G, and Bb.

### CM7 (C major seventh)

### CM7 = C E G B

#### The capital C is the root, the uppercase M stands for “Major 7” We take the I, bIII, V, and VII notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, E, G, and B.

### Cm7b5 (C minor seven flat five)

### Cm7b5 = C Eb Gb Bb

#### The capital C is the root, the lowercase m stands for minor, the b5 means to use the flatted fifth note of the major scale in place of the usual fifth note, the 7 means to add the flatted seventh note to the chord. We take the I, bIII, bV, bVII notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, Eb, Gb, and Bb.

### Cdim (C diminished seventh)

### Co7 = C Eb Gb A

#### The capital C is the root, the “o” means diminished. Diminished means to flat the third and the fifth note of the major scale and double flat the seventh. We take the I, bIII, bV and bb7 from the C major scale and we get the notes C, Eb, Gb, A.

### C6 (C sixth)

### C6 = C E G A

#### The capital C is the root, the 6 stands for “Major 6”. We take the I, III, IV, and VI notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, E, G, and A.

### Cm6 (C minor sixth)

### Cm6 = C Eb G A

#### The capital C is root, the m stands for minor, the 6 means to add the sixth note of the major scale to the triad. We take the I, bIII, V, and VI notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, Eb, G, and A.

### C9 (C ninth)

### C9 = C E G Bb D

#### Whenever there is a number greater that 7 in a chord symbol simply subtract 7 from it and use the remainder as the desired interval. The remainder will indicate the desired degree of the major scale to add to the triad. The fact that a number higher than 7 is used in the chord symbol instead of a 2 (in this case 9) is because the added 2nd should be from a higher octave and should appear on the top of the other notes in the chord. A number higher than 7 also requires that we add a flat seven to the triad. In the case of C9, for example:

#### The capital C is the root, the 9 means to add the ninth note and the flatted seventh note of the major scale to the triad. Subtract 7 from 9 to get 2. We take the I, III, V, bVII and II notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, E, G, Bb and D. It is important that the 9 (D) should be played in the upper register of the chord voicing.

### C13 (C thirteenth)

### C13 = C E G Bb A C9 = C E G Bb A

#### Like the 9 chord, whenever there is a number greater that 7 in a chord symbol simply subtract 7 from that number and use the remainder as the desired interval. In this case 13 - 7 = 6, so a 13 chord is really a 6 chord. The fact that a number higher than 7 was used in the chord symbol means the added 6th should be from a higher octave and should appear on the top of the other notes in the chord. A number higher than 7 also requires that we add a flat seven to the triad. In the case of C13, for example:

#### The capital C is the root, the 13 means to add the thirteenth note and the flatted seventh note of the major scale to the triad. Subtract 7 from 13 to get 6. We take the I, III, V, bVII and VI notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, E, G, A and Bb. It is important that the 13 (A) should be played in the upper register of the chord voicing.

### Cm9 (C minor nine)

### Cm9 = C Eb G Bb D

#### The capital C is root, the 9 means to add the ninth note and the flatted seventh note of the major scale to the triad. Subtract 7 from 9 to get 2. We take the I, bIII V, bVII and II notes from the C major scale and we get the notes C, Eb, G, Bb and D. The 9 means (D) should be places in the upper register of the chord voicing.

### C+ (C Augmented) (also called “C#5”)

### C+ = C E G#

#### The capital C is the root, the + means to augment (raise) the fifth note of the major scale by one half step. We take the I, III, and #5 from the major scale and get the notes C, E, G#.

### Csus4 (C suspended fourth)(also called C11)

### Csus4 = C F G Bb

#### When you see a chord with the word “sus” in it this means “suspended”. It instructs you to leave out or “suspend” the third note of the major scale and replace it with the fourth note of the major scale instead. Because

#### there is no longer a third degree included in the chord, this chord is neither major nor minor. The flatted seventh note of the scale is optional. This means we would take the I, IV, V, and b7 notes from the major scale to get the notes C, F, G, and Bb.

### “SLASH” CHORDS

#### Whenever you see a chord with a slash in it such as “C/Bb” this means you should play a C triad (C E G) but instead of playing C as the bass note (meaning lowest note) of the chord you would play a Bb as the bass note instead.

#### Another example: D/A would mean to play a D major triad with an A in the bass instead of D. Chord symbols can be confusing at first but after studying them for a while there is a clear, reassuring logic that

#### emerges - a “method to the madness” so to speak. It is well worth your time to hang in there until you see that logic because the language surrounding chord nomenclature is the essential language of harmony. Understand that language and in a very real sense you will understand much about music that was previously beyond your grasp.

# 21. UNDERSTANDING MODES

#### Much has been written about modes and many students are completely mystified by them. It is too bad because

#### really they are just different types of scales. As we learned in the chapter on scales this simply means that they have a distinctive pattern of whole and half step patterns. In this sense they are just like major and minor scales. In fact the major and minor scales themselves are two of the seven modes! This means that there are only five remaining modes to learn. THE NAMES OF THE SEVEN MODES

#### 1. Ionian (Major Scale) 2. Dorian 3. Phrygian 4. Lydian 5. Mixolydian 6. Aeolian (Minor Scale) 7. Locrian

#### I once came across a useful mnemonic device for remembering the names of the seven modes in the proper order. That is: "I Don't Play Lame Music Anymore Lately". CHANGING THE ROOT OF THE MAJOR SCALE TO BUILD A MODE

#### You can think of a mode as a major scale using a different scale degree as its root. The numbers in the above list are important because they correspond to the scale degree of the major scale that we will use as our new root. The actual notes of the scale will stay the same. You will just consider a different note from the scale to be your root. The following list illustrates which note of the scale to use as your root for each mode.

#### 1. Ionian (Major Scale) - The first note of the major scale is the root 2. Dorian - The second note of the major scale becomes the root 3. Phrygian - The third note of the major scale becomes the root 4. Lydian - The fourth note of the major scale becomes the root 5. Mixolydian - The fifth note of the major scale becomes the root 6. Aeolian (Minor Scale) - The sixth note of the major scale becomes the root 7. Locrian - The seventh note of the major scale becomes the root

#### To hear what any of these modes sounds like, play the notes of any major scale starting on the proper note for each mode. In the key of C you would play the notes from C major but start on the following notes instead of starting on C.

#### 1. Ionian (Major Scale) Play from C to C 2. Dorian Play from D to D 3. Phrygian Play from E to E 4. Lydian Play from F to F 5. Mixolydian Play from G to G 6. Aeolian (Minor Scale) Play from A to A 7. Locrian Play from B to B

#### STEP PATTERNS OF THE SEVEN MODES The sound of any scale or mode is a result of the pattern of whole and half steps in the scale. This alone is what

#### gives the modes their distinctive and characteristic sounds. When you change your starting note you change the step patterns. Here are the step patterns for the seven modes.

#### 1. Ionian (Major Scale) W W H W W W H 2. Dorian W H W W W H W 3. Phrygian H W W W H W W

#### 4. Lydian W W W H W W H 5. Mixolydian W W H W W H W 6. Aeolian (Minor Scale) W H W W H W W 7. Locrian H W W H W W W

#### CONSIDERING MODES TO BE RELATIVE SCALES Now you can see that the C major scale and the A minor scale have the same notes and that the only difference is

#### the starting note. This relationship is often referred to as “relative”. We can now see that there are five other relative scales (or modes) of C major. All of the following scales use the notes of the C major scale.

#### 1. The C Ionian Scale 2. The D Dorian Scale 3. The E Phrygian Scale 4. The F Lydian Scale 5. The G Mixolydian Scale 6. The A Aeolian (Minor Scale) 7. The B Locrian Scale

#### These modes could all be described as relative scales of C major. Just like the relationship between major and minor is said to be relative because they share the same notes, each of these modes share the same notes too. We can therefore consider these scales to be relatives as well. The family’s getting bigger!

#### We have been using the key of C as an example so far, but they can be applied to all twelve keys. At this point it may be helpful to look at how this plays out in a key other than C major. Let’s look at the key of G major to get some perspective. NOTES IN THE KEY OF G MAJOR

#### G A B C D E F# G If we apply the mode patterns to the G major scale, we get the following scale names.

### MODES OF THE G MAJOR SCALE

#### 1. The G Ionian Scale 2. The A Dorian Scale 3. The B Phrygian Scale 4. The C Lydian Scale 5. The D Mixolydian Scale 6. The E Aeolian (Minor Scale) 7. The F# Locrian Scale

#### The names of the scales/modes have changed because we are now using the G major scale as our source instead of C major. The step patterns of each of the modes remains the same.

#### We could continue this chapter and go through all twelve keys in this fashion, but that would not be necessary, productive, or very much fun. If you understand this for one key, it is easy to apply it to all other keys. You would choose a major scale, use each degree of that scale to name its modes, and consider each of those degrees to be your roots. What results in each case is a scale relative to the major scale that has a new whole step half step pattern, but uses notes identical to the major scale. APPLYING THE MODES

#### The next step in understanding modes is knowing how to use them in your playing. This step greatly depends on the underlying chord progression that you will be soloing over. An entire book could easily be devoted to this subject so I will use one example and assume you can extrapolate this to other keys and playing situations.

#### To best illustrate this we will compose a chord progression to be used for our purposes. This is not to say that you have to compose your own song in order to apply the modes. When you understand the concept you will be able to look at an existing chord progression and choose an appropriate mode to play in. This ability will enable you to purposefully conjure up a wide variety of moods and colors in your sound.

#### For our example we will choose to use the phrygian mode. We know that the phrygian mode is based on using

#### the third note of the C major scale, which is E. This means we will want to use E as our root note, but play all of the notes from the C major scale. Our scale now looks like this: E F G A B C D E. When we play this scale from root to root we can be said to be playing in the "E Phrygian Mode" or to be playing the "E Phrygian Scale", take your pick.

#### Next we will create a chord progression using chords from the C major scale, but for the same reason our scale now resolves to E instead of C, our chord progression must do the same: it too must resolve to the third degree in the key of C which happens to be an E minor chord. There aren't many rules beyond this, so here goes, here is our chosen chord progression. Em F Am7 Em. The chords were all chosen from the key of C major. You can see that things begin and end on The E minor chord so the chords and the melodic phrases will both be resolving to Em.

#### Now you simply need to record that chord progression so you can practice soloing over it or have a friend play the chords for you if you want to hear how it sounds. If you are a pianist you can play the chords with your left hand and the notes with your right. If you are a guitarist you can play the open E string while simultaneously playing the notes of C major and resolving the phrases to E.

#### This is a very simple example but should demonstrate the point well. Modes have confused musicians for centuries. It will pay off to keep things as simple as possible at first. To try the other modes simply repeat the process using different degrees of the scale and choosing the corresponding chord to resolve to. For example, to try D Dorian mode which is based on the second note of the major scale, play the notes of the C major scale, using D as your root, over a chord progression that resolves to D minor. Do this for all seven modes and then try it with a different key.

#### To apply the modes in existing songs that you may need or want to solo over, look for songs or chord progressions that use chords from a key but seem to resolve to a chord other than the expected one. For example, if you notice that all of the chords in a progression belong to the key of C major, but the last chord of the solo section resolves to G (the fifth degree of C major) and not C, then it is likely that the Myxolydian mode is a great choice. Likewise, if you notice that the chords are all from C major but everything resolves to F, then you can try the Lydian mode - because F is the fourth degree of C major.

#### When players get into trouble with modes it is usually because they are trying to use them in the "wrong" situation. That is why a careful examination of the underlying chord structure is essential for any aspiring soloist.

#### It can sometimes happen that you can accidentally discover these modes. Before I knew anything about them, I asked a fellow guitar player how he learned so much about the modes. I'll never forget this because what he told me was: "I hear you using them all the time. You just don't know that your using them." Later I figured out that he was right. I would often experiment with playing what I knew to be "wrong" scales in certain situations because they just sounded cool. I didn't know why they sounded so good because I thought I was playing in the wrong key. The fact was that while I was just using my ears and applying some scale fingerings that I had learned, I was accidentally happening upon one or another of the modes. This was okay, but I wanted to get the sound I was looking for intentionally and not have to rely on accidentally finding that sound by trial and error. This is what inspired me to finally get serious, open the theory books and start learning my modes.

# 22. THE NATURE OF INTERVALS

#### Exploiting the differences between major and minor is a fairly well kept secret among the great players. It is not

#### a purposeful conspiracy however. The primary reason that this seems so elusive is that it requires a deep understanding of music theory, chord construction and knowledge of your instrument. If you are unfamiliar with any of those areas, go get a music theory book and study, study, study. If you want to be a great player, there is no substitute for understanding the intricacies of harmony.

#### The best composers and soloists will use the relative tensions created by the major and minor intervals to create interesting phrases and cadences that draw the listener in and “cast the spell”. The great ones are never constrained by the concept of a major or minor scale or by any particular mode. They understand these things completely, but use their knowledge to break the “rules” and conjure up the magic. USING TWELVE TONES

#### Rarely if ever do you need to stay within one scale or key signature when composing or improvising. You simply need to know what the main key signature is. After that, any of the twelve tones are fair game. Each and every note is available to you. Improvisation and composition aren’t usually taught this way, but it is how all the great composers actually work whether they are aware of it or not!

#### Each and every note performs a sonic function that, if you have explored it, creates a specific effect. That effect might be consonant (harmonious) or dissonant (not so harmonious) but it is available to you as a creative choice nonetheless.

#### In music there are many shades of light and dark and these shades are defined by the various intervals. In my own playing I have found it helpful to distinguish the various major and minor intervals by their relative level of what I will call “bluesiness” or “poppiness” or simply “bluesy” or “poppy”.

#### Generally speaking the major intervals will sound poppy and the minor intervals will sound bluesy. Let’s add a third shade to the mix and call it “mysterious” and another called “jazzy” Now we have four basic shades:

#### 1. Bluesy (minor third, flat five, minor seventh) 2. Poppy (major second, major third, major seventh 3. Mysterious (minor second, minor sixth, major seventh) 4. Jazzy (all intervals can sound jazzy)

#### There are certainly other ways to describe these shades and you can make up your own if you like but these work for me pretty well and should serve our purposes for this discussion.

#### Here is a brief look at how the tones generally function given a central tonality that we either call a key or a chord depending on your scope or granularity. THE ROOT

#### Use the root to resolve your phrases and to establish your “home base”. Like a home, the root is where you ultimately belong. You always leave it, but no matter how strange your wanderings you always come back. THE THIRD

#### The third is what determines the minor or major (bluesy or poppy) tonality. The usual classification is that a major third sounds happy and a minor third sounds sad. Those definitions don’t work so great for me, so I’m just changing those to poppy or bluesy. As you are composing or soloing over a given chord and choose to use the third, try thinking about your choice as bluesy or poppy instead. If you want to sound bluesy or funky or soulful, use the minor third. Just don’t hang on it for too long if the band is playing a major third because it can become dissonant in some situations (unless this is the effect you are trying to achieve). THE FIFTH

#### The fifth of a chord will have very much the same effect as the root. It will sound like home too. You can almost always resolve to the fifth instead of the root and as long as someone else in the band is hitting the root, everything resolves just fine. You could think of it as crashing at your best friends house – it’s just like home! Our good friend the power chord consists of nothing but the root and the fifth for this reason. The fifth is neither bluesy nor poppy so it is often called “perfect”. (Well la di da…) Now if you flat the fifth, you have serious blueseyness. So much so, that it has earned itself the nickname “bluenote”. The flat five is also known as a “tritone” or more nefariously as the “devil’s interval”, it was banned by the church for many years. (How can you ban a note?). It could also be referred

#### to as an augmented (raised) fourth. The flat five is very dissonant and for that reason is used all the time especially in Blues, Jazz, and Heavy Metal. It’s one of my personal favorites. It’s effect is difficult to describe so we’ll stick with “seriously bluesy” for now. THE SEVENTH

#### A minor seventh sounds bluesy and a major seventh sounds poppy. Again, as you are choosing your notes, determine the effect you want to have. As in all these examples, you can use always use both. You can use them in succession or as passing tones, as long as you know what your root is and quickly resolve to it. Using the seventh is a really useful technique in ending a phrase because the tension created by hearing the seventh sets up a strong expectation of resolving to the root. When it is not resolved it leaves a bit of a question in the air. A major seventh has a similar effect and can often sound a bit mysterious, depending on it’s context. Experiment. THE SECOND (OR NINTH)

#### The major second is an interesting interval because it is the same whether you are thinking bluesy or poppy. It is often used as part of a major chord because though it is not the root, it sounds at home when included along with the foundation of the root, the third and the fifth. It tends to brighten up a chord and including it in a chord leaves the listener with a slight feeling of “questioning” or “wondering”. In Jazz, the second (or nine) is often included in seventh chords because it brightens them up so much. You can think of seconds to be like when your best friend stays at your house and brightens things up a bit. You are relaxed and at home, but a little brightened up because your friend is hanging out with you there. When we add a second to a chord and exclude the third however, the urge to resolve to the root is greatly increased. We call is a “suspended” or “sus 2” chord. I suppose this situation is like when your girlfriend (the third) comes over and you decide that your friend has hung around long enough and has worn out his welcome. You want to resolve to be perfectly at home, so you include the third, remove the second, and end up with a perfectly resolved major chord.

#### A minor second creates a dissonant sound that when used as a passing tone provides a mysterious effect. If you want to sound dissonant on purpose use this in combination with the root and you will succeed. If you want to create an eeriness, use it back and forth with your root note. THE FOURTH (OR ELEVENTH)

#### The fourth is also used to create a “suspended” chord. It is both bluesy and poppy or neither bluesy or poppy, because (like the second, fourth and fifth) it functions the same in both the major and minor scales. For this reason it is called “perfect”. The fourth is usually added to a chord in exclusion of the third and this is what creates a suspended chord. Without the third, a suspended chord cannot be considered to be major or minor. THE SIXTH (OR THIRTEENTH)

#### The major sixth sounds poppy and jazzy and the minor sixth sounds downright mysterious. You can use this to great effect. Like the second, the difference between a minor and a major sixth is dramatic. One is extremely harmonious and the other is much more dissonant. CONCLUSION

#### Some artists prefer to stay within a certain context such as Blues or Pop or Rock so they mainly choose specific intervals when composing or soloing. Jazz artists tend to use the entire mix within a single phrase and that is what gives Jazz it’s special flavor of seeming to be all types of music at once, just like America – a melting pot.

#### In summary you can see that there are no wrong notes when it comes to soloing or composing. There are just different notes to choose from that create different effects. If you explore the spectrum of the available sounds you can begin to see scales and chord tones as simply starting points or guides for your music. Then you can go out and confidently break all the rules knowing full well that you can “get in line” whenever you feel you need to.

# 23. MEMORY

### “The true art of memory is the art of attention.” — Samuel Johnson

#### A good memory is the single most important skill a musician can have. If you haven't been blessed with a great memory, don't worry, it is something that can be improved and enhanced. Memories are like muscles and they can be developed with a little work. Recent science reveals that when a thought occurs or an action is performed, the brain constructs electronic pathways that represent that thought or action. If these pathways are traversed regularly, the pathways literally get thicker and fatter, exactly like a real path in the woods does. The more a path is traveled, the wider it gets from the use. This by itself doesn't provide us with any new revelations about how to build a good memory. We already knew that if you want to remember something you should repeat it over and over. What it does provide is a new way of thinking about memory: memory is a very real, very physical thing. It is no longer just something that happens mysteriously in some nebulous concept of mind. It is real and it is pliable, and like a muscle it can be changed and molded by how we use it or how we don't use it. THE IMPORTANCE OF MEMORY IN MUSIC

#### In today's world where any fact or figure can be referenced digitally upon a whim, it is too easy to discount the importance of a good memory. Unlike bankers or salesmen however, musicians can't afford to outsource their memories to computers. We have to remember melodies, chord progressions, key signatures, riffs, phrases, finger positions, scale patterns, rhythmic patterns, lyrics, and the notes several different staves; and we need to recall them instantly as a song progresses in real time. A guitarist or saxophone player can't consult their smart-phone in the middle of a solo to find what note to play next, or respond to what the drummer or piano player just played. A musician's memory must be quick, fluid and changeable.

#### Memory is not a subject that gets enough attention in formal or private music education. Most of the material learned in music schools is based on the concept of music literacy and the ability to read music. Think about it. Why is music written down in the first place? It is written down so we can remember it or to communicate it. Written music is a memory device; but it is also a communications tool. Composers write down music as a way of both remembering it themselves and as a way of sharing it and teaching it to other musicians.

#### What if we made the development of our memory a top priority in our personal music education? We don't need to quit reading or writing music. On the contrary, a developed memory improves our sight reading abilities enormously. I contend that improving your memory is the absolute best way to improve yourself as a musician. Improve the memory, and everything else improves in kind. What if you could hear or read the lyrics to a song and from that moment on be able to recall them instantly at any time? What if you could read through a piece of sheet music once and be forever able to remember what you read? Even if you could not physically execute the music without extensive practice, you would have quite an advantage to say the least. What if you could read about the seven modes of the major scale and remember the whole and half step patterns of them all and apply them at will? What if you could memorize the complex chord and key changes to you favorite Jazz songs after one or two run times through? What if you could listen to a Bach fugue once or twice or even three times and then remember the entire sequence of notes? Do you think you would begin to feel that you were growing as a musician?

#### The above examples are extreme. Only a savant would be capable of such things right? But the fact that some people, savants or not, can do these things is proof that the human mind is capable of these things. We call them special because they are so rare. But they are no more or less human than any one of us. The interesting thing is that most savants didn't have to work hard, if at all, to acquire this ability. They just somehow discovered that they had this strange talent. I have heard stories of people who have been hit by lightning and suddenly can play Classical piano pieces fluently after the fact. Not only do they not know where they got their newfound talent, they wonder where they even got the desire. I recall one story where a person hit by lightning never had an interest in Classical music let alone any piano education, yet they were afterward drawn to the piano and to Classical music and found they had amazing abilities and aptitude to learn it. RAISING OUR EXPECTATIONS FOR MEMORY

#### In the Ray Bradbury science fiction novel Fahrenheit 451 firemen, instead of putting out fires as we know them to do, are employed by a corrupt government to seek out and burn every book in the country. People begin hiding books away and memorizing them word for word, before they can be burned, as a way to preserve the knowledge, wisdom and imagination contained in the books. They begin taking as their own names, the title of the books. Two people meeting on the street might say to each other, "Hello Wizard of Oz" and "Hi Sea Wolf". The memory of the

#### citizens was one thing that the corrupt government could not take away. Even as the citizens were thrown in jail for their crimes, the stories lived on in their memories and were transfered to others orally for safe keeping.

#### Can you imagine committing a 400 page novel to memory? Fahrenheit 451 is a science fiction novel, but it is not an extreme exaggeration. In the days before books or paper (or even papyrus) were invented neither stories nor musical compositions were written down. There was nothing to write them on if you wanted to other than stone or sand or clay. This meant that music and stories were stored in people's memories. Epic poems like the Iliad and the Odyssey were passed orally from person to person for hundreds of years before it was technologically possible to write them down, and they are each hundreds of pages in length! The Bradbury science fiction example demonstrates the human desire and potential need for such memory; and the Homer example is a real world, historical event that demonstrates that humans are capable of remembering much more information than our culture currently requires of us.

#### What does all this mean for us as musicians seeking to improve our memories? It means we can and should raise the bar when it comes to what we are capable of. We have proof that memory can be expanded, built, developed and strengthened just like a muscle or an idea. It means we can be confident that if we open our imaginations to the new possibilities we can find new, or rediscover ancient ways of remembering. MEMORY TECHNIQUE

#### Once upon a time there was a Greek goddess named "Mnemosyne" (pronounced nem-o-seen). She was the goddess of remembrance. The people worshiped her because they saw that the ability to remember brought them great confidence, power, and fortune. They may also have noticed how a lack of memory often brought great misfortune and strife. As the years passed, people began inventing new ways to remember things in order to attain such power and good fortune and avoid misfortune and strife. They soon began calling these techniques "mnemonic devices". MNEMONIC DEVICES

#### A mnemonic device is simply a technique for enhancing or stimulating memory. There are many ways to create mnemonic devices. Some have been passed down for generations, others we make up on the spot when we need to remember what we need to pick up at the grocery store, or the names of your neighbors seven cats.

#### Two popular music related examples are the ways we are taught to remember the names of the lines and spaces on the musical staff. The G Clef , which is used for instruments such as guitar, violin, and the right hand side of the piano, consists of four spaces and five lines. We remember the spaces by the convenient fact that, from bottom to top, the letters assigned to the spaces spell the word FACE. We remember the names of the lines on the staff with a slightly different technique. Since they don't spell out a word, we assign a word that begins with each letter and create a memorable sentence: Every Good Boy Does Fine. The first letter of every word describes the name of each line of the staff. EGBDF.

#### These are good examples of mnemonic techniques that use the familiarity of language as the central device for remembering. But there are other ways. The ancient Greeks are said to have used a visual technique where they imagined objects representing things they wanted to remember placed in a room they were very familiar with. The more bizarre the placement of the object the better. When they wanted to recall the data, they would simply imagine themselves walking through the room, observing the items they needed to recall. The familiarity of the room and the strangeness of the placement of the items somehow burns the information into memory much better than does rote memorization. TYPES OF MEMORY

#### We may be able to apply mnemonic techniques to some of our fact or list based memorization tasks but a memory of the music itself is not much like remembering lists of items. Musical memory has more to do with muscle memory or the visual patterns of our fingers on our instruments or the recalling of a series of actual sounds, melodies and chord progressions. MUSCLE MEMORY

#### Muscle memory is built on frequent and consistent repetition of movement that results when we produce notes on our instrument or sing. This type of memory is developed over time. Muscle memory is the most reliable kind of memory because its retrieval seems to be completely automatic. It is like simply turning on a machine in your mind that recalls the notes and triggers the synapses in you brain. These synapses in turn trigger the muscles and tendons required to play the notes. The muscle memory contains not just the notes but all of the information about rhythm, tempo and dynamics as well.

#### VISUAL PATTERNS Much of what we remember about how to play a piece of music is not so much related to the sound of the notes

#### themselves or to automatic muscle memory, but comes from the visual recollection of the patterns our fingers form on the instrument. For example, piano players will remember the general “shape” of a piece by the forms their hands take over this or that section of the music. The chords and arpeggios take on a visual pattern that can be conjured up in the mind and recalled. The same is true for guitar players who memorize chords, scales and melodies by relating them to geometrical shapes on the guitar’s fretboard. When we practice visualization techniques we are using these sight based patterns as our link to remembering the music and triggering the muscle memory, not necessarily the sound of the music itself. SOUND BASED MEMORY

#### Memory based on the actual sound of the music is what we are trying to access when we ask someone to “hum a few bars” so we can recall a song. We have probably all experienced moments when even though you knew a particular song extremely well, just hearing or thinking about the songs name was not enough to trigger memories of how to play it. All it would take is for someone to sing the opening notes and the entire tune would immediately came flooding back instantly. ATTENTION: LET THE MUSIC REMEMBER YOU

#### It seems the best way to retain music in memory is to use all these various ways in combination. When we practice we put them to use both consciously and subconsciously. When we practice we are paying attention to the music, and that attention is the key to memory.

#### Think about a piece of music that you have memorized extremely well and ask yourself how it is that you came to remember it so well? The first thing that comes to your mind is that you “just know it”. It is this feeling of “just knowing” that is what we are aiming for in our remembering. But think a bit more about it. Most likely you didn’t actively try to remember it, and you probably didn’t use any sort of trick or gimmick to force your memory. You probably practiced it a lot because you loved the music and spent lots of quality time with it. You simply had given the song so much attention it has become part of who you are. You remember it not just with your mind but in your fingers and toes and heart and soul. You couldn’t forget that song if you tried.

#### This tells us that a good memory is not achieved with gimmicks for recalling things that you carelessly stuff into dusty pigeon holes in your brain. Good memory is earned by putting your attention on the music long enough and consistently enough that is seeps into your physical body as well as your spiritual essence. The best way to learn music is to love it and attend to it. If you do this, the music will take on a life of its own and you won’t have to worry about remembering it, it will remember you.

### MIDNIGHT ARPEGGIOS

#### This book looks beyond the technicalities of theory and into the mental and spiritual processes we go through in

#### practicing and in becoming musical human beings. You will find a collection of 23 short, original essays on music that are inspired, informal, and illustrated with classic artwork. Together, they examine a wide range of musical concepts as inspired by the thoughts of great musicians, philosophers, poets and scientists.

#### GROW, TEACH, REPEAT: THE ART OF LEARNING & TEACHING GUITAR An in depth guide for guitar teachers and students, but also informative for private music instructors of all kinds.

#### Using a combination of personal stories and unique insight on instructional methods, the author explores the cycle of teaching and learning that is the at the core of a career in instruction and performance. You will find advice on how to begin and maintain a private teaching business, build a bond with students and how to solve musical challenges by changing the way you look at them. This is not a collection of musical exercises; it is a treasure chest of expert advice that will inspire continued growth and creativity in your career as teacher and life as a student.
