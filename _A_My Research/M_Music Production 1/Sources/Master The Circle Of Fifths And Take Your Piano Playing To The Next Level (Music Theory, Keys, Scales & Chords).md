# Keys, Sharps, Flats and Intervals

#### First of all, to make things clear from the start, and to set a proper foundation, let's quickly define what a key is. A key is a set of notes that correspond to a particular scale. It's like a song's home. It tells you whether there are sharps or flats in a song and how many there are. It consists of the major or minor scale around which a piece of music revolves. If a song is in a major key, it is based on a major scale. If it is in a minor key, it is based on a minor scale.

#### Let's say a song is in the key of C major. This means that the song is based on the seven notes of the C major scale. These notes are C D E F G A B. This means that the fundamental notes that make up the songs' melody, chords, bassline and so on, are all based on that group of seven notes, C D E F G A B.

#### Secondly, what are sharps and flats? Simply put, a sharp raises a note, while a flat lowers a note. When a note is sharpened, its pitch is raised by a half step. When a note is flattened, its pitch is lowered by a half step. A half step is the shortest interval in music. For example, C to C sharp is a half step. E to F is also a half step and we can see this very clearly on the piano, since the keys are next to each other.

#### Thirdly, what is an interval of a fifth? It simply means that there are five letters from one note to the next. For example, C to G is an interval of a fifth. Why? Because moving in alphabetical order, there are five letters, C, D, E, F and G from C to G. C to D would be an interval of a second, C to E is an interval of a third, and C to F is an interval of a fourth.

#### G to D is an interval of a fifth. How do we know this? Just count the letters. The letters are G, A, B, C and D. How many letters? Five! It's that simple.

#### Okay. Now that we understand what keys, sharps, flats and intervals are, we're ready to move on.

# The Circle of Fifths Explained

#### Let's take a look at the circle of fifths. What is this "crazy" circle about? You will see that it's not crazy at all. Let's do this.

#### Here’s a simple, basic diagram of the circle of fifths.

#### Right Side of the Circle of Fifths

#### Take a look at the right side of the circle of fifths. You will see the letters move clockwise from C to G, D, A, E, B, F# and C#. What is this about? All that is happening is that the keys are moving in fifths. C to G is an interval of a fifth as we have seen, G to D is an interval of a fifth, and the same applies for D to A, A to E, E to B, B to F# and F# to C#. There are five letters from one note to another for each of these intervals.

#### We already saw that there are five letters from C to G, and G to D. Let's do this for D to A. The letters are D, E, F, G and A. How many letters? Five! Let's try A to E. The letters are A, B, C, D and E. How many letters? Five! It's that simple. This is what an interval of a fifth is.

#### But there is more to it, as we shall see with the interval of B to F#. The letters are B, C, D, E and F. But why did we say F# and not F? I want you to do some counting on your piano keyboard. If you count the keys from B to F#, you will notice that there is a total of 8 keys. You must have a total of 8 keys (black and white) in an interval of a fifth. Another way to look at it is to say that you must have a total of 7 half steps from one key to another to come up with a fifth. Try it out on piano and you will see. Compare B to F# with C to G and you will notice that it's the same number of keys. Every key on the circle of fifths is a fifth apart.

#### I want you to memorize the keys moving clockwise on the circle of fifths. These keys are C G D A E B F# C#.

#### Now here's what's interesting about the circle of fifths. As you move clockwise (or right) each key has one sharp more than the other. Let's start with the key of C. The key of C has no sharps and flats. Its notes are C D E F G A B. The key after C on the circle of fifths is G. G has one sharp. G is followed by D. The key of D has two sharps. The key of D is followed by A. A has three sharps. Then E has four sharps, B

#### has five sharps, F# has 6 sharps and C# has 7 sharps. Every note in C# is sharp. So every time you move to another key, that key has one more sharp in its scale. Stay with me and it will become clearer and clearer to you. Don't worry about what the sharps are for now. All you need to know for now is the number of sharps in each key.

#### Left Side of the Circle of Fifths

#### So now that we have looked at the right side of the circle of fifths, let's now look at the left side. Let's now move counter-clockwise (anti-clockwise). What are the keys on the left side? The keys, as can be seen in the previous diagram, starting with C, are C F Bb Eb Ab Db Gb Cb. As you move left or counter-clockwise, you add a flat each time. This means that C has no flats, F has one flat, Bb has two flats, Eb has three flats, Ab has four flats, Db has five flats, Gb has six flats and Cb has seven flats.

#### I want you to memorize the pattern of keys moving counter clockwise on the circle of fifths. This will prove to be so handy for you as a musician, you won't believe! Very soon it will become part of you. In other words it will be second nature. You will have it fully grasped. Just keep practicing and commit it to memory.

#### So how did we come up with the keys, C F Bb Eb Ab Db Gb Cb on the left side of the circle of fifths? We simply moved in fifths. Except that moving counter-clockwise, the fifth is from the key that follows a key to the key that precedes it. Let me explain with some examples. After C, we got F. This is because F to C is a fifth. After F, we got Bb. This is because Bb to F is a fifth. After Bb, moving counterclockwise, the next key is Eb. Eb to Bb is an interval of a fifth. On your piano keyboard, you have to count 8 keys moving backwards, including the one you're on.

#### Enharmonic Equivalents

#### Some keys on the circle of fifths are enharmonic equivalents. This means that while they are named differently, they are played by the same keys on your instrument. For example, C# and Db are enharmonic equivalents. On piano, the same key is used to play these notes. On guitar it's the same. No matter what instrument, it's the same. These keys are different on paper, but if a song is written in the key of C# or Db, they will sound the same when played. They are just written differently in music notation. Other enharmonic equivalents are B and Cb, and F# and Gb.

# Major Scales and the Circle of Fifths

#### We saw that the order of sharps are F# C# G# D# A# E# B#, and the order of flats are Bb Eb Ab Db Gb Cb Fb. We can use these to form major scales.

#### Every major scale makes use of the entire musical alphabet. In other words, you must have the letters, A B C D E F G in every major scale. The only difference between major scales is the fact that some of these notes may be sharp or flat.

#### If you are asked to form a major scale, you can basically write all the letters first of all, and afterwards add the sharps or flats as necessary. Let's try this with the G major scale.

#### Starting on G, the letters are G A B C D E F. But according to our circle of fifths diagram, G major has one sharp, and that one sharp is F#. So all we do now is change F to F#. It's that simple. The notes of the G major scale are G A B C D E F#.

#### Let's try a key with 5 sharps. What is that key? The answer is B. This means that 5 of the 7 notes in the B major scale are sharp. Let's write the letters. They are B C D E F G A. What notes should we sharpen? Well according to the order of sharps, it has to be F#, C#, G#, D# and A#. So the correct notes for the B major scale would have to be B C# D# E F# G# A#.

#### This is fun, so let's try the key that has three flats. That key is Eb. Let's write the seven letters, E F G A B C D. The order of flats is Bb Eb Ab Db Gb Cb Fb. This means that the three flats in the key of Eb are Bb, Eb and Ab. Therefore the notes of the Eb major scale have to be Eb F G Ab Bb C D. We kept every other note natural except B, E and A. B, E and A became Bb, Eb and Ab.

#### When forming a major scale, every letter of the alphabet must be used and you can only use each letter once. For instance, the notes of the D major scale are D E F# G A B C#. Notice how we never said Gb, even if the notes, Gb and F# are played by the same key on piano, or the same fret and string on guitar. We couldn't say Gb again because we already used the note, G. Likewise, we couldn't say Db, because there is a D in the scale already. The letters must move alphabetically and can’t be repeated.

#### I want you to practice forming major scales. Write down all the letters, then change notes to sharps or flats as indicated by the circle of fifths and the order of sharps and flats.

# How to Form Major Scales

#### There is another way to form major scales. We can form major scales using the formula, W W H W W W H. It stands for whole step - whole step - half step - whole step - whole step - whole step - half step.

#### What does this mean?

#### A half step (also called a semitone) is the distance between one key and the key that is next to it. For example, C to C sharp (C#) is a half step. E to F is a half step. Two half steps equal one whole step (also called a whole tone or tone). Therefore C to D is a whole step. D to E is a whole step. A whole step skips a key while a half step doesn't.

#### On a guitar, if you move from one fret to the next fret, that is a half step. If you skip a fret, it's a whole step.

#### Every major key has 7 diatonic scale degrees. Take a look at the key that represents the note, C on your piano keyboard. C to B is a total of 7 diatonic scale degrees. But from C to C, there are actually 12 semitones or half steps.

#### For every key, you must pick seven scale degrees as suggested by the formula, W W H W W W H.

#### Since we know what half steps and whole steps are and we know our formula for forming a major scale, we can literally start on any key on the piano and form a major scale.

#### Here's a piano keyboard diagram (with natural notes, sharps and flats included).

#### Let's start on C and form a C major scale using the formula W W H W W W H. Starting on C, we move one whole step to D, a whole step to E, a half step to F, a whole step to G, a whole step to A, a whole step to B and a half step to C. Therefore, the notes of the C major scale are C D E F G A B C.

#### Let's try this with G. If we start on G, we move one whole step to A, a whole step to B, a half step to C, a whole step to D, a whole step to E, a whole step to F# and a half step to G. The notes of the G major scale are G A B C D E F# G. Notice how we played F# instead of F, as suggested by the major scale formula.

#### Let's try one more with D. From D, we move one whole step to E, a whole step to F#, a half step to G, a whole step to A, a whole step to B, a whole step to C# and a half step to D. The notes of the D major scale are D E F# G A B C# D. You would have noticed the two sharps in the D major scale, F# and C#.

# How to Form Major, Minor and Diminished Chords

#### Now that you understand how scales are formed for each major key, we now move to chords. You can use every note of the major scale as a root note or starting note for a chord. In other words, you can form seven triad chords based on each scale degree.

#### Let's learn how to form these triad chords first of all. They are major, minor and diminished chords.

#### How to Form a Major Chord

#### Major chords are formed by combining the first, third and fifth notes of the major scale. In other words, you combine the root, major third and perfect fifth.

#### The root is the note upon which the chord is based. It is the first note in the scale. The major third is the third note in the major scale. The perfect fifth is the fifth note in the major scale.

#### Let's say you want to form the chord, C major. You simply take the C major scale, C D E F G A B, combine the root note, C, the major third, E and the perfect fifth, G. C, E and G are the notes of the C major chord.

#### Let's say you want to form the chord, F major, you simply start with the F major scale, F G A Bb C D E, combine the root note, F, the major third, A, and the perfect fifth, C. F, A and C are the notes of the F major chord.

#### Here’s one more example. To form the G major chord, simply take the G major scale, G A B C D E F#, combine the root note, G, the major third, B, and the perfect fifth, D. G, B and D are the notes of the G major chord.

#### Another way to form major chords is by using half steps. The formula is R + 4HS + 3 HS (root plus 4 half steps plus 3 half steps). In other words, you can start on any note, hold that note, then add the key that is four keys higher on your piano, plus the key that is three keys higher.

#### How to Form a Minor Chord

#### The minor chord consists of a root, a minor third and a perfect fifth. For example, the chord, A minor consists of the root note, A, a minor third, C and a perfect fifth, E. The minor third is 3 half steps above the root note.

#### You can form a minor chord by using half steps (semitones). The formula for forming a minor chord is R + 3HS + 4 HS (root plus 3 half steps + 4 half steps). What does this mean? All it means is that to form a minor chord, you can start with the root note of the chord (the note which gives the chord its name), play the key that is 3 half steps higher, then the key that is 4 half steps higher. For instance, to form the chord, C minor, you can start on C, then play Eb, since that note is 3 half steps higher, then move 4 half steps higher from Eb and play G.

#### You can think of a minor chord in this way. Simply play a major chord and play the middle note of the chord (in root position) a half step lower. In other words, play the key that is to the immediate left of the major third instead. Change the major third into a minor third or flat third. A minor third is a half step lower than a major third.

#### How to Form a Diminished Chord

#### To form a diminished chord, combine the root, flat third and flat fifth of the major scale. For example, to form a B diminished chord, combine the root note, B, the flat third, D and the flat fifth, F. Therefore, the notes of the B diminished chord are B-D-F.

#### To build a diminished chord, you can simply take a major chord and flatten the third and fifth notes of the chord by a half step. For instance, to form an F diminished chord, you can take the F chord with the notes F-A-C and flatten A and C by a half step, resulting in F-Ab-Cb or an F diminished chord.

# Triad Chord Patterns in Major Keys

#### We've already taken a look at scales in every key. You can use every note of each major scale as a root note or starting note for a chord.

#### In music theory, Roman numerals are used to indicate each note's position in the scale. For a major scale, these Roman numerals are I ii iii IV V vi vii°. For example, for the C major scale, the Roman numerals are as follows. C - I, D - ii, E - iii, F - IV, G - V, A - vi, and B - vii°.

#### Notice that some Roman numerals are in capitals and some are lower case. Why is this? First of all the pattern of chords for a major key or scale are major - minor - minor - major - major - minor -diminished. (M m m M M m d.) For major chords, the Roman numerals are in capital letters, and for minor and diminished chords, you use lower case. For the seventh chord, there's a small circle, °, to indicate that it's a diminished chord.

#### Following the chord pattern, major - minor - minor - major - major - minor - diminished, the chords in the key of C major are C major - D minor, E minor - F major - G major - A minor - B diminished. In other words, for the key of C major, the I chord is C major, the ii chord is D minor, the iii chord is E minor, the IV chord is F major, the V chord is G major, the vi chord is A minor and the vii° chord is B diminished.

#### The following diagram lists major keys and the 7 chords in each of these keys.

#### The I, IV and V chords are called primary chords. These are the most important chords in any major

#### key and they are all major chords. You can play many songs using primary chords only. When you combine them, they make use of all the notes of the major scale.

#### I want you to play the seven chords in the key of C major on your keyboard. You will notice that they all make use of the notes of the C major scale only. In other words, only white keys are used to form all these chords.

#### Let's form chords for another major key using the pattern, major - minor - minor - major - major - minor -diminished. Let's do this with the key of D major. On what side of the circle of fifths will you find D major? The answer is the right side. How many sharps are in the key of D major? The answer is two sharps. What are these sharps? The answer is F# and C#. So what are the notes of the D major scale? The notes have to be D E F# G A B C#. Using the pattern, the chords are therefore, D major, E minor, F# minor, G major, A major, B minor and C# diminished.

# How to Form Extended Chords

#### In addition to three note chords (or triads), you can form extended chords that contain four notes, based on the major scale. These are major 7th, minor 7th, dominant 7th and minor seventh flat 5 chords. Before we apply them to the major scale, let's learn how they are formed.

#### How to form a major seventh chord

#### To form a major 7th chord on piano, you combine the root, major third, perfect fifth and major 7th of the major scale. A total of four notes. For example, the notes of a C major seventh chord are C-E-G-B. This chord is really a major chord with an added major 7th note. Pretty straightforward. How is it written? The symbols used for this type of chord are M7 or maj 7. For instance C major seventh could be written as CM7 or Cmaj7. D major seventh could be written as DM7 or Dmaj7.

#### Let's take a random scale, the E major scale for example. Its notes are E-F#-G#-A-B-C#-D#-E. To form an Emaj7 chord, combine the 1st tone of the scale, E, with the 3rd tone, G#, the 5th tone, B and the 7th tone, D#. E-G#-B-D# are the notes of the E major seventh chord.

#### How to form a minor seventh chord

#### Let's now take a look at the minor seventh chord. Since, you already know how to play a minor chord, all you have to do now is add one more note to it. That note is the flat 7th note of the major scale.

#### To build a minor 7th chord, you combine the root, minor third, fifth and flat seventh of the major scale. The chord spelling/formula relative to the major scale is 1-b3-5-b7.

#### Let's form a C minor seventh chord. We know that the notes of the C major scale are C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C. The third note in that scale is E. This is a major third. To change the major third to a minor third, you must play the note a half step lower. Change E to Eb. The 7th note in this scale is B. Therefore, the flat 7th note is Bb. You simply play the 7th note a half step lower on your keyboard. You play the key that is to the immediate left of B. C-Eb-G-Bb is a C minor 7th chord.

#### This chord is represented by the symbols “-7”, “m7” or “min7”. For example, A minor seventh can be written A-7, Am7 or Amin7. D minor seventh can be written D-7, Dm7 or Dmin7.

# Minor Keys and Scales and the Circle of Fifths

#### Throughout the previous chapters of this book, we've been looking at major keys and major scales. Right now, we will take a look at minor keys and scales. Actually, once you understand major scales, it is very easy to understand the minor ones, as we shall see.

#### Let's take a look at the circle of fifths once again. This time we include minor keys.

#### We saw at the start of this book, that starting with C and moving clockwise tells us the keys containing sharps, while moving counter clockwise from C tells us the keys containing flats. If we start on C and move clockwise, the keys move in the order, C G D A E B F# C#. If we start on C and move counter clockwise, the keys move in the order, C F Bb Eb Ab Db Gb Cb.

#### We will now talk about the relative minor. Each major key has what is known as a relative minor, built on the 6th degree of the major scale. A major key and its relative minor consists of the same notes. For example, the notes of the C major scale are C D E F G A B C. The 6th degree of the C major scale is A. Hence, the relative minor of C major is A minor. The notes of the A minor scale are the same as that of the C major scale. The notes of the A minor scale are A B C D E F G A. The only difference is the starting note.

#### How about the F major scale and its relative minor? The notes of the F major scale, as we have seen before are F G A Bb C D E F. To find the relative minor, go to the 6th degree of the scale, D. Starting on D, move to E, F, G, A, Bb C. D E F G A Bb C are the notes of the D minor scale. Clearly, the relative minor of F major is D minor.

#### Here’s another example: The notes of the G major scale are G A B C D E F# G. The 6th degree of the scale is E. Hence, the relative minor of the G major scale is E minor. The notes of the E minor scale are E F# G A B C D E. Once again, we see that the notes are identical, and the only difference is the root note of the scale.

#### A major scale and its relative minor has the same key signature. For instance there are no sharps or flats in the C major key signature and likewise, there are no sharps or flats in the A minor key signature. There

#### is one sharp in the G major key signature and similarly, there is one sharp in the E minor key signature. There is one flat in the F major key signature and the same applies to D minor.

#### If you know your major scales, it's very easy to form their relative minors. Simply start on the 6th degree of the major scale.

#### On the right side of the circle of fifths, after the A minor key, minor keys containing sharps move in the order Em, Bm, F#m, C#m, G#m. On the left side, after the key of A minor, minor keys containing flats move in the order, Dm, Gm, Cm, Fm, Bbm, Ebm.

#### Minor Scale Formula – How to Form a Minor Scale

#### Natural minor scales follow a simple formula. That formula is whole - half - whole - whole - half - whole -whole or W H W W H W W. As we saw earlier, "whole" stands for whole step and "half" stands for half step. So in other words, the formula for forming a minor scale is whole step - half step - whole step - whole step - half step - whole step - whole step.

#### With this formula, we can start on any note and form a minor scale.

#### Let's start on A. We are going to form an A minor scale. Starting on A, let's move a whole step higher. This takes us to B. Let's move a half step higher. This takes us to C. Following our minor scale formula, let's move another whole step higher. This takes us to D. From D, we move a whole step higher. This takes us to E. From E, we move a half step higher. This takes us to F. From F, we move a whole step higher. This takes us to G. From G we move another whole step higher and this takes us to A. A-B-C-D-E-F-G-A. There we have it. The A natural minor scale. All we did was follow the formula, W-H-W-W-H-W-W and we came up with an A minor scale.

#### Let's try a D minor scale. Starting on D, move one whole step to E, a half step to F, a whole step to G, a whole step to A, a half step to Bb, a whole step to C and a whole step to D. D E F G A Bb C D are the notes of the D minor scale, the relative minor of F major.

# Minor Key Chord Patterns

#### Now that you know the notes of the minor scale, similar to the major scale, you can form chords based on every note. And it is very easy to do. Actually, the chords of a major key are the same as the chords of its relative minor. The only difference is the position of the chords on the scale.

#### For example, the chords of the key of C major, as we have seen are C major, D minor, E minor, F major, G major, A minor and B diminished. The relative minor of C major is A minor. So all we do is start on A minor, and the chords in the key of A minor are A minor, B diminished, C major, D minor, E minor, F major and G major. These are the same chords, as can be clearly seen, except that the major and minor keys start with different chords.

#### While major keys follow the pattern, major - minor - minor - major - major - minor - diminished, the pattern for minor keys is minor - diminished - major - minor - minor - major - major.

#### Another thing to notice, is that the capital letters and lower case letters on the Roman numerals are different. For a major key, it's I ii iii IV V vi vii°. But for a minor key, its, i ii° III iv v VI VII. Major chords are always in capital letters, while minor and diminished chords are always in lower case.
