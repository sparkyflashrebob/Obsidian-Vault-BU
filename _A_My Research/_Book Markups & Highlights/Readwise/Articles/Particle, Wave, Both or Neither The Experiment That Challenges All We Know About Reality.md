## Highlights
- Before the measurement — in this case, detection by the photographic plate — the mathematics says the particle exists in a superposition of states ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ykxtwzq3x4em5fmdga78fn))
- in a sense, it has taken both paths, through the right slit and the left. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ykzjed4971z45ha9nnzkee))
- Standard quantum mechanics says that the wavefunction ‘collapses’ when measured, and that the act of observation in some way precipitates that collapse. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2yky9za6ntcefr17qwtm6mw))
- Before this, the photon has a finite probability of being found in many different regions, but on measurement, the wavefunction peaks at the location in which the photon appears (the probability there equals 1) and is nullified everywhere else (probability equals 0). ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ykzsjmggebz45ewx86mfnv))
- f you can determine which path the photon took on its way to the detector, it acts like a particle that does indeed go through one slit or the other: the interference pattern disappears. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym0793jwmb5ep0md03px8s))
- But if you cannot glean this ‘which-way’ information, the photon acts like a wave. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym0p3fczj9w7anghhn9kvx))
- Whenever there are two or more ways for a photon — or, indeed, any quantum object — to get to a final state, quantum interference occurs. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym0s7a3qgdqmegeakv4x4z))
- But to generate interference, something has to go through — or at least interact in some way with — both slits. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym14dr7c8jjh6nn86e08fy))
- But you can explain the interference pattern if you consider the wavefunction to be real. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym1m0f4a5d6nwmsj4xdytp))
- This creates its own problems. Imagine a real wavefunction that spreads for kilometres and kilometres before an observer detects the photon. At this point, the wavefunction peaks at the photon’s location, and simultaneously drops to zero everywhere else — over a large, macroscopic distance. This suggests a kind of instantaneous, non-local influence that bothered Einstein no end. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym2adv7getqywsqr1vt2p5))
- One can avoid this with interpretations of quantum theory that don’t collapse the wavefunction, but that opens other cans of worms. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym2hds18xe5fwnrbpz5wvy))
- many-worlds interpretation ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym2tdx5cngr224p0ep4e2e))
- This argues that every possible event — in the case of the double slit, a particle going through the left and the right slit — happens, each in its own world. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym3828vehyedc23gr26mrk))
- no collapse ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym3dy302zna8emtba634pz))
- measurement simply reveals the state of the quantum system in that world. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h2ym3k1s0yjq25rt3x4wdabg))
- The de Broglie–Bohm theory, named after quantum pioneers Louis de Broglie and David Bohm, provides another alternative. It says that particles exist with definite positions and momenta, but are guided by an all-encompassing, invisible ‘pilot’ wave, and it’s this wave that goes through both slits. The most profound implication of this theory, that everything is linked to everything else in the Universe by the underlying pilot wave, is one many physicists have trouble accepting.
