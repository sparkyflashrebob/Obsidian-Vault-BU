# Ariyavaṃsa Sutta (AN 4.28) — The Four Noble Lineages

> *"Monks, these four noble lineages are primeval, of long standing, traditional, ancient, unadulterated and never before adulterated; they are not being rejected, will not be rejected, and are unblamed by wise ascetics and brahmins."*  
> — ***Ariyavaṃsa Sutta*** (AN 4.28)

> *"When struck by an arrow, the untrained person sorrows, grieves, and laments, beating their breast and becoming distraught. Thus they feel two feelings—a bodily one and a mental one. It is as if a man were pierced by an arrow, and following that, immediately pierced by a second arrow."*  
> — ***Sallatha Sutta*** (SN 36.6)

---

## Sutta Opening & Contextual Metadata

### 1. Location
The discourse was delivered at **Sāvatthī**, the capital city of the Kosalan kingdom, in the tranquil sanctuary of **Jeta’s Grove (*Jetavana*)**, within the monastery built and offered by the wealthy merchant **Anāthapiṇḍika**. Jeta’s Grove served as the primary monastic seat during the Buddha’s latter decades, functioning as a vibrant intersection between monastic renunciation and the surrounding civic life.

### 2. Circumstances
The Buddha addresses the monastic Saṅgha to articulate the eternal ancestral pedigree of the Noble Ones (*ariyavaṃsā*). In ancient Indian society, social identity, respect, and hereditary privileges were governed strictly by bloodlines, clanship (*gotta*), and Vedic ancestry (*vaṃsa*). Individuals derived their sense of legitimacy, entitlement, and status from physical lineage.

In this discourse, the Buddha radically inverts this cultural paradigm by defining the true spiritual genealogy of the Awakened Ones. The *ariyavaṃsā* are not passed down through biological birth, nor are they subject to historical obsolescence or cultural fads. The Buddha declares them to be *aggaññā* (primeval), *rattaññū* (of long standing), *vaṃsaññū* (traditional), *porāṇā* (ancient), *asaṅkiṇṇā* (unadulterated), and *appaṭikuṭṭhā* (unblemished and blameless). 

Historically, this sutta attained immense renown. Two centuries after the Buddha’s Parinibbāna, Emperor **Ashoka the Great** engraved his famous **Bhabru (Bairat) Rock Edict**, specifically selecting seven foundational Dhamma texts for regular, mandatory study by monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen. The first and most prominent text listed in Ashoka’s edict is the *Aliyavāsa*—the Ariyavaṃsa Sutta—underscoring its role as the quintessential moral and contemplative bedrock of early Buddhism.

### 3. People Involved
* **The Buddha (Bhagavān)**: The awakened progenitor of the Noble Sangha, revealing the four timeless hallmarks of noble monastic and contemplative inheritance.
* **The Bhikkhu Saṅgha**: The assembly of resident and wandering monastics who receive the instruction, challenged to measure their conduct and mental orientation against these ancient standards.
* **Wise Ascetics and Brahmins (*Viññū Samaṇa-Brāhmaṇā*)**: Referenced by the Buddha as objective, discerning spiritual witnesses who across all eras find no ground to criticize or fault these four lineages.
* **The Fourfold Assembly (*Parisa*) Across Time**: Both monastic renunciates and householders who walk the noble path, applying these principles to navigate material requisites and emotional reactivity.

### 4. Theme of the Dharma Talk
The discourse expounds **The Fourfold Heritage of the Noble Ones (*Ariyavaṃsa*)**:
1. **Radical Contentment with Requisites (*Itarītara-Santuṭṭhi*)**: Contentment with whatever clothing, almsfood, and shelter are available, using them with clear comprehension of their danger and escape.
2. **Inner Devotion to Cultivation and Relinquishment (*Bhāvanārāmatā & Pahānārāmatā*)**: Finding genuine joy and delight in developing wholesome mental qualities and letting go of unwholesome states.
3. **The Elimination of Spiritual Conceit**: Maintaining profound humility—neither exalting oneself nor disparaging others on account of one’s simplicity or meditative dedication.
4. **Mastery Over Emotional Polarities (*Arati-Rati-Sahana*)**: Conquering both discontent (*arati*) and sensual intoxication (*rati*), allowing the heart to remain unshakable in all directions and environments.

### 5. Condensed Version of the Teachings
The Buddha identifies four enduring noble lineages that have sustained the Awakened Ones across all time:

1. **Contentment with Whatever Robe (*Cīvara-Santuṭṭhi*)**: A practitioner is content with whatever robe is received, praises contentment, does not commit improper or undignified acts to secure clothing, does not fret or worry when clothing is lacking, and when receiving robes, wears them untied, uninfatuated, seeing the danger, and understanding the escape. Furthermore, they do not exalt themselves or look down upon others because of their contentment.
2. **Contentment with Whatever Almsfood (*Piṇḍapāta-Santuṭṭhi*)**: A practitioner is content with whatever food is received, praises contentment, avoids unseemly grasping, does not agitate when food is coarse or scarce, and partakes of alms without greed or clinging, neither elevating themselves nor disparaging others.
3. **Contentment with Whatever Lodging (*Senāsana-Santuṭṭhi*)**: A practitioner is content with whatever shelter or resting place is provided—whether an empty hut, a root of a tree, or an open hillside. They do not scheme for upgrades, do not fret when dwellings are rudimentary, and dwell without clinging, avoiding comparison or pride.
4. **Delight in Meditation and Abandoning (*Bhāvanārāmatā & Pahānārāmatā*)**: A practitioner finds joy in cultivating the mind (*bhāvanārāmo hoti bhāvanārato*) and finds joy in relinquishing unwholesome states (*pahānārāmo hoti pahānarato*). They do not turn this spiritual dedication into a badge of vanity or contempt for others.

A person established in these four lineages conquers **discontent (*arati*)** and **delight (*rati*)** wherever they live—east, west, north, or south. Like a refined coin of pure Jambu gold (*nekkhaṃ jambonadasseva*), their heart is blameless, praised by discerning peers, devas, and the highest gods.

## Expanded Version of the Teachings Raised

### Section 1: The Doctrinal Framework of the Four Lineages

In classical Indian society, belonging to an aristocratic clan (*khattiya-vaṃsa*) or priestly lineage (*brāhmaṇa-vaṃsa*) conferred immediate worldly authority. Yet biological descent provided zero protection against greed, hatred, and delusion. The Buddha redefines lineage from a biological accident into an **internal spiritual disposition**.

To enter the family of the Noble Ones is to inherit four specific behavioral and psychological traits. Notice the structural symmetry: the first three lineages govern the **external relationship to material requisites**, while the fourth lineage governs the **internal cultivation of the mind**.

| Noble Lineage (*Ariyavaṃsa*) | Pāli Formulation | Domain | Core Psychological Practice | Shadow / Pitfall Avoided |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Contentment with Robes** | *Cīvarena santuṭṭho itarītarena* | Physical Requisite (Clothing / Protection) | Non-infatuation (*amucchito*), seeing danger (*ādīnavadassāvī*), knowing escape (*nissaraṇapañño*) | Anxiety over appearance, hoarding, soliciting upgrades, self-righteous austerity |
| **2. Contentment with Almsfood** | *Piṇḍapātena santuṭṭho itarītarena* | Physical Requisite (Nourishment) | Partaking untied (*agatitho*), freedom from greed, receiving coarse or fine without agitation | Culinary fussiness, manipulation of donors, gluttony, complaining |
| **3. Contentment with Lodging** | *Senāsanena santuṭṭho itarītarena* | Physical Requisite (Shelter / Environment) | Residing peacefully anywhere, no territorial grasping or status-seeking | Architectural dissatisfaction, spatial jealousy, complaining about conditions |
| **4. Delight in Cultivation & Abandoning** | *Bhāvanārāmo bhāvanārato, pahānārāmo pahānarato* | Internal Mental Development & Relinquishment | Joyful absorption in stillness (*samatha*), insight (*vipassanā*), and letting go of defilements | Viewing practice as a grim chore, complacency, intellectualizing, spiritual pride |

These four are described as:
* *Aggaññā*: Primeval, recognized from the earliest dawn of spiritual discernment.
* *Rattaññū*: Of ancient duration, enduring through generations.
* *Vaṃsaññū*: Hereditary to all Buddhas and their awakened disciples.
* *Porāṇā*: Time-honored and rooted in reality.
* *Asaṅkiṇṇā*: Unadulterated, pure, unmixed with worldly ambition.
* *Appaṭikuṭṭhā*: Uncensured by discerning beings.

### Section 2: The First Three Lineages — Radical Contentment (*Itarītara-Santuṭṭhi*)

The Pāli term **itarītara** means *\"whatever comes to hand,\" \"any whatsoever,\" \"modest or coarse.\"* The phrase *itarītarena santuṭṭho hoti* is the foundational canonical formula for contentment.

The Buddha outlines five rigorous psychological criteria that define genuine contentment with each physical requisite:

| Stage of Encounter | Canonical Criterion | Inner Attitude & Contemplative Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Lack / Absence** | *Aladdhā na paritassati* | When the requisite is not obtained, one does not fret, worry, panic, or feel shortchanged. |
| **Pursuit / Acquisition** | *Anācāraṃ na āpajjati* | One does not engage in improper conduct, manipulative speech, subtle hints, or compromises to acquire it. |
| **Usage / Consumption** | *Agatitho amucchito anajjhopanno* | When received, one enjoys it untied, uninfatuated, unobsessed, without emotional addiction. |
| **Clear Seeing** | *Ādīnavadassāvī nissaraṇapañño* | One clearly perceives the inherent limitations and danger (*ādīnava*) and knows the spiritual escape (*nissaraṇa*). |
| **Social Balance** | *Nevattānukkaṃseti, na paraṃ vambheti* | One neither exalts oneself for living simply nor disparages others who live differently. |

#### 1. Contentment with Clothing / Robes (*Cīvara*)
In monastic life, a robe is merely an instrument to shield the body from cold, heat, wind, biting insects, and for the sake of decency (*hirikopīna-paṭicchādanatthaṃ*). When a robe is coarse, faded, or patched, the mind of the noble disciple remains steady. If clothing is not readily available, there is no inner collapse (*aladdhā na paritassati*). When a fine or comfortable robe is received, the mind does not become intoxicated, possessive, or vain.

In daily householder life, this principle directly speaks to our relationship with apparel and material status symbols. We can notice how easily our culture hooks attention into fashion cycles, brand identity, and the subtle anxiety of *\"what will others think of my appearance?\"* Practicing with this lineage means recognizing clothing simply as practical utility and protection, releasing the urge to accumulate unnecessary wardrobes or judge others by their clothes.

#### 2. Contentment with Sustenance / Almsfood (*Piṇḍapāta*)
Monastic almsfood in ancient India—and in the living forest monasteries of today—is radically unpredictable. Monks walk silently from house to house, receiving whatever leftovers or simple offerings people place into their bowls: cold rice, bland soup, or occasionally rich, sweet foods. 

The noble lineage requires that:
* If the food is plain, cold, or meager, the practitioner does not grumble or despair.
* If the food is exquisite, the practitioner does not gorge or develop sensual greed.
* The food is consumed solely to sustain the biological organism for the holy life, just as a traveler oils an axle to complete a journey.

In modern everyday life, our relationship to food is frequently entangled with emotional soothing, comfort-seeking, and relentless preference. When our meal is delayed, poorly cooked, or not what we anticipated, we can observe the immediate somatic contraction in the body: our stomach is tight or knotted up, impatience surges, and we may find ourselves snapping at a server or family member. Bringing this noble lineage into daily life allows us to treat nourishment with gratitude, simple appreciation, and non-reactivity.

#### 3. Contentment with Shelter / Lodging (*Senāsana*)
The ancient monk lived under the canopy of trees, in caves, or in austere huts (*kuṭis*) made of bamboo and thatch. Discontent with lodging (*senāsana-arati*) was a constant hazard: huts could be drafty, damp, insect-infested, or noisy.

The noble lineage trains the practitioner to find peace wherever the body is placed. When given a rough shelter, they do not scheme to negotiate a better dwelling. When given a comfortable room, they do not cling or declare territorial ownership.

In contemporary life, we see this reflected in our relationship to housing, work cubicles, hotel rooms, and physical comfort. How much of our vital energy is consumed by wanting larger spaces, better views, superior furniture, or becoming irritable when our environment is noisy, cramped, or inconvenient? Contentment with whatever space is available creates immense psychological spaciousness.

### Section 3: The Danger of Spiritual Conceit — Non-Exaltation and Non-Disparagement

One of the most profound psychological insights of the Ariyavaṃsa Sutta is the Buddha’s explicit warning against **spiritual narcissism**:

> *\"And on account of that contentment, he does not exalt himself (*nevattānukkaṃseti*), nor does he disparage others (*na paraṃ vambheti*).\"*

Whenever an individual undertakes simplicity, asceticism, or rigorous discipline, the ego (*asmi-māna*) finds a subtle backdoor. The mind readily turns renunciation into an identity:
* *\"Look at how simply I live! I only own three shirts, while those people are obsessed with shopping.\"*
* *\"I eat plain, clean food and fast regularly, while others indulge themselves.\"*
* *\"I live in an austere, tiny space, while others cling to luxury.\"*

The moment this comparison arises, the noble lineage is fractured. The practitioner has merely exchanged worldly materialism for **spiritual materialism**. 

| Mental Movement | Trap of Spiritual Conceit | The Authentic Noble Lineage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Self-Assessment** | \"I am austere, disciplined, and superior to the unmindful.\" | \"Simplicity is simply an efficient tool for freeing the mind.\" |
| **Assessment of Others** | Condescension, judgment, looking down on those with possessions. | Equanimity, understanding varied conditions, non-interference. |
| **Internal Fruit** | Subtle pride, brittle identity, defensiveness, hidden conceit (*māna*). | Gentle ease, true humility, deep inner peace, natural openness. |

The true follower of the Ariyavaṃsa recognizes that contentment is not an accomplishment to be proud of; it is simply the natural, unburdened baseline of a clear mind. There is no need to make a performance out of austerity.

### Section 4: The Fourth Lineage — Delighting in Cultivation and Abandoning (*Bhāvanārāma & Pahānārāma*)

Contentment with physical requisites, while essential, is not the goal of the Buddha's path. If a person lived in a cave with one ragged blanket, ate dry crusts, and spoke to no one, yet spent their days daydreaming, brooding in resentment, or lost in mental torpor, their physical simplicity would be spiritually sterile.

Thus, the Buddha introduces the fourth and supreme lineage:
> *\"Here, a monk delights in mental development, finds pleasure in mental development (*bhāvanārāmo hoti bhāvanārato*); he delights in abandoning, finds pleasure in abandoning (*pahānārāmo hoti pahānarato*).\"*

#### 1. Delighting in Mental Cultivation (*Bhāvanārāma*)
The word **ārāma** means a pleasure-grove, a sanctuary of joy, or delight. *Bhāvanārāma* does not mean treating meditation as an agonizing obligation or a grim chore that one endures with clenched teeth. It represents a heartfelt delight in cultivating wholesome mental qualities:
* Delighting in the calm of **serenity (*samatha*)**.
* Delighting in the clarity of **mindfulness (*sati*)**.
* Delighting in the penetrating investigation of **insight (*vipassanā*)**.
* Delighting in the opening of **loving-kindness (*mettā*)** and **equanimity (*upekkhā*)**.

When meditation is grounded in genuine delight, practice becomes intrinsically self-sustaining. We are not forcing the mind to sit through sheer willpower; rather, we recognize that stepping out of worldly agitation into present-moment awareness is the sweetest refuge available.

#### 2. Delighting in Abandoning (*Pahānārāma*)
Equally vital is *pahānārāma*—taking joy in letting go, relinquishing, and abandoning unwholesome states (*akusala-dhammā*). 

Normally, the unawakened mind experiences letting go as a painful loss. We cling to our grudges, our anxieties, our self-justifications, and our sensory addictions because we believe they protect or define us. 

The practitioner of the noble lineage discovers the profound relief of dropping heavy burdens. As Venerable **Ajahn Chah** famously taught:
> *"If you let go a little, you will have a little peace.  
> If you let go a lot, you will have a lot of peace.  
> If you let go completely, you will know complete peace."*

When aversion arises, we take delight in dropping it. When obsessive mental proliferation begins, we take delight in stepping back. Relinquishment becomes a source of clean, unalloyed happiness.

### Section 5: Conquering Discontent and Delight (*Arati-Rati-Sahana*) Across All Quarters

The Buddha concludes the core prose exposition of AN 4.28 with a majestic declaration:

> *\"A monk possessed of these four noble lineages, if he resides in the east, conquers discontent, and discontent does not conquer him; if he resides in the west... north... south, he conquers discontent, and discontent does not conquer him; he conquers delight, and delight does not conquer him. What is the reason? Because the wise one conquers both discontent and delight.\"*

Here, the Buddha pairs the twin emotional poles that govern worldly life:
* **Arati (Discontent / Boredom / Aversion)**: The restless dissatisfaction that arises when outer conditions are plain, solitary, or lacking sensory entertainment. In meditation, *arati* manifests as spiritual dryness, the urge to get up, checking devices, or wanting to rearrange the room.
* **Rati (Sensual Delight / Infatuation / Intoxication)**: The sticky attraction to pleasure, comfort, praise, and worldly excitement that sweeps the untrained mind off its feet.

| State | Pāli Term | Psychological Manifestation | Somatic / Behavioral Expression | Contemplative Resolution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Discontent** | *Arati* | Restlessness, boredom, complaining, feeling trapped, irritation with simplicity | Agitation in the limbs, pacing, reaching for phone, fidgeting, stomach knotted up | Recognizing *arati* as an impermanent condition; staying still; resting in bare awareness |
| **Delight** | *Rati* | Sensual intoxication, infatuation with luxury, pride in recognition, chasing novelty | Flushed excitement, seeking indulgence, possessiveness, mental dispersion | Seeing the danger (*ādīnava*); remembering impermanence (*anicca*); non-grasping |
| **Mastery** | *Dhīro / Sahana* | Unshakeable poise, freedom in all environments (east, west, north, south) | Centered breathing, relaxed solar plexus, calm upright posture, dignified presence | Dwelling in the unconditioned; contentment with whatever arises (*itarītara*) |

When we conquer both *arati* and *rati*, we become spiritually autonomous. Whether stationed in a remote mountain hut, an urban apartment, a supportive monastery, or an inhospitable environment, our peace of mind is not held hostage by external geography.

### Section 6: Somatic Contraction, Conditionality (*Idappaccayatā*), and the Two Arrows

In our daily practice, how do the teachings of AN 4.28 translate into lived, embodied experience?

When outer circumstances fail to match our desires—when we receive a meager meal, when our room is cold, when unexpected bills arrive in the mail, or when someone interacts with us rudely—a biological reaction instantly occurs. 

#### 1. Somatic Contraction as Conditionality (*Idappaccayatā*)
Before any conscious thought is formulated, the nervous system registers friction. We can notice:
* Our stomach is tight or knotted up.
* The breath shortens or catches in the chest.
* The shoulders tense, and the jaw tightens.

Under the law of **Conditionality (*Idappaccayatā*)**, this somatic contraction is simply an evolutionary biological survival reflex. It is nature responding to perceived threat or discomfort. It is not a moral failure, a character defect, or a sign that we are \"bad meditators.\"

Recognizing this conditionality frees us from guilt. We do not need to condemn ourselves for having a knotted stomach. We can simply notice: *\"Tightness is present. The body is contracting around unpleasant contact.\"*

#### 2. The Two Arrows (*Sallatha Sutta*, SN 36.6)
The teaching of the **Two Arrows** clarifies the distinction between unavoidable sensory experience and self-created suffering:

1. **The First Arrow (Unavoidable Sensory Impact)**:  
   The coarse fabric of the robe, the tasteless almsfood, the hard wooden bed, the bad weather, the physical ache in our joints, the cold draft under the door. These things enter our senses directly through eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body. Because we possess a living physical form, the first arrow is physically inevitable.
2. **The Second Arrow (Dukkha / Added Mental Proliferation)**:  
   The mental reaction we reflexively heap on top of the contact:
   * *\"Why do I always get the worst room?\"*
   * *\"They don't appreciate all the work I do here.\"*
   * *\"I can't practice in these conditions!\"*
   * *\"If only I had more resources, then I could finally be at peace.\"*

This secondary proliferation—fueled by greed, aversion, and self-pity—is the Second Arrow. **This is dukkha.** The Ariyavaṃsa Sutta teaches us to absorb the First Arrow with patient endurance (*khanti*) while refusing to fire the Second Arrow into our own heart.

#### 3. Animals vs. Humans in Threat Response
Consider how animals handle unpleasant conditions compared to humans:
In nature, when a deer encounters a predator, its nervous system activates with intense adrenaline. It flees into the brush, leaps across the stream, and reaches safety. Once the threat is gone, the deer physically shakes off the shock, relaxes its muscles, and calmly goes back to grazing.

Humans, however, keep the threat alive through endless narrative thinking:
> *"We think about the tiger when there is no tiger."*

Hours or days after a minor disagreement, an uncomfortable meal, or a frustrating commute, our mind continues rehearsing the grievance, keeping the somatic contraction active in the belly. The noble lineage breaks this loop by bringing attention back to the reality of the present moment: *Here and now, the body is safe. Contentment is available right where we stand.*

### Section 7: Rewiring the Habit of Stress into Skillful Energy

In modern society, dissatisfaction and complaining easily become an entrenched, chronic habit of the mind. We develop a compulsive habit of auditing reality, constantly scanning our environment, our relationships, and our possessions for flaws:
* The coffee is too lukewarm.
* The room is too bright or too dim.
* The traffic is intolerable.
* Our colleagues are inefficient.

This chronic scanning drains our vital life force. When the mind is trapped in constant complaint, our energy is wasted fighting conditions that have already arisen.

The practice of the four noble lineages offers immediate, real-time relief, but its deeper long-term fruit is **rewiring the habit of stress into peace**. 

| Progression Stage | Reactive Worldly Mode | Noble Lineage Mode (*Ariyavaṃsa*) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Initial Impingement** | Sensory friction occurs (coarse food, noise, disappointment). | Sensory friction occurs (coarse food, noise, disappointment). |
| **Somatic Phase** | Somatic contraction (belly knots up, tension arises). | Somatic contraction observed with patient mindfulness (*khanti*). |
| **Mental Response** | Second Arrow fired: complaint, blaming, feeling victimized. | Conditionality recognized (*idappaccayatā*); no second arrow fired. |
| **Action & Energy** | Energy depleted in resisting reality and demanding upgrades. | Contentment established (*santuṭṭhi*); vital energy preserved. |
| **Long-Term Fruit** | Chronic stress habit reinforced; perpetual dissatisfaction. | Habit of peace established; energy available for meditation (*bhāvanā*). |

When we train ourselves to be content with whatever is received (*itarītara-santuṭṭhi*), we stop leaking energy into petty resistance. That preserved vitality becomes immediately available for deep meditation (*bhāvanā*), clear investigation, and grounded, compassionate presence in our work and relationships.

### Section 8: Historical Legacy — From Ashoka’s Bhabru Edict to the Living Forest Tradition

#### 1. Emperor Ashoka's Bhabru Rock Edict
Around 250 BCE, Emperor Ashoka carved his personal message to the Buddhist Sangha on the granite boulders of Bhabru (Rajasthan):

> *\"King Piyadassi of Magadha greets the Saṅgha and wishes them good health and comfortable living... These texts on Dhamma, reverend sirs, I desire that many monks and nuns should frequently listen to and reflect upon, and likewise the laymen and laywomen:  
> 1. Aliyavāsāni (The Ariyavaṃsa Sutta)...\"*

Ashoka understood that an empire’s moral integrity depended on the spiritual purity of its contemplative practitioners. If monastics succumbed to luxury, competitive building of large monasteries, and demanding fine robes and food, the entire Dhamma would degenerate into empty institutional politics. The *Ariyavaṃsa* was prescribed as the ultimate antidote to institutional decay.

#### 2. The Living Thai Forest Tradition
In the 20th century, the revival of the Forest Tradition under masters such as **Ajahn Mun Bhuridatto**, **Ajahn Chah**, and **Ajahn Sumedho** was nothing other than the living re-enactment of the Ariyavaṃsa Sutta.

Forest monks took only two or three robes, patched them until they were rags, dyed them with heartwood boiled from jackfruit trees, walked miles every morning for whatever alms were offered, and slept on split bamboo in remote jungle clearings. 

Venerable **Ajahn Sumedho** frequently reflects on the liberating simplicity of having *\"just enough\"*:
> *"When we are content with what we have, we are the richest people in the world. When we are always wanting something more or something better, we are living in chronic poverty, no matter how much wealth we possess."*

The noble lineage reminds us that freedom is not found in orchestrating the perfect outer paradise, but in possessing a mind that is at ease in any conditions.

### Section 9: Contemplative Practice Protocol & Daily Reflections

To integrate the Ariyavaṃsa Sutta into daily life, we can use the following reflective contemplation throughout the day:

| Life Domain | Reflection Point (*Dhamma Inquiry*) | Practical Daily Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Possessions & Clothing** | *\"Is this necessary for bodily protection and decency, or am I feeding identity and comparison?\"* | Wearing simple, comfortable clothes without vanity; using items until worn out; giving away unused possessions. |
| **Food & Nourishment** | *\"Can I receive this meal with gratitude, noticing bodily hunger rather than mental greed or boredom?\"* | Pausing before eating to reflect; accepting plain, simple, or imperfect meals without irritation; eating mindfully. |
| **Living Space & Comfort** | *\"Can the heart find spaciousness right here, regardless of the size or quietness of this room?\"* | Making peace with modest quarters; treating temporary accommodations with care; releasing territorial friction. |
| **Emotional Boredom (*Arati*)** | *\"When restless urges arise to escape or distract myself, can I hold this somatic itch without obeying it?\"* | Sitting still through dry meditation periods; noticing the stomach knotting up; observing the urge to reach for a screen and letting it pass. |
| **Sensual Intoxication (*Rati*)** | *\"When pleasant experiences arrive, can I enjoy them without getting hooked or clinging to their return?\"* | Observing pleasant feeling (*sukha-vedanā*) arise, stay, and vanish; not turning enjoyment into an identity. |
| **Relinquishment (*Pahāna*)** | *\"What burden can I drop right now?\"* | Consciously letting go of an old grudge, an imaginary future worry, or an urge to prove ourselves right. |

## Concluding Verses of the Ariyavaṃsa Sutta

The Buddha closes the discourse with verses celebrating the unshakable sage:

```
Nāsantuṭṭhi sahate taṃ, na taṃ vaheti arati;
Aratiñca sahate dhīro, ratiñca sahati vase.

Sabbakammavihāyī yo, panuṇṇo taṃ ko ninditumarahati;
Nekkhaṃ jambonadasseva, ko taṃ ninditumarahati;
Devāpi naṃ pasaṃsanti, brahmunāpi pasaṃsito.
```

> *Discontent does not overcome him,*  
> *Nor does delight overpower him;*  
> *The steadfast one conquers both discontent and delight,*  
> *Holding them under mastery.*  
>  
> *One who has relinquished all karma,*  
> *Who has dispelled the defilements—who is fit to blame him?*  
> *Like an ingot of refined Jambu gold,*  
> *Who could ever cast blame on him?*  
> *Even the devas sing his praise;*  
> *Even by Brahmā is he praised.*
