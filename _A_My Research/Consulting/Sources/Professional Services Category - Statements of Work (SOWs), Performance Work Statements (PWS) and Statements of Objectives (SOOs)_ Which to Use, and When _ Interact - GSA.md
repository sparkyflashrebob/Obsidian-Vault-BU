#### Statements of Work (SOWs), Performance Work Statements (PWS) and Statements of Objectives (SOOs): Which to Use, and When

By Karla Qawar
05/18/2023 10:26 AM
Are you stuck on which documents come into play for your acquisition needs? Performance Based Acquisitions (PBAs) have requirements set forth in clear, specific, and objective terms with measurable outcomes per [FAR 2.101](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/2.101). A key ingredient to successful acquisitions is understanding Statements of Work (SOW), Performance Work Statements (PWS), and Statements of Objectives (SOO). We'll dive on the differences and similarities below!
**Statement of Work (SOW)** defines exactly how and when the contractor will carry out the requirements and tasks within the project. This prescriptive document is used when the technical details are known, and the Government instructs the contractor on the preferred approach or solution to the problem. The work must be described in a detailed manner. Remember, a SOW written too broadly may lead to ambiguity and lack of clarity in performance standards, hence its importance when clearly identifying the job and directing the contractor on how to do and what is to be done. Many, if not most of the existing SOWs in use today are highly ambiguous, overly prescriptive, or both ambiguous in places and prescriptive in others.
If you are contracting for services, then you should generally NOT use a SOW. Per FAR [37.102(a)](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-37#FAR_37_102__d2379e10) “Performance-based acquisition is the preferred method for acquiring services.” A SOW is not performance-based because it tells the contractor “'how"'the work is to be accomplished or the number of hours to be provided” (see [FAR 37.602(a)(1)](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-37.6#FAR_37_602__d2425e21%5C)).
**Advantages:**
By describing the work in such detail, the government essentially;
\* provides the preferred approach or solution to the problem,
\* and locks in the approach the contractor must take.
**Disadvantages:**
SOWs requires the contractor to follow the government's approach.
\* If the contractor follows the government's approach and the result is unacceptable, the government has no recourse.
\* Many SOWs are so broadly written that not enough detail is provided to the contractor to appropriately bid.
\* Tendency to limit competition and result in incumbency
\* *You cannot have a Performance-Based Acquisition if you use a SOW*
**Performance Work Statement (PWS)** describes work in terms of results and outcomes rather than how the work is to be accomplished OR the number of hours to be provided OR the required set of labor categories to be used. A PWS contains a comprehensive set of performance requirements or task statements written with the desired outcome, result and/or deliverable in mind, not how the work is to be accomplished. Importantly, every requirement should have an accompanying performance standard (ex. quality, timeliness, progress). These performance standards should not just focus on timely delivery, but also transparently communicate the government's expectation of quality. A PWS gives contractors the flexibility to determine the best way to accomplish the task.
When using a PWS, it is important to include “Performance incentives where appropriate. When used, the performance incentives shall correspond to the performance standards set forth in the contract.” [ [FAR 37.601(a)(3)](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-37.6#FAR_37_601__d2424e37)] Incentives may be established at the performance requirement level to incentivize positive performance or disincentivize negative performance for each task or at least for critical tasks. You may also choose to consider specific incentive type contract, but that pertains more to the acquisition strategy than to the PWS.
Requirements, standards and incentives should be captured in a Performance Requirements Summary table inside the PWS.
A Quality Assurance Surveillance Plan (QASP) is a complimentary document to the PWS and outlines how the government will monitor that performance requirements are being achieved to the standards outlined in the PWS. The QASP should NOT be incorporated into the contract, therefore, it is very important that the QASP does not contain additional requirements and that performance standards, acceptable quality levels, and incentives are captured in the PWS, not the QASP.
**Advantages**
\* Focuses on measurable performances, outcomes and deliverables.
\* PWS can be prepared by the government OR result from a SOO issued by the government where the offeror responds by proposing a PWS.
\* Allows the government to evaluate and select distinct solutions/proposals that address the government's requirements.
\* Enables assessment of contractor performance against measurable standards.
\* Encourage competitors to develop and institute innovative and cost-effective methods of performing the work.
**Disadvantages**
The disadvantages are more inconveniences than actual disadvantages
\* Preparing a PWS requires a team based approach to develop (including program office, contracting, SMEs, requirements owners), which is often difficult to get agency support to pull together.
\* It can be challenging to establish performance requirements, develop performance standards, write the performance work statement, and produce the quality assurance surveillance plan.
**Statement of Objectives (SOO)** is a broad description of the government's overall goal(s) for the requirement written as High Level Objectives or performance objectives (ex. “Stop the spread of COVID-19 in the United States of America”). It focuses on broad outcomes rather than on how the work is to be done or even what tasks might be involved. The SOO ( [FAR 37.602(c)](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-37#FAR_37_602__d2425e42)) is written by the government and gives industry the ability to develop innovative, cost effective approaches to apply their expertise and knowledge. At a minimum, the SOO shall include the following sections:
1. Purpose;
1. Scope or mission;
1. Period and place of performance;
1. Background;
1. Performance objectives, i.e., required results; and
1. Any operating constraints.
**Advantages:**
\* Provides potential offerors maximum flexibility to propose alternative approaches to meeting the government's objectives.
\* Industry is able to apply its knowledge and experience to craft the most effective and innovative solution possible.
\* Enables the government to better assess the offerors' understanding of the requirement by eliminating the “how to” instructions normally contained in the SOW.
\* Requires contractors to submit their solution as a PWS.
\* The contractor developed PWS will include necessary performance requirements or tasks to achieve each outcome/objective.
\* The contractor developed PWS will also include performance standards for each requirement and a Quality Control Plan (QCP) along with any proposed incentives or disincentives.
\* The PWS (inclusive of requirements, standards, and any incentives) and QCP will be incorporated into the award.
\* The government can typically get a requirement on the street with a SOO faster than the other methods.
**Disadvantages:**
\* The evaluation process will be more time consuming. The government will not be comparing apples to apples. Consideration: Agencies may identify the PWS format offerors must submit in the solicitation. Identifying the format helps the evaluation and source selection process, but may limit creativity.
\* The Independent Government Cost Estimate (IGCE or IGE) won't be easy (or may be impossible) to determine prior to receiving offers. The solicitation should provide a ceiling so that contractors get a better understanding of size and scope.
**NOTE** : The PWS and SOW are incorporated into the contract, whereas the SOO is not incorporated into the contract ( [FAR 37.602(c)](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-37#FAR_37_602__d2425e42)). Rather, the PWS from the selected offeror responding to the SOO is incorporated into the contract.
