### Toward a Psychology without a Psyche: An Abhidhamma Treatise on Anatta and the Discrete Stream of Consciousness
#### 1. The Epistemological Foundation: Apparent vs. Ultimate Reality
The deconstruction of the "self" within Abhidhamma philosophy begins with a rigorous epistemological pivot: the distinction between  *Sammuti-sacca*  (conventional truth) and  *Paramattha-sacca*  (ultimate reality). From the perspective of cognitive phenomenology, this shift is not merely semantic but strategic. It moves the analytical focus away from the "person" as a substantial, unified entity and toward the fundamental constituents of experience. Conventional truth is a pragmatic necessity—it allows us to navigate a world of "beings," "men," and "women." However, Abhidhamma asserts that these are conceptual constructs ( *Paññatti* ) superimposed upon a flow of impersonal phenomena. To reach ultimate reality, one must apply a reductive, microscopic method that dissolves these composites into irreducible, abstract components.
This shift in perspective is best illustrated by the following comparison between the objects of daily experience and their fundamental ontological status:
| Dimension | Apparent Reality ( *Sammuti-sacca* ) | Ultimate Reality ( *Paramattha-sacca* ) |
| ------ | ------ | ------ |
| **Object Entity** | A table, a chair, a vessel | Forces, qualities, and vibrations |
| **Scientific Analogy** | Water (conventional substance) | $H_2O$  (the laboratory components) |
| **Human Application** | A "being," "self," or "soul" | Aggregates ( *Khandha* ), Elements ( *Dhatu* ) |
| **Characteristic** | Conventional names and concepts | Irreducible, abstract, and "thing-in-itself" |

The term  *Paramattha*  is a compound of  *parama*  (immutable or abstract) and  *attha*  (thing or reality). In Abhidhamma,  *attha*  corresponds to the English "thing" rather than "meaning." These realities are considered "immutable" because they preserve their distinctive identity across different combinations. For instance, the material forces inherent in a brass vessel remain fundamentally the same whether they are shaped as a cup or a vase. While these entities are not eternal—they arise and perish with staggering velocity—their essential nature is constant. By shifting from the study of "beings" to these abstract "things," Abhidhamma establishes a "psychology without a psyche," replacing the phantom of the soul with a rigorous inventory of discrete, conditioned events.
#### 2. The Microscopic Analysis: The Psycho-Physical System (Nama-Rupa)
Abhidhamma treats the human being not as a singular ego, but as a "complex machinery" of interrelated mental and material factors. This microscopic approach is intended to facilitate the understanding of things as they truly are, rather than as they appear to be. By breaking down the "so-called being" into its constituent parts, the illusion of a permanent "self" is replaced by a dynamic system of  *Nama-Rupa* .
The components of this system are categorized as follows:
*   **Nama (Mind):**  The non-material components, comprising consciousness and its associated mental states.
*   **Rupa (Matter):**  The 28 species of matter, comprising fundamental units and material forces that provide the physical basis for experience.
To further refine this analysis, the Abhidhamma utilizes the framework of the  **Pañcakkhandha**  (Five Aggregates), which explains the functional "whole" of experience through its phenomenological concomitants:
1.  **Rupa (Matter):**  The material body and physical forces.
2.  **Vedana (Feeling):**  The affective tone of experience—pleasurable, painful, or neutral ( *upekkhā* ).
3.  **Sañña (Perception):**  The faculty of recognition, "marking" objects based on past experience like a carpenter marks wood.
4.  **Sankhara (Mental States):**  A collective term for the fifty mental states, including volition and other intentionality-shaping factors.
5.  **Viññana (Consciousness):**  The specific term used within this fivefold division to denote the fundamental awareness or "receptacle" that knows the object.
Through this structural breakdown, the "being" is revealed as a flow of these five aggregates. The functional driver of this entire complex, the core of the mental experience, is  *Citta* .
#### 3. The Architecture of Consciousness: Citta and the 52 Cetasikas
In Abhidhamma,  *Citta*  is strategically classified to reveal its lack of a central "agent" or "soul."  *Citta*  is defined not as an "agent that thinks," but as a  **relation**  between a subject ( *arammanika* ) and an object ( *arammana* ). It is the mere awareness of an object. To maintain technical precision, the source provides synonyms including  *Ceta, Cittuppada, Nama, Mana* , and  *Viññana* , noting that  *Viññana*  is specifically employed when dividing the being into the "Five Aggregates."
*Citta*  never arises in isolation; it is always accompanied by  **Cetasikas**  (mental states), which serve as intentionality-shaping factors. There are 52 such states:
*   **Universals (Sabbacittasadharana):**  7 states common to all consciousness (e.g.,  *Phassa*  or contact,  *Vedana* ,  *Sañña* ,  *Cetana* ).
*   **Particulars (Pakinnakas):**  6 states arising in specific types. This includes  *Vitakka*  (initial application), likened to a bee flying toward a flower, and  *Vicara*  (sustained application), likened to the bee buzzing around it.
*   **Immorals (Akusala):**  14 states rooted in attachment ( *Lobha* ), ill-will ( *Dosa* ), or ignorance ( *Moha* ).
*   **Beautiful (Sobhana):**  19–25 states found in moral consciousness, such as  *Sati*  (mindfulness) and  *Amoha*  (wisdom).
A fascinating phenomenological nuance is found in the "six classes of laughter" ( *Hasituppada* ). While a worldling might laugh with an outburst involving the whole body ( *Atihasita* ), an Arahant’s laughter is refined to a mere smile ( *Sita* ) or a slight movement of the lips ( *Hasita* ), illustrating how different types of  *Citta*  manifest physically.
Crucial to this architecture is  **Cetana**  (Volition). It acts as the "chief disciple" or "carpenter" who fulfills his own duties while simultaneously regulating the functions of the other concomitants.  *Cetana*  coordinates the mental states and determines the kamma-producing quality of the thought-process. These discrete mental states arise and perish together with consciousness, forming a "staccato" reality of discrete atomism.
#### 4. Dynamics of the Stream: The Thought-Process (Citta-Vithi)
To deconstruct the illusion of a continuous "self," one must understand the thought-process ( *Citta-Vithi* ) as a sequence of discrete events. What we perceive as a steady stream is actually a rapid succession of "thought-moments." When an object enters a sense-door, a full process of perception consists of exactly  **17 thought-moments** :
1–3.  **Bhavanga (Past, Vibrating, and Arrest):**  The passive life-continuum is interrupted by the stimulus (3 moments). 4.  **Pancadvadavajjana (Sense-door adverting):**  The mind turns toward the sensory object (1 moment). 5.  **Sense-Consciousness:**  The momentary awareness of the stimulus, such as eye-consciousness (1 moment). 6.  **Sampaticchana (Reception):**  The mind receives the object (1 moment). 7.  **Santirana (Investigation):**  A momentary examination of the object (1 moment). 8.  **Votthapana (Determining):**  The stage where discrimination is exercised and "freewill" comes into play (1 moment). 9–15.  **Javana (Impulsion):**  The stage of Kamma and ethical reaction, where the mind "runs" on the object (7 moments). 16–17.  **Tadalambana (Registering):**  The final registration of the experience (2 moments).
This rigorous mathematical model (3+1+1+1+1+1+7+2 = 17) stands in stark contrast to Western concepts like William James’s "flowing" stream of consciousness. While James viewed the stream as having a continuous "fringe," Abhidhamma posits a "staccato" flow of discrete units. The extreme velocity of these perishing moments generates a false perception of an identical substance, much like the individual frames of a film create the illusion of smooth movement. This mechanical process of perception is the ultimate functional evidence for  *Anatta* .
#### 5. Philosophical Synthesis: Anatta and the Path to Insight
The  *Anatta*  (No-Soul) doctrine is the "crux of Buddhism" and the ultimate conclusion of the Abhidhamma analysis. Insight ( *Vipassana* ) is achieved when the seeker ceases to see a "person" and instead discovers only "fleeting mental states" and "material forces." This realization is often grounded in simple observation, such as the story of Patacara, who realized the truth of impermanence by watching the disappearance of water as it washed her feet.
The path of realization follows specific stages of knowledge ( *Ñana* ):
*   **Bhanga Ñana (Dissolution):**  The direct perception of the constant flux and dissolution of mind and matter.
*   **Bhaya Ñana (Fearfulness):**  The realization that all conditioned, dissolving things are a source of danger.
*   **Sankharupekkhá Ñana (Equanimity):**  The attainment of a balanced mind that views all conditioned things with impartiality, moving beyond the dualities of attachment and aversion.
The Abhidhamma model of "psychology without a psyche" fundamentally challenges the Western ego. "Being" is revealed not as a static entity, but as a "flowing movement" with no underlying agent. This microscopic analysis leads the seeker to transcend the world of aggregates and realize the Supramundane:  **Nibbana** . Unlike mundane feelings of happiness ( *Vedana* ),  *Nibbana*  is the "absolute reality" and the "bliss of relief"—a total release from suffering that exists beyond the rising and falling of conditioned thought-moments.
