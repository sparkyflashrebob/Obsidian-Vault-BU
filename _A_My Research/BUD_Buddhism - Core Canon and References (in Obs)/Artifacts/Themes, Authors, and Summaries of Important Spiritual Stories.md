| **Becoming / Clinging** | Ajahn Chah | The Worm in the Orchard | A man’s attachment to his orchard makes him like a worm boring into the trees; when they are harmed, he suffers because he has "taken birth" in that becoming through attachment. | [1, 2] |
| **Becoming / Clinging** | The Buddha | The Fire and the Wind | Rebirth is compared to a fire jumping from one house to another, sustained by the wind; the fire represents the being, the wind represents craving, and the house is the next destination. | [3, 4] |
| **Compassion** | Ajahn Chah | The Pig and the Farmer | When a farmer complains about low pork prices, the teacher notes the pigs have more reason to complain as they are slaughtered. This shifts perspective from self-centered worry to empathy for others' greater suffering. | [5] |
| **Compassion** | Anālayo | Angulimāla's Conversion | The Buddha transforms the serial killer Angulimāla through peace and non-violence. By stating, "I have stopped; it is you who have not stopped," he teaches that stopping harm is the path to redemption and awakening. | [3, 6-10] |
| **Compassion** | Bhadantācariya Buddhaghosa | Maṇḍūka the Deity | A frog killed accidentally while listening to the Buddha’s sermon is reborn as a deity due to its peaceful state of mind, showing that even animals benefit from acts of faith and attention. | [11] |
| **Compassion** | Patrul Rinpoche | Asanga and the Maggot-Infested Bitch | After twelve years of failed meditation, Asanga’s heart breaks for a dying dog. He cuts his own flesh to feed it and removes maggots with his tongue; this selfless act purifies his mind, allowing him to finally see the Buddha Maitreya. | [12] |
| **Compassion** | The Buddha | The Care of the Sick Monk | Finding a monk lying in his own waste, the Buddha personally washes and tends to him. He admonishes the Sangha that because they have no parents, they must care for one another, declaring, "Whoever would tend to me should tend to the sick." | [3, 13-17] |
| **Compassion** | Thich Nhat Hanh | The Wounded Swan | As a boy, Siddhartha rescues a swan shot by a hunter. He argues that the bird belongs to the one who saved its life, not the one who tried to take it, establishing compassion as the highest value. | [18] |
| **Concentration** | Bhikkhu Bodhi | The Three Boys and the Tree | To pick flowers from a tall tree, one boy offers his back and another his shoulder for stability so the third can reach. This illustrates that concentration requires the energy of effort and the stability of mindfulness to succeed. | [19] |
| **Concentration** | The Buddha | The Simile of the Lute (Tuning the Vina) | The Buddha teaches Ven. Soṇa that spiritual persistence is like tuning a lute; if the strings are too tight they snap, if too loose they don't play. Effort must be balanced to achieve the "middle pitch" of concentration. | [3, 18, 20-27] |
| **Discernment** | The Buddha | The Blind Men and the Elephant | A king has blind men touch different parts of an elephant; they brawl because each thinks the whole elephant is like a jar, a post, or a plow. This illustrates the danger of dogmatic attachment to partial truths and sectarian views. | [28-34] |
| **Equanimity** | S.N. Goenka | The Two Rings | A brother with a diamond ring suffers emotional instability, while the other with a ring engraved "This will also change" remains balanced. Remembering impermanence allows one to maintain peace through all ups and downs. | [35] |
| **Heedfulness** | The Buddha | The Simile of the Bowl of Oil | A man must carry a bowl of oil filled to the brim through a boisterous crowd; a swordsman follows, ready to behead him if he spills a drop. This illustrates the intense mindfulness and sense restraint required to guard the mind. | [4, 24, 36-38] |
| **Impermanence** | Kīsā Gotamī | The Case of the Mustard Seed | Seeking to revive her dead son, Kīsā Gotamī is told to find a mustard seed from a house where no one has died. Finding death in every home, she realizes mortality is universal and enters the path of practice. | [10, 39, 40] |
| **Interbeing** | Thich Nhat Hanh | The Cloud in a Sheet of Paper | Looking deeply at paper, one sees clouds, rain, and loggers. Because the paper is made only of "non-paper" elements, it has no separate self; it "inter-is" with the entire cosmos. | [41-43] |
| **Mindfulness** | Ajahn Chah | The Mange-Afflicted Jackal | A jackal blames its surroundings for its misery, but the real cause is the mange on its skin. Similarly, practitioners often blame external locations for their suffering when the root is the "mange" of their own internal wrong views. | [2, 44-46] |
| **Mindfulness** | Ajahn Chah | The Spider in the Web | A spider sits still in the center of its web and only moves when an insect vibrates the silk. Meditators should remain centered in awareness, observing sense impressions as they arise and pass without being pulled away. | [44, 47-49] |
| **Non-Self** | Ajahn Chah | The Broken Glass | The teacher views a glass as "already broken" even while it is whole. By accepting its inherent impermanence in advance, he uses it fully but remains free from grief when it inevitably shatters. | [1, 5, 44, 46, 47, 50, 51] |
| **Non-Self** | Sister Vajirā | The Simile of the Chariot | Just as the word "chariot" is a conventional label for an assemblage of parts like wheels and axles, the concept of a "being" is a mere convention for the five aggregates. In reality, no permanent soul exists. | [11, 52-55] |
| **Non-Self** | The Buddha | The Simile of the Raft | The Dhamma is like a raft used to cross a dangerous river; once the far shore is reached, the raft is left behind rather than carried. This teaches that even skillful teachings are tools for liberation, not to be clung to as an identity. | [3, 6-9, 18, 28, 30] |
