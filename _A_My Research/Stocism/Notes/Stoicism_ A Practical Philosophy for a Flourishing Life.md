"How to Be a Stoic" raises several core themes related to ancient Stoic philosophy and its application to modern life. These themes revolve around personal development, emotional regulation, social conduct, and the individual's relationship with the universe and others.

Here are the main themes explored in the book:

* **The Quest for a Happy and Meaningful Life**

  + The central question addressed is **how to live well**, handle life's challenges, conduct oneself, and prepare for death [1-3].
  + Stoicism is presented as a **practical philosophy** aimed at achieving a happy and meaningful life, known as *eudaimonia* or *ataraxia* (tranquillity of mind) [4-9].
* **The Dichotomy of Control**

  + A fundamental Stoic principle is to **recognize and seriously consider the difference between what is within our power and what is not** [10-15].
  + Things in our power include our opinions, impulses, desires, aversions, and actions, while things outside our power include our physical characteristics, possessions, reputation, and external circumstances like the weather or other people's behavior [12, 15-19].
  + The core advice is to **focus efforts on what is controllable and accept everything else with equanimity**, thereby saving energy and worry [13, 20-25].
* **Virtue as the Highest Good**

  + **Moral character and excellence (virtue) are considered the only truly worthy things to cultivate** [4, 6, 8, 10, 26-31].
  + The four cardinal virtues are **practical wisdom**, **courage**, **temperance**, and **justice** [13, 29-36]. These are seen as interlinked and inseparable [37].
  + "Preferred indifferents" (e.g., health, wealth, education) are things that can be pursued as long as they do not compromise one's virtue [6, 14, 28, 38-42].
* **Living According to Nature**

  + This Stoic catchphrase means **applying reason to social living**, based on the understanding that human beings are naturally rational and social animals [8, 19, 28, 42-64].
  + It emphasizes understanding human nature and our place in the cosmos [19, 28, 46].
* **Managing Emotions and Adversity**

  + Stoicism is **not about suppressing emotions but about acknowledging, reflecting on their causes, and redirecting them** [10, 65, 66].
  + The book provides strategies for dealing with common challenges like **anger, anxiety, and loneliness**, often by applying the dichotomy of control and cognitive restructuring [7-9, 13, 14, 26, 28-31, 36, 40, 41, 47-53, 65, 67-378].
  + It highlights how Stoicism can help individuals cope with **physical disability and mental illness** by focusing on agency and abilities rather than limitations [26, 379-394].
* **Death and Suicide**

  + The book directly addresses the **inevitability of death** and how Stoicism provides a framework for preparing for it in a dignified way, emphasizing that the fear of death is the real issue, not death itself [1, 2, 10, 67, 101-104, 374, 395-401].
  + It also explores the Stoic concept of the **"open door"**, discussing suicide as a matter of personal judgment under truly unbearable circumstances, emphasizing choice and integrity rather than resignation [402-408].
* **Social Engagement and Relationships**

  + Stoicism is portrayed as a **philosophy of social engagement**, encouraging love for all humankind and Nature, rather than withdrawal [11, 35, 266, 409, 410].
  + It discusses **love and friendship** through the lens of duty and rational assessment, distinguishing between natural affection and what is "right" or virtuous [67, 83-95, 411].
  + The concept of *oikeiôsis* (appropriation of other people's concerns), visualized as **contracting circles of concern**, underpins the idea of cosmopolitanism [35, 85, 410].
* **The Role of Role Models**

  + The book emphasizes the importance of **observing and imitating role models** (e.g., Socrates, Marcus Aurelius, James Stockdale, Malala Yousafzai) to cultivate virtue, acknowledging that these figures, while inspirational, are not perfect [26, 379-394, 412-423].
* **Understanding "Evil" as Ignorance (Amathia)**

  + A crucial Stoic concept, derived from Socrates, is that people do "evil" not on purpose but out of **"ignorance"** (*amathia*), meaning a lack of wisdom or dis-knowledge of what is truly good for themselves [70, 129, 285, 287, 348, 424-437]. This perspective encourages compassion rather than indignation [430, 434, 436].
* **Stoicism's Connection to Modern Psychology**

  + The book highlights that Stoicism is the **philosophical root of various evidence-based psychological therapies**, including Viktor Frankl's Logotherapy, Albert Ellis's Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT), and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) [11, 66, 438-440]. This connection underscores its practical relevance for mental well-being in the 21st century.
* **Practical Spiritual Exercises**

  + A significant theme is the **daily practice of Stoic principles** through specific "spiritual exercises." These include examining impressions, recognizing the impermanence of things, applying the "reserve clause," using virtue in challenges, pausing before reacting, practicing "other-izing," speaking little and well, choosing company wisely, responding to insults with humor, and regular self-reflection [13, 26, 67, 98-125].
* **Ecumenical and Open Philosophy**

  + Stoicism is presented as an **"ecumenical philosophy"** with points of contact and shared goals with various ethical traditions and religions (Buddhism, Taoism, Judaism, Christianity, secular humanism, ethical culture) [4, 12, 37, 70, 130, 131, 430, 431, 439, 441-444].
  + It is highlighted as an **open-ended philosophy**, ready to incorporate criticism and new scientific discoveries, demonstrating its adaptability to modern times [32, 445, 446].
