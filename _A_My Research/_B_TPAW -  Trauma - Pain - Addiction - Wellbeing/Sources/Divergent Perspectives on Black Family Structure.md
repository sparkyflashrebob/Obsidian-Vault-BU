The "breakdown" of the Black family is a highly debated topic. The colonial perspective argues that historical trauma—chattel slavery and Jim Crow—systematically dismantled original kinship systems. Conversely, the Western beliefs perspective (often linked to the 1965 Moynihan Report) attributes this breakdown to departures from the traditional, patriarchal nuclear family structure. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

The Colonial Perspective (Systemic & Historical Trauma)

This view argues that the Black family was fundamentally fractured by forced displacement and colonial legal frameworks.

Transatlantic Slave Trade: Enslaved people were stripped of their African names, languages, and traditional kinship networks. Family units were constantly disrupted through the buying and selling of human beings.

Legal Erasure: Under chattel slavery, Black individuals were legally prohibited from marrying, which prevented the establishment of recognized patriarchal family structures.

Adaptation vs. Breakdown: In response, enslaved communities formed extended "fictive kin" networks to ensure survival and protect orphaned children. Black families relied heavily on these decentralized, community-based structures.

Legacy of Oppression: Later, policies such as the exclusion of Black agricultural and domestic workers from New Deal labor laws and mass incarceration continued to destabilize Black households. [14, 15, 16]

The Western Beliefs Perspective (Cultural Deviation)

This view, rooted in normative Western sociology, judges family stability against the European or American middle-class standard—the nuclear family.

The Moynihan Report Framework: Authored by Daniel Patrick Moynihan, this viewpoint argued that a "tangle of pathology" in the Black community—specifically high rates of out-of-wedlock births and female-headed households—impeded economic and social progress.

Matriarchy as "Dysfunction": Western perspectives often viewed the prevalence of Black matriarchal or female-headed households as inherently unstable, arguing that the absence of a male breadwinner/father figure disrupted the traditional socializing of children.

Welfare Dependency: This viewpoint also points to the post-1960s expansion of the welfare state, arguing that certain policies inadvertently incentivized single motherhood and penalized the presence of fathers in the home.

Cultural Deficit Model: Proponents of this view assert that Black families suffer because they deviate from traditional (often religiously based) patriarchal norms of family structure and economic responsibility. [2, 22]

Ultimately, sociologists and historians debate whether this breakdown is the result of deliberate institutional destruction (the colonial view) or a failure to adapt to Western economic and social norms. [2, 14, 17, 23, 24]
