"Colonial Behavior Therapy" is a critical framework used by a growing movement of psychologists, activists, and theorists to describe how traditional Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) can inadvertently pathologize normal human reactions to systemic oppression, racism, poverty, and historical trauma.

Instead of addressing the harmful environments causing distress, traditional models often place the entire burden of change on the individual's mind.

The Core Critique of CBT as "Colonial"

Prominent mental health scholars—such as Dr. Jennifer Mullan (author of Decolonizing Therapy) and Dr. Samah Jabr—argue that traditional Western psychology is deeply rooted in Eurocentric, colonial mindsets.

The primary critiques include:

Individualizes Systemic Harm: It treats symptoms like depression or anxiety as internal "thought distortions" rather than logical, healthy responses to ongoing systemic oppression or institutional violence.

Enforces Assimilation: It prioritizes "functioning" and compliance within a toxic system over collective liberation, resistance, or structural change.

Dismisses Context: It assumes a universal standard of "rationality" based on white, Western, middle-class norms, ignoring the lived realities of marginalized communities.

Erases Interconnectedness: It cuts off the mind from the body, community, ancestors, and the land, treating wellness as an isolated, mechanical process.

Shifting Toward Decolonized Mental Health

When you cannot "CBT your way out" of structural or systemic wounds, the mental health paradigm must shift toward approaches that honor context, community, and somatic reality.

Liberation Psychology: An approach developed by Ignacio Martín-Baró that focuses on understanding the psychology of oppressed communities within their specific socio-political context, aiming for social justice rather than individual adaptation.

Somatic and Body-Centered Reconnection: Healing practices that recognize that racial and systemic trauma lives inside the nervous system and tissues, requiring ancestral or body-based release rather than purely intellectual intervention.

Collective Healing: Shifting the focus from isolated, one-on-one clinical hours to community care, mutual aid, and shared cultural practices that foster resilience.

Politicized Somatics: A practice that links individual nervous system regulation with social justice movement-building, acknowledging that true healing requires changing material conditions.

If you are looking to explore these frameworks further, organizers and practitioners often share peer resources through networks like the Decolonizing Therapy Platform, which provides educational workshops and reading lists dedicated to unlearning colonial psychological practices.
