### 14 Predictions for Therapy's Futures (2026 and Beyond)

It's crystal ball time. My (semi) annual predictions list focused on the therapy field.
Every year I want to do this exercise, not because I think I can actually predict the future, but because speculation forces us (me) to notice what's already happening. Futuring isn't fortune-telling, it's pattern recognition made explicit. It's asking what trajectories are we on, and what might they become.
These predictions are provocations. Some will land, some will miss, but all of them point toward questions the field needs to be asking. This year, I'm tracking 14 shifts that feel imminent, economic, technological, cultural, and epistemic. Consider this an invitation to think alongside me about what's coming and what we might do about it.
**The Economic Reckoning**
**1. The therapy diaspora accelerates**
Trained therapists are abandoning clinical practice at increasing rates, not because they've failed but because they've succeeded in seeing the constraints. They're moving into product design (AI mostly), organizational consulting, HR, coaching, anywhere they can do cultural architecture work without insurance company oversight or the grinding economics of fee-for-service. By 2026, we'll see the first major therapy training programs explicitly market themselves as preparing “cultural architects” (or something like that) rather than clinicians. The skills are therapeutic, but the application is everywhere except the therapy room. This isn't brain drain, it's recognition that the most impactful therapeutic work might not be called therapy at all.
**2. In-person therapy becomes a luxury good as class divide hardens**
Recently a therapist friend asked if he should lower his rates. He was concerned about dropping referrals, competition from AI platforms, the whole economic squeeze. I told him, raise your rates, don't lower them. Don't hate the player, hate the game.
Here's what's coming, in-person therapy stratifies completely along class lines. The wealthy get concierge services, therapists who make house calls, hold space at retreats, provide truly relational presence without time constraints. The middle and lower classes get AI chatbots and app-based interventions, not because these are better but because economics and time constraints make them the only viable option. Single parents working two jobs can't take off work for weekly 50-minute sessions at $200 plus each. The progressive pricing model that sustained private practice for decades collapses under this pressure. The math stops working, you can't charge three clients $150/hour to subsidize offering two clients $90/hour when your rent is $3000/month and student loans take another $800+.
Therapists face a binary choice, commit fully to the luxury model (in-person only, premium fees, concierge services, no insurance) or move to alternative funding structures, community supported therapy, patronage models, subscription services, sliding scale collectives with actual mutual aid backing them. The middle position, the solo practitioner trying to be both accessible and economically viable while maintaining professional legitimacy, becomes untenable. You can't compete with free AI on price while charging enough to survive in expensive cities. You can't offer deep relational work while churning through insurance authorizations. Something has to give. We'll see therapists pick sides in 2026. Some will raise rates unapologetically and serve wealthy clients who can afford the luxury of human presence. Others will build community-funded models or leave private practice entirely. The pretense that therapy is accessible middle-class healthcare ends. It becomes either luxury service or mutual aid project, with very little in between.
The AI companies will position themselves as the democratizing force , “therapy for everyone!” But it's the same logic as fast food replacing home cooking. Technically more accessible, actually less nourishing, and nobody's asking why people can't afford the real thing anymore.
**3. Insurance-based therapy enters zombie phase**
The system is functionally dead, unsustainable reimbursement rates, crushing administrative burden, outcome measurement theater that serves no one. But it continues stumbling forward because no alternative infrastructure exists at scale. Too many therapists depend on it, too many clients can only access care through it, too many training programs assume it as the default model. Therapists increasingly work the insurance system like a side hustle while building parallel practices of some sort. The contradiction becomes undeniable but unresolved. We'll spend 2026 watching the zombie shuffle along, everyone knowing it's dead but no one willing to kill it.
**4. Credentialing wars intensify, then collapse**
State licensing boards and professional organizations will double down on credential requirements, more supervised hours, more exams, more CEUs, just as the actual practice of therapy fragments beyond recognition. This creates a legitimacy crisis, the most innovative therapeutic work happens outside credentialed spaces entirely. The response will be regulatory capture attempts that backfire. Meanwhile, clients increasingly don't care about credentials, they care about results, relationship, and whether they can afford it. The gap between professional legitimacy claims and actual practice becomes a chasm.
**5. Federal deregulation attacks expose licensing's contradictions**
The Trump administration's moves to delegitimize or eliminate professional designations, therapists, social workers, potentially even licensing requirements, will create a crisis that forces the field to articulate what credentials actually do. This won't be good-faith deregulation to increase access. It will be ideologically motivated (attacking “woke” professions, undermining expertise generally) and economically motivated (making it easier for corporations to hire cheaper workers for mental health roles). It will be dangerous. But it will also expose contradictions therapists haven't wanted to face, how much does licensing actually protect clients versus gatekeep the profession? Why do states have wildly different requirements if this is really about competence? Why are peer support specialists often more helpful than credentialed therapists for certain populations?
We'll see a split. Therapists who fight to preserve professional legitimacy (defending credentials as essential consumer protection) versus those who see this as an opportunity to radically restructure who gets to do therapeutic work and how it's authorized. Both positions will have merit. Both will have blind spots. The danger is deregulation that serves corporate interests, therapy becomes gig work with no training requirements, AI companies flood the market with uncredentialed “coaches,” vulnerable populations get exploited. The opportunity is finally asking, what if we authorized therapeutic work through community accountability rather than state licensing? What if peer knowledge and lived experience were valued alongside graduate degrees?
Expect chaos. Federal versus state conflicts, emergency legislative sessions, professional organizations in crisis mode. Some therapists will leave rather than navigate it. Others will use the disruption to build alternative structures.
**The AI Shakeout**
**5. AI mental health companies consolidate after failures**
The venture capital funded “AI therapy” boom produces exactly what VC booms always produce, lots of companies solving the same problem badly, a few that find product-market fit, and a consolidation wave. Most AI mental health startups will fail or merge by end of 2026.
**6. Survivors pivot from “therapy replacement” to “therapeutic infrastructure”**
After the shakeout, surviving companies stop pretending to replace therapists and instead become the administrative backbone, documentation, billing, outcome tracking, even clinical decision support ( the picks and shovels). They become infrastructure that therapists rent. This creates a new dependency relationship. Therapists become contractors on platforms they don't control, subject to algorithmic management they can't see. The efficiency gains are real, but so is the loss of autonomy. We'll be debating whether this is liberation from administrative drudgery or a new form of professional capture.
**7. The “prompt engineer therapist” emerges as identity**
A split develops between therapists who work *with* AI (using it to generate questions, track patterns, create resources) and those who refuse it entirely. The former become more efficient and economically viable, the latter position themselves as “authentic” luxury services. Neither approach addresses underlying structural issues. But the fight over whether therapists should use AI will generate more heat than light, while the actual question, what are we trying to accomplish and for whom, gets lost.
**The Cultural Turn**
**8. Trauma-informed reaches saturation and backlash**
The therapeutic community begins openly questioning whether “trauma-informed” has become an empty signifier that pathologizes normal human suffering. We'll see the first major trauma-informed backlash, a conference, a publication (see Traumatized by [Catherine Liu](https://www.noemamag.com/the-problem-with-trauma-culture/) coming in 2026), a movement, arguing the framework itself is defuturing by making the past determine the future. This won't be abuse apology or denying harm. It will be questioning whether we've created a cultural narrative where everyone is wounded, everyone is triggered, and healing is the only legitimate life project. The backlash will be messy and get misunderstood, but it's necessary.
**9. “Therapeutic supervision” expands beyond therapy**
Organizations in tech, education, creative industries adopt formalized supervision models borrowed from therapy. Not therapists supervising these workers, but the structure of reflective practice becoming standard professional infrastructure. This is the Great Dispersion accelerating, therapeutic ways of thinking spreading everywhere. It's also potentially therapy's greatest cultural architecture contribution. The question is whether therapists will claim credit for the innovation or watch others implement it without attribution.
**10. “Therapy” and “coaching” legally merge in unexpected ways**
Rather than therapy winning the legitimacy battle, boundaries blur beyond distinction. Some states and countries create hybrid licenses or eliminate the distinction entirely. This creates chaos but also possibility. The question shifts from “is this therapy?” to “what are we actually doing together?” That's a better question, even if the regulatory uncertainty is painful. Expect major fights in state legislatures and professional organizations as this unfolds.
**The Decolonial Challenge**
**11. Western therapy faces its first genuine epistemological crisis**
This isn't a diversity crisis or inclusion challenge. This is a fundamental challenge to Western therapy's foundational assumptions about personhood, healing, time, and knowledge. Global majority therapists trained in Western models increasingly reject the frame entirely. They're not asking for cultural adaptations of CBT or trauma-informed care with better representation. They're creating parallel systems that refuse translation into Western therapeutic language. Cyclical time, ancestral time, dreamtime aren't metaphors, they're different chronotopes that make your linear trauma narratives impossible. This is an epistemological challenge Western therapy has no response for because it requires giving up universality claims. It requires being one healing tradition among many, not the scientific truth all others are evolving toward.
**12. Traditional healing becomes economically competitive with Western therapy**
As therapy becomes unaffordable luxury, communities turn to traditional healers who never left but were delegitimized by medicalization. This isn't about “complementary” or “integrative” medicine. It's about economic collapse forcing recognition that Western therapy isn't the only option. When therapy costs $300/session and curanderas, traditional Chinese medicine practitioners, Indigenous healers, or community elders offer services at accessible rates, the economics shift. When insurance doesn't cover therapy anyway, why privilege Western approaches? This isn't romanticizing traditional healing or claiming all such practices are effective. It's recognizing that Western therapy's monopoly was always economic and cultural, not proven superiority (see [How Psychotherapy Lost Its Magik: The Art of Healing in the Age of Science](https://www.psychotherapynetworker.org/article/how-psychotherapy-lost-its-magick/?srsltid=AfmBOoqbKCxLxTh_xVLzb7eaqnk_gfBWh9OXJGziQniBx_zMrHqIEU4f)). When the economic advantage disappears, so does the monopoly.
**13. Therapy training programs face decolonization demands they can't meet**
Students, especially from global majority backgrounds, stop accepting “add diversity content” solutions. They demand fundamental curriculum transformation. Why is Freud required but not Indigenous healing traditions? Why is the DSM treated as neutral description rather than cultural artifact? Why are we learning Western evidence standards as if they're universal? Some programs will close rather than change. Others will split into Western and explicitly decolonial tracks that can't speak to each other. Some will attempt synthesis and fail. A few might genuinely transform, but it will require faculty and administrators giving up authority they've never had to question. The uncomfortable truth, narrative therapy, ACT, psychodynamic work, even liberation-focused approaches, all emerge from Western assumptions about personhood, time, causality, and healing. Those assumptions might not travel. And that's okay. The pluriverse has room for many healing traditions. Western therapy's crisis is its inability to be one tradition among many.
**The Wildcard**
**A major therapy modality declares itself obsolete**
Here's the prediction I least expect but most want to see. One of the established approaches publicly announces it has been superseded by its own evolution and needs to be retired rather than continuously updated. Imagine if CBT or IFS (or narrative therapy) communities said, “This frame has reached the end of its useful life. Here's what we're building instead.” It would be genuinely unprecedented. Therapy modalities usually claim eternal relevance, just needing minor updates. They never declare victory and step aside. This would reframe every conversation about theoretical evolution. It would model what it looks like to outgrow your own framework. It would be an act of intellectual honesty rare in any field. I don't think it will happen. But I want to plant the seed of possibility.
**Counter-Defuturing Work**
These predictions aren't just about what's coming, they're about what's already here, intensifying. They're trajectories we're on, for better and worse. Some of these futures are defuturing, they close down possibilities, concentrate power, pathologize resistance. The AI infrastructure trap, the credentialing arms race, the insurance zombie consuming resources while producing nothing. Others are counter-defuturing, they open up new possibilities, challenge monopolies, pluralize healing futures. The therapy diaspora creating new roles, the decolonial challenge forcing epistemic humility, traditional healing reclaiming space.
The question isn't which predictions are correct. The question is, where are the intervention points? Where can we nudge trajectories toward futures worth inhabiting? Where do we need to let old structures collapse rather than propping them up? I don't have answers, just patterns and provocations. But that's the point of futuring work, not prediction but preparation, not certainty but scenario mapping.
What futures are you tracking? What am I missing? What needs to be said that I'm not seeing?
Let's think together.
