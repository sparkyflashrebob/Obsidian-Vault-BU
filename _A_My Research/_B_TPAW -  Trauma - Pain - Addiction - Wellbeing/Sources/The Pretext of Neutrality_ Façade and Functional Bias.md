The pretext of neutrality is the use of a detached, "objective" stance as a facade. It masks underlying biases, agendas, or implicit power dynamics. By claiming to be neutral, a person, institution, or policy can shield their actions from scrutiny, maintain the status quo, or justify inaction in situations that demand a moral stance. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

This concept appears across several key areas:

International Law & Foreign Policy: In armed conflicts, traditional laws of neutrality treat all belligerents equally. However, strictly enforcing this during an unjust invasion often serves as a pretext for inaction. For example, the 1930s Neutrality Acts made no distinction between aggressor and victim, effectively empowering totalitarian regimes by blocking aid to democracies.

Media & Journalism: Strict adherence to "balance" requires reporters to give equal weight to two opposing sides. When one side relies on scientific consensus or factual evidence and the other relies on falsehoods, balanced reporting becomes a pretext for neutrality that misinforms the public.

Judicial & Legal Systems: Courts often claim to apply "neutral principles" to the law. Critics argue this is a pretext. Elevating formal neutrality—such as ignoring historical disparities to enforce formal colorblindness—often protects systemic advantages and suppresses efforts to remediate historical discrimination.
