#### Peer Groups

MINDFULNESS MEDITATION TEACHER CERTIFICATION PROGRAM
Guidelines for Giving and Receiving Feedback
Skillful feedback can be a vital support to developing your skills as a mindfulness teacher. The following is a form for offering feedback that many people in teacher training programs have found useful and supportive. It emphasizes a simple, concise process of offering both positive feedback (what’s working well and why) and constructive feedback (what didn’t work so well and what could be done to make it more effective).
A few features of this approach:

* Each round of feedback begins with the person offering feedback about their own meditation,
  talk, or other practice element. This heightens and develops the ability to self-assess and monitor and makes your learning active rather than passive.
* It’s practical and actionable. Rather than giving vague feedback like “I really liked it,” “that was really great,” or “that didn’t do it for me,” you offer specific points about exactly what worked (or didn’t work), how things might be improved, and even more important, why things work or could work if done differently. Emphasizing the “why” turns it into a learning opportunity for everyone in the group.
* It’s also manageable for the person receiving the feedback because each group member only offers one specific piece of positive feedback and one specific piece of constructive/suggestive feedback. This makes it easier to absorb the information. (As the program progresses, Mentors may offer additional pieces of feedback but participants please keep it to one.)
  In your first group meeting, discuss and set your overall intention for feedback over the duration of your time together. What are the general principles of wise speech that can guide your time together?
  BASIC PRINCIPLES OF THE FOUR-STEP FEEDBACK LOOP PROCESS
  *Keep the focus on behavior—what gets said or done or what does not get done—along with the rationale, i.e. why the behavior worked or didn’t work. Together, they create content and context, which are both vital components that help the brain actually place the learning into long-term memory.*
  Offer both positive and corrective/suggestive feedback: Positive Feedback What works (be specific) and why it’s effective. Corrective (or Suggestive) Feedback What didn’t work (briefly point out the mistake or the missed opportunity), then what might work instead (sometimes called an “Alternative Positive Behavior”) and why (how your suggested Alternative Positive Behavior could be effective if used next time).
  Note It is vital to our brains that we provide a “why” to both positive and corrective feedback. That’s the way that the “right side of your brain” knows to store the information into long-term memory. If you simply give vague feedback, the brain doesn’t take the information seriously because no new content is created. However, for this very same reason, note that when giving corrective feedback, we strongly encourage you to skip the “why” after the “what” that highlights the mistake or ineffective behavior, focusing on “why” the APB could work instead. We actually want the mind to help you focus on the suggestion rather than dwelling on the mistake.
  THE FOUR-STEP PROCESS
  Step 1: Positive Feedback from Self In this step, if you are leading the meditation, talk, or other practice element, you offer positive feedback to yourself. Name one thing that you really feel worked well, and why. There may be many things that you enjoyed or thought went well—but it’s useful to single out one thing and really unpack it. Be as specific as possible about what it was and why it was effective in particular.
  *“WHAT I noticed that I did that I’m particularly proud of (or that went well, or that I would want to be sure to do again next time) is [name the specific behavior] so that or because [tell WHY that specific behavior was effective].”*
  For example, you might say, “One thing I felt was very effective was the way I deliberately left plenty of silence between verbal instructions. This gave time for my students to practice the instructions on their own and settle into the meditation a bit before receiving another cue from me that they might not be ready for.”
  You might notice at this step that it’s tempting to start pointing out what you feel you did wrong. Notice the judgmental mind at work! And for this round, only offer positive feedback.
  Step 2: Positive Feedback from Other In this step, other members of your peer group (and your Mentor if this is a Mentor-led group) will each offer positive feedback. Again, it’s a good idea to keep it limited to one point per person (although the Mentor, at their discretion, may name more than that). Avoid vague praise like “I really liked it.” Instead, focus on being specific and unpacking the “why” of what worked.
  *“WHAT I noticed you did that I would want you to repeat/reinforce next time is [name the specific behavior] so that or because [tell WHY that specific behavior was effective].”*
  For example, your Mentor might say, “When you responded to that student’s question about working with difficult emotions, I appreciated the way you asked a clarifying question about what the student was experiencing. That helped the student feel seen and heard and enabled you to respond to their question more sensitively.”
  Note Discourage any dialogue about the feedback at this point. Simply encourage the person receiving the feedback to say “thank you.” Encourage him or her to take the feedback under advisement. If necessary, remind the person that they are the one who ultimately gets to choose whether or not to use the feedback offered or to simply let it go.
  Step 3: Corrective Feedback from Self In this step, the person who led the meditation, talk, or other presentation names one thing that they didn’t think worked so well. Again, keep it to just one thing! Emphasis is placed on simply pointing out the mistake and then moving directly to an Alternative Positive Behavior followed by WHY you think that new behavior could make a difference.
  *“WHAT I noticed I did was [name the specific mistake or ineffective behavior or perhaps a missed opportunity]. WHAT I suggest I DO DIFFERENTLY next time is [name the APB or suggestion] so that [tell WHY the suggestion could make a difference].”*
  For example, you might say about a talk you gave, “I was so concerned about covering all four foundations of mindfulness in my 15-minute talk that I packed in too much information and it felt a little rushed and dry. Next time, I’ll just focus on one foundation and use more stories and poems— that way I think my students will be able to relate to it better and understand it more deeply.”
  Step 4: Corrective Feedback from Other Now, go around the group and have each person name one thing that they thought could use improvement. (Again, at their discretion, a Mentor may name more than one thing.) Again, use the
  “WHAT, then WHAT & WHY” format.
  *“WHAT I noticed that didn’t seem to work was [name the mistake or ineffective behavior or perhaps a missed opportunity]. WHAT I suggest you DO DIFFERENTLY next time is [name the APB or suggestion] so that [tell WHY the suggestion could make a difference].”*
  For example, after your talk, one of your peers might say, “I noticed that you used a lot of Buddhist terminology and Pali words in your talk, which I didn’t really understand. What I suggest is that next time you translate those concepts into modern secular language—that way I’ll feel like they relate directly to my own life.”
