# And so, it's important to develop the skills for handling them, and handling them in a mindful way that builds relationship. One of the major researchers into microaggressions is Dr. Derald Wing Sue, who is a psychologist.

# And he explains them as "brief and commonplace daily verbal, behavioral, or environmental indignities, whether intentional or unintentional, that communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative racial slights and insults towards people of color."

# So, as Kitsy was saying before, these are seemingly slight, seemingly small, and yet very impactful. Racial microaggressions are one of the important ways that racism is expressed and that white supremacy culture and racial inequity are reinforced and maintained.

# At the same time, the racist assumptions that underlie them may be unconscious or so deeply embedded in cultural practices that people who are socialized as white may not even realize the impact of what they're saying. Think of the phrases "working like a slave," "cracking the whip," "Jewing him down," "she gyped me," "illegal aliens," "ghetto," "good neighborhood," "good school."

# These are all phrases that have their origins in slavery and other racist practices. Or the question, "Where are you from?" asked of someone who's not white or who has an accent. It may seem like an innocent question, but it can convey a sense of "You don't belong here." Especially if the person is asked that question repeatedly.

# I have a friend who is Japanese American, and when she travels abroad, people ask her, "Where are you from?" And she says, "I'm American." And they say "No, where are you really from?" As if she can't be American because she's not white.

# Microaggressions have many negative effects, especially when they're combined with the persistent experience of being targeted by racial slurs, negative media images, as well as direct and indirect violence. Microaggressions harm the physical and mental health of people of color, leading to increases in depression, anxiety, physical illness, and chronic conditions that are related to stress.

# Microaggressions leave BIPOC feeling excluded or othered in important settings like work, school, and mindfulness classes. They also harm relationships between BIPOC and white people and fuel the sense that white people don't understand the experience of people of color or don't respect it.

# These interactions may seem insignificant or accidental to the white person. They do not seem that way to the people of color. So, there's a big asymmetry in these experiences.

# Microaggressions reinforce and normalize racism, and their impact is often denied by the person speaking. And this denial is, as you may have guessed, it's one of the ways that we protect the good white self by saying, "Oh, I didn't mean it that way" or "Oh, I didn't know."

# As a white mindfulness teacher, it's pretty much inevitable that you'll commit microaggressions. And this is not at all surprising since we grew up learning white supremacy culture as normal. So, it's important to know how to respond when a student points out that you've done this, whether it's a white student or BIPOC student.

# And as Kitsy was saying earlier, difficult emotions can arise in these moments-- shame, self-blame, anger, many, many more. And you may be completely convinced that the other person speaking to you is wrong, they misheard you, they misunderstood you. And, by using mindfulness and compassion for yourself and for them, you can respond skillfully in a way that will build connection rather than enforce separation.

# Because this isn't about whether or not you're a good white person. This is about the mindfulness practice of seeing clearly and your commitment to support the learning and well-being of your students.

# So, what are microaggressions about? I would like to share with you some of the themes, the racist themes, assumptions, of white normality and the practice of separation. So, one of the key themes is "You are other." And this is like the question that I mentioned earlier, "Where are you from?" said by a white person to a person of color.

# Or "You speak such good English," you know, said by a white person to someone with Asian features who actually was born and raised as an English speaker. Or "Can I touch your hair?" said to a Black person, as if Black hair is so, so, different and exotic.

# Another theme is "You are less than." So, said to a Black person, "You are so articulate." And I remember in the early years of the Obama presidency reading news reports where they talked about how articulate Barack Obama is. And it carries the kind of assumption that "Wow, it's surprising that a Black person is articulate."

# Another thing that I've heard is-- I overheard one day a white person asking a young Latina, "Where are you going to college next year?" And she said, "I'm going to Harvard." And the white person said, "Oh, the one in Massachusetts?" As if there are many Harvards out there, but conveying the sense that "Oh, someone like you wouldn't go to Harvard."

# Another theme is "I don't see you." So, this is where I might call a woman of Indian descent by another Indian woman's name. Or I'd say, "I don't see color," by which I intend to say, "Oh, I'm not racist." But what it actually conveys is "I don't see this huge part of who you are. I choose not to know."

# Repeatedly mispronouncing someone's name. Stepping in front of a person of color in line. Or only calling on white students in the class, which is something that teachers do and are often completely unaware of. And yet, it has an impact on the BIPOC students who are present.

# Denying the reality of racism is another theme. People will say, "I'm not racist. My best friend, my partner, my spouse is Black," as if having a Black spouse means that you are no longer a white person.

# "If you work hard, you'll get somewhere," spoken encouragingly to a young person of color, as if that's all that there is to it. Denying the presence of all of the barriers that are present for someone of their race.

# "I'm sure they didn't mean it that way," in an effort to pseudo-comfort a person of color who has just received an insult. Denying their experience. Denying their reality.
