happen to you as you take the teacher's seat. And Tara and I are just each going to speak for a

about answering difficult questions. And as Tara and I were talking, it's really the theme about

Some things to keep in mind. One is just to take a pause after you hear the question. Take a
breath so that you don't have to immediately leap in. You can let the question be held in the
space in the room.
[EXHALES]
That helps. There's no hurry with it. And everybody else is digesting the question as well.
Sometimes, it helps to appreciate people, because they're nervous when they ask a question.
And in almost every question-- especially the really naive ones-- there's some seed of
something important and delicious in it. And when you can recognize that and acknowledge
that, that's also really helpful. The person feels engaged and the room feels like it's safe to be
able to inquire. And that's what questions are. They're really a part of inquiry and discovery.
If the question is intense, and it has emotion, anger, or fear, or confusion, or delusion, or
whatever-- or some important theme in it-- and it brings up something intense-- again, we're
talking about difficult questions-- about racism, or abortion, or war, or conflict, or addiction, or all
the kinds of topics we've talked about, it can also be helpful to name it and say, "Oh, this
question has a lot of suffering in it."
Or this question is something that we're all wrestling with as a culture. Can you feel that? It's not
just this person's, but it's our collective struggle. Or there's a lot of uncertainty in this question,
isn't it? And we all have our wrestling with uncertainty.
Or if there's a lot of grief and tears in it, just to take a pause and say, "I can really both
understand your question, and I feel the pain in what you're saying." And let's just stop and hold
that with some kindness or compassion before you even respond. So the naming of what's
brought up-- you then are demonstrating that it can be held with loving awareness and
mindfulness.
And you're inviting a presence with that which is difficult. Bringing your own presence into it.
Saying, "OK, this is difficult. This is sticky."

Then one of my most important suggestions for you-- and from Tara as well-- especially if
there's anything abstract or even general that's asked in the question, might be half the time in
certain groups, I'll respond, "What makes you ask?" And we've already talked about this.
Because if somebody raises their hand and say, "Can you tell me about karma?" I could go on
for an hour about karma even though the Buddha said it's unexplainable in certain ways, but I
would try. But if I say, "What makes you ask?" they might say, "Well, my favorite aunt just died
in a car accident, and she was just going to be there for the birth of her granddaughter. What
made that happen?"
Or this person who I thought was great turns out to have become an addict. Or we lost all our
money with Bernie Madoff. Or I don't know what the answer would be-- "What makes you ask?"
But the minute you hear, "Oh, we lost all our money," or this person died, or this other
circumstance, then you know how to answer that question. It's not a general question about
karma. It's, "How do I understand what's happening to me?" So that response of what makes
you ask is really important.
And sometimes, you want to clarify and speak to their mind, because they're looking for some--
"Is there such a thing as healthy desire or is attachment always bad?" And you can say, "Well,
there's healthy attachment and unhealthy," and so forth.
But more often, you speak to their heart, because it's the heart that wants to know something.
That's what brings them, in a way. "How do I live in this world? How do I understand?"
And so it's kind of a heart-to-heart. In the Sufi tradition, they call it sobat. It's this kind of intimate
connection from one to another.
And two last little things: It's OK not to know. One of the most refreshing things to hear a
teacher say is, "Oh, that's a great question. I don't really know the answer to that. Maybe
someone does, or let's reflect on it." Don't-know mind is a very highly regarded and celebrated
state in Zen tradition. So you show yourself as the master that you are. "I don't know."

therapist began to formulate a dream interpretation in her head, she thought it was really a no-

that person's life.
But this wasn't how the elders there responded. After hearing the dream, Jung's grandson
looked out at the whole large audience and said, "Would you all please rise? We will stand
together," as they rose, in a moment of silence in response to this dream.
The audience stood for a minute. And this woman who was the therapist waiting impatiently for
the discussion that would follow. She was certain there were all these themes and things that
would be unpacked from these images. But then when they sat, they all remained quiet.
And a few days later, she was able to ask one of the panel members who was close to Jung's
grandson. And he said, "Oh my dear, there is in life a vulnerability so extreme, or suffering so
unspeakable, that it goes beyond words. In the face of such suffering, all we can do is stand
together in witness so no one need bear it alone."
And so your job isn't really so much to answer questions, although you can. But a big part of
your answering questions is also just the bearing witness to the life of another human being.

When we think of the more challenging questions that come at us-- the ones that are really
complex, and have paradox and charge, or whatever-- in a way, there's never really a good
answer. And even the idea "answer," like there's these words that are an answer, is smaller
than the response that's possible.
And I really like the frame of that this is inquiry. It's a process, not a question and then an
answer. And that the more that our response keeps a process going, paying attention to
experience the more powerful it is. Does that make sense?
Now, some questions are a total setup to have a more cut-and-dry response. So I'm not trying
to say everything should lead us into the great mystical beyond-words unknown, because they
don't all do that. But just-- I think the Buddha had a beautiful set of these Pali words,
"Ehipassiko," which is "come and see for yourself," that kept on bringing people back to
checking in and sensing what was true for themselves. And this is something we've spoken to
before and we'll probably do it again and again, because it's not our reflex. Our reflex is to
answer more specifically.
So a few examples of ways that we can continue the process, have it keep on deepening. If a
person's stuck in a difficult experience-- and Jack described it beautifully-- just that pausing and
saying, "OK, so this is suffering." And that you right away get the sense it's not your fear. It's the
fear. And we all are living with it.
And you can even check in with the group and ask, "How many are struggling with this particular
kind of experience?" And you can even go beyond that in certain formats of saying, and, "What
have you discovered that helps you?" And there's ways of drawing on the collective wisdom that
can be more powerful than anything you might say. It involves them.
So I found that really, really helpful. Sometimes, what makes it complex and charged is that the
person's energy is angry or hostile. And I've had many times people have asked questions--
sometimes it's questions about no self, or it can be about anything, but there's an anger that
they're fighting something.
And if that happens, again, it's this pausing, being aware of what comes up in you-- whether
there's this thing that you have to defend the teachings or defend what your view is-- and then
to really look and sense that if somebody is asking a question from an anger or a hostile place,
there's some suffering in there that's asking for your attention. And to just see if you can notice
what it is. I found that if I can respond in some way that is deeply respectful of the who they are
behind that suffering, then something else unfolds that is useful, if there's something in the
question that's useful.
