# Study Guide: Key Concepts and Practices

## Quiz

1. According to the provided texts, what is a key question to ask yourself when engaging in habitual behaviors like overeating or using nicotine/alcohol?
2. When judging others, what might be an underlying feeling to explore within yourself?
3. How is self-forgiveness described in the context of having hurt or harmed yourself?
4. What is the "great doubt," and how is it presented as an antidote to self-doubt?
5. In the context of mindfulness and emotions, how does "allowing" help loosen identification with difficult feelings?
6. The text mentions finding "stillness in the attention" alongside the "ever-changing stream" of experiences. What does this duality highlight about human experience in meditation?
7. According to Rick Hanson, what is a simple practice for amplifying wholesome feelings?
8. When observing thoughts during meditation, what is the purpose, and is it necessary to eliminate them?
9. What is the meaning of "autonoetic consciousness" as described in the provided text?
10. The text mentions feeling the hands "from the inside out." What aspect of meditation practice does this emphasize?

## Answer Key

1. According to the texts, a key question to ask when engaging in habitual behaviors is what needs you are trying to satisfy or what fears you are trying to soothe. These behaviors often stem from underlying emotional states or unmet needs.
2. When judging others, the texts suggest that you might be feeling fearful yourself. This implies that projection and judgment can be rooted in one's own insecurities and fears.
3. Self-forgiveness is described as recognizing the ways you have hurt or harmed yourself through thought, word, or deed, knowingly or unknowingly, and extending forgiveness for these instances. It involves acknowledging the sorrow carried from these actions.
4. The "great doubt" is the doubt of fundamental questions like "who am I," "what is this life," "what is love," or "what does it mean to open." It is presented as a way to turn self-doubt into a magnificent question that encourages deeper inquiry.
5. "Allowing" helps loosen identification with difficult feelings by releasing the resistance that keeps us hooked to those emotions. It's an attitude of saying "yes" to the felt sense of the experience in the body and heart.
6. This duality highlights the reality of our human experience, where a stable awareness or stillness exists amidst the constant flux and change of thoughts, sounds, feelings, and sensations.
7. According to Rick Hanson, a simple practice for amplifying wholesome feelings is to let yourself feel the feeling for about 15 seconds when you notice it. This helps to register and deepen the positive experience.
8. The purpose of observing thoughts is not to eliminate them, but to recognize when thinking is happening and to witness them with mindful loving awareness. Thoughts are seen as ephemeral and dissolving like clouds.
9. Autonoetic consciousness is described as the ability to link past, present, and future to each other. This is presented as an important function and a sign of integration in the brain.
10. Feeling the hands "from the inside out" emphasizes bringing awareness to the subtle physical sensations, volume, and space within the body parts. This is a practice of deep relaxation, concentration, and calming through internal awareness.

## Glossary of Key Terms

**Aboriginal Title:** Refers to the rights of Aboriginal Peoples to the occupation, use, and enjoyment of their land and its resources. It includes the right to exclusive use and occupation, the right to choose land uses (subject to sustainability), and an economic component.

**Allowing:** In mindfulness and self-compassion, the attitude of non-resistance and acceptance towards present moment experiences, including difficult emotions or sensations. It's saying "yes" to the felt sense in the body and heart without agreeing with the content.

**Autonoetic Consciousness:** The ability to connect the past, present, and future, considered an important function and a sign of brain integration.

**Bodhisattva:** Originally a term for Gautama Buddha's earlier lives, it is expanded in Mahayana Buddhism to mean anyone on the path to becoming a Buddha with a boundless heart and vows to help others.

**Deka:** A poetic form consisting of ten syllables, typically arranged in three lines with a 2-3-5 syllable structure, meant to be spoken aloud.

**Desire:** The wanting and activation related to something anticipated, which can feel powerful in its buildup but may diminish after satisfaction is achieved.

**Forgiveness:** The process of releasing the hurts and sorrows associated with being harmed by others, or recognizing and releasing the ways one has hurt or harmed oneself. It is presented in the context of forgiving others, oneself, and asking for forgiveness.

**Great Doubt:** A profound inquiry into fundamental aspects of existence, such as "who am I," "what is this life," or "what is love," presented as an antidote to smaller, self-limiting doubts.

**Jataka Tales:** Mythical teaching stories about the many lives of the Buddha, illustrating the virtues expressed through his different incarnations.

**Mindful Loving Awareness:** A state of attention characterized by presence, kindness, and non-judgmental observation, applied to the breath, body sensations, emotions, and thoughts.

**Mindfulness:** The practice of bringing open, kind, and interested attention to present moment experience, allowing for recognition and awareness of sensations, emotions, and thoughts without getting lost in them.

**Non-identification (in RAIN):** The aspect of the RAIN practice where one recognizes that the experience is not personal or a fixed part of oneself, often realizing that the feeling has been experienced before in different contexts.

**RAIN:** An acronym for a mindfulness practice: Recognize, Allow, Inquire, Non-identification. It is used for working with difficult emotions and experiences.

**Spaciousness (with Yes):** The sense of openness and ease that can arise when one adopts an attitude of allowing and acceptance towards difficult experiences, in contrast to the contraction felt with resistance ("no").

**Stillness in the Attention:** A stable quality of awareness that can be found through meditation practice, existing alongside the ever-changing flow of thoughts, sounds, feelings, and sensations.

**Window of Tolerance:** A term used in neuroscience to describe the range of experience that a person can be present for with mindfulness. Mindfulness practice can expand this window.
