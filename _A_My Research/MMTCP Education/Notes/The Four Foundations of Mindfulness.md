The Foundations of Mindfulness, often referred to as the Four Foundations of Mindfulness, are a core concept in mindfulness practice and teaching [1-6]. They represent different dimensions or aspects of human experience that can be the focus of mindfulness practice [3-9].

Here are the four foundations:

1. **Mindfulness of the Body** (Contemplation of the body) [3-10]:

   * This is often described as the **first foundation of mindfulness** [10].
   * It involves guiding students to **rest with the breath** as a way of collecting attention, quieting the mind, and coming into presence [10]. The breath is a universal meditation anchor because it's always with us and can be used to steady and focus attention [11].
   * If the breath is difficult, alternative anchors like touch points in the body (e.g., sensations in hands, feet, buttocks) can be used [12-18].
   * Mindfulness of the body emphasizes being awake in the physical body, recognizing its aches and pleasures as exactly what is needed to be fully human and alive [19]. It involves sensing the body inwardly and outwardly, including even the "body of the earth" [9].
   * The practice is about letting the body breathe itself as it chooses, allowing mind, body, and heart to come together in presence [20]. It is not about breath control but rather about being sensitive to each moment and open to what is now [20].
   * It helps to bring attention out of being lost in thought and back into the living reality of physical sensations [17, 21].
2. **Mindfulness of Feelings** (Contemplation of feelings) [3-9]:

   * This involves becoming aware of the feelings present, sensing them deeply inwardly and outwardly [22].
   * Initially, practitioners become aware of feelings as pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral [22, 23].
   * The typical human habit is reactivity to these feelings (grasping for pleasant, aversion to unpleasant, distraction from neutral) [23]. Mindfulness allows for being present with a greater range of experience without reactivity, expanding one's "window of tolerance" [23, 24].
   * When working with feelings, particularly difficult ones, teachers might ask questions like "Where do you feel that in your body?" to deepen mindful presence [25-27]. Naming the feeling gently can also be helpful [28].
   * Acknowledging and making space for all states, including joy and ease as well as pain and fear, is essential [29].
3. **Mindfulness of Thoughts** (Contemplation of thoughts) [3-9]:

   * This involves directing mindful, loving awareness to observe thoughts without becoming lost in their content [30, 31].
   * The "monkey mind" is a common experience, characterized by a mind that is constantly thinking, planning, remembering, and judging [11]. Mindfulness of thoughts helps to wake up from the "trance of thinking" where thoughts are mistaken for reality [32-34].
   * The goal is not to get rid of thoughts, but to recognize thinking as it happens and then gently return attention to an anchor like the breath [31, 35].
   * Naming thoughts (e.g., "planning," "worrying," "judging") can help shift identity from believing the thought to acknowledging it [31, 36].
   * Mindfulness helps to observe that thoughts dissolve like clouds under sunlight, revealing their insubstantial nature [31, 37].
   * Investigating the "somatic roots" of thoughts (how they feel in the body) is a key part of inquiry, as it's not primarily a cognitive process [38-40].
4. **Mindfulness of Mind States and Relation to Experience** (Contemplation of mind states or the process of life itself) [3-9]:

   * This foundation involves seeing how life operates and shifts attention from the content of experience to the potential for freedom [9].
   * It recognizes that wherever we become present, there is a moment of freedom, and that a quality of witnessing or knowing allows us to acknowledge what is happening without being entangled in it [9].
   * This deepest level of inquiry explores the nature of awareness itself, asking "Who is aware?" or "What is listening?" to investigate the awareness that is behind all experience [41-47].
   * It reveals that "form is emptiness, and emptiness is form," meaning that the changing forms of experience are not separate from the vast, formless presence of awareness [48-53].
   * This practice helps to dissolve the sense of a separate, limited self, leading to the realization of our true nature as loving awareness [54-59].

These four foundations form the basis of insight practice and are designed to lead to deep spiritual intention, peace, clear comprehension, and ultimately, freedom from suffering [9, 10, 60]. They emphasize meeting all experiences with a quality of "loving awareness"—a non-judgmental, caring attention in the here and now [58, 60-65].
