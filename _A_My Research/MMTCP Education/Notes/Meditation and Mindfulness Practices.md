Drawing from the provided sources and our conversation history, several methods and practices for meditation and mindfulness are detailed.

At its core, **meditation** is described as a deliberate training or directing of attention that awakens us beyond the conditioned mind and habitual thinking, revealing the nature of reality [1-4]. It opens us to perspectives larger than ordinary consciousness or the small sense of self [3]. It's emphasized that "meditation" isn't one single thing, but rather there are many forms or skillful means to quiet the mind and open the heart [1-3, 5]. Mindfulness is one of these forms [6, 7].

**Mindfulness** is defined as awareness cultivated by paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally [6-8]. It is also described as loving attention or loving awareness [5, 9, 10]. Mindfulness involves being a spacious, kind, nonjudging awareness of the present [9]. It also includes an appropriate response to the present situation, indicating it is not merely passive [9]. Mindfulness serves awakening and freedom [9, 11-14]. Bringing awareness to experience deepens presence and freedom [15-17].

Mindfulness is traditionally taught as focusing on **four different dimensions or foundations of experience**: the body, feelings, mind (thoughts), and the process of life itself or mind states [1, 2, 18-21].

Here are some specific methods and practices mentioned:

* **Mindfulness of Breath:** A basic mindfulness practice often used to stabilize attention and serve as an anchor to the present moment [21-32]. It's not about controlling the breath but allowing the body to breathe itself [33]. You can notice where you feel it most easily or experience the whole body breathing [33]. Details include noticing if the breath is long or short, pausing, and becoming sensitive to the body's life [33]. One practice involves bringing in qualities like calming or ease with each breath [33-36]. Using a half smile can support this [28-30, 33]. Gently naming the breath, like "in breath" and "out breath," can be helpful, but the focus should be 95% on feeling the direct experience [33]. Counting breaths can also engage the mind slightly, but the numbers are secondary (5%) to the feeling [33]. Resting with the breathing and relaxing with it are also mentioned [34-36].
* **Mindfulness of the Body:** This involves cultivating awareness in and through the body [10, 37-43]. It can include scanning the body to notice and soften tension [27, 31, 32]. Mindful movement practices, like those adapted from Taijiquan focusing on the "Five Fields of the body" (feet, legs, hips/pelvis, torso/arms, neck/head), can help establish mindfulness in the body, provide inner focus, and release tension [38, 39, 42, 43]. Paying attention to interoception (what's happening inside the body) and exteroception (what's happening in the space) is part of embodied mindfulness [37]. The body can also serve as an anchor for presence [24, 26, 44, 45].
* **Mindfulness of Feelings/Emotions:** This involves paying mindful loving awareness to feelings as they arise [10, 21, 46-51]. Working with difficult emotions or "hard stuff" is an important aspect, involving opening to them with self-compassion, presence, and mindfulness [52]. Bringing awareness to feelings is described as profoundly liberating [21]. When working with strong or intense emotions, particularly with trauma, it can be necessary to step back and use strategies to find stability and groundedness [53-57].
* **Mindfulness of Thoughts:** This practice is not about getting rid of thoughts but about recognizing when thinking is happening so you are not lost in it [10, 58, 59]. It allows you to witness the activity of mind rather than being that activity [60]. You can observe thoughts with mindful loving awareness [61-64]. Techniques include gently naming thoughts according to their quality (e.g., planning, judging, worrying) [61-64]. The practice helps you step out of the stories thoughts tell and see common patterns without being caught in them, resting instead in mindful loving awareness [64-66]. Thoughts are seen as ephemeral, dissolving like clouds [61-64].
* **Heart Practices:** These include practices cultivating qualities like lovingkindness, compassion, joy, equanimity, gratitude, forgiveness, and generosity [1, 2, 11-14, 19, 20, 52, 67-70]. Self-compassion is specifically mentioned as adding kindness and common humanity to mindfulness [52, 71-73]. Practices can involve offering phrases of care to oneself [70]. These are seen as aspects of the awakened heart [74, 75].
* **RAIN (Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Nurture):** Listed as a practice that students learn and are expected to understand, practice, and lead others in [18-20, 72, 73]. It is also a theme for Dharma talks [72, 73].
* **Concentration Meditation:** This is a major category of meditation aimed at developing the ability to attend fully, focus, and potentially unify with a particular experience [1, 2, 11-14, 23, 25, 69, 76, 77]. It can use various anchors like the breath, body, sound, visual objects, mantras, or koans [22-26, 69, 78-81]. It often involves repetition [69] and is used to settle and focus the mind [76].
* **Insight Meditation (Vipassana):** Described as being central to the teachings of awakening [82, 83]. Its purpose is to strengthen the capacity to experience reality directly ("things as they are") [82, 83]. It involves bringing natural and clear attention to whatever arises in the present moment [82, 83]. It is also referred to as awareness or mindfulness practice [9].
* **Contemplation:** A form of meditation involving reflecting on sacred passages, prayers, words, or themes to raise spiritual questions and reflect deeply [69].
* **Devotional Practice:** Focuses attention on inspiration and love, opening practitioners to prayer, worship, or connecting to something bigger [3, 69]. It often involves surrender and can use practices like prayer, song, or visualization [69].
* **Pause, Relax, Open:** A three-step practice particularly useful for staying present to difficult experiences [84-86]. It involves consciously pausing, relaxing the nervous system (which can include softening the eyes and a slight smile, referred to as "smile yoga" [84, 85]), relaxing the throat and belly [84, 85], noticing body pressure [84, 85], and opening to presence [84, 85].
* **Grounding:** A strategy for managing strong fear or trauma [53, 54]. It involves becoming aware of physical contact points, such as feet on the ground, body on the seat, or hands resting [53, 54]. It can also involve slowing the breath and feeling the sense of gravity [53, 54]. Using the senses to acknowledge "I am here in this moment" is part of it [53, 54], as is touching a pleasant or neutral object [53, 54]. Resourcing is also mentioned as a strategy for groundedness with trauma [55-57].
* **Mindfulness in Daily Life:** Applying mindfulness to everyday activities [24, 26, 37, 44, 45, 47-49, 55, 56]. This can include using the pause, conscious breathing, the body as an anchor, or engaging mindfully in activities like walking, eating, washing dishes, folding laundry, or drinking tea [24, 26, 40, 44, 45].
* **Choiceless Awareness:** Mindfulness of whatever is predominant without trying to direct or control it, simply noticing and allowing [24, 26, 44, 45]. This can be cultivated as an open-focused awareness [87, 88].
* **Mindful Dialogue:** A practice for interaction that involves creating a safe, caring container, allowing pauses between speaking, and speaking from one's own experience and heart [89, 90].
* **Setting Posture:** Many practices begin with establishing a comfortable yet alert posture, often described as spine erect but not stiff [27-32, 50, 51, 87, 88]. Releasing obvious physical tension is also a common starting point [27-32, 50, 51, 91]. Practitioners are often given choices regarding posture (sitting, standing, lying down) and whether to close or open their eyes [92-96].
* **Invitational Language:** When guiding others, using language like "I invite you to," "perhaps," or "if it feels comfortable/accessible for you" is suggested to offer choices and acknowledge that experiences can vary [96-100].
