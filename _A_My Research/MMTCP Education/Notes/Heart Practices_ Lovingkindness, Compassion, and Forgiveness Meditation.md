Lovingkindness, Forgiveness, and Compassion meditations are fundamental "heart practices" within mindfulness, often described as two wings of a bird alongside mindfulness itself [1, 2]. They are considered natural outcomes of mindfulness, softening and opening the practitioner in a way that deepens mindfulness and enhances clear seeing, as love and wisdom are interdependent [2]. These practices are also referred to as "skillful means" (upaya) that foster awakening and love [3-5].

### Lovingkindness (Metta) Meditation

**Lovingkindness (Metta)** is described as a **general, open-hearted, caring, friendly attitude** [6]. Its purpose is to **awaken connectedness with all of life** [7] and to **cultivate a loving heart and a collected, settled mind** [8]. This practice helps to **dissolve the identity of an isolated, deficient self**, creating a foundation for including others in an unconditionally loving heart [7]. The metaphor of a "rainbow of compassion" appearing when the "sunshine of lovingkindness meets the tears of suffering" highlights its relationship with compassion [6].

**How to Practice:**

* **Beginning the Practice**: It is often recommended to **start by directing lovingkindness to those you love** deeply and uncomplicatedly, such as a child, a pet, or one or two beloved individuals, as this is where it is easiest to open the heart [9-11].
* **Using Phrases**: Practitioners inwardly recite or whisper **traditional phrases** that express well-wishing and kind intentions [10, 12, 13]. Examples include:
  + "May you/I be filled with lovingkindness" [10, 12, 13].
  + "May you/I be safe from inner and outer dangers" [10, 12, 13].
  + "May you/I be well in body and mind" [10, 12, 13].
  + "May you/I be at ease and happy" [10, 12, 13].
  + For self-compassion, phrases can be adapted to "May I be kind to myself" or "May I accept myself as I am" [14].
  + The wording can be modified to "I wish to be," "I hope," or "I want," or include "as possible" if "May I" feels too much like a prayer or seeking permission [14].
* **Expanding the Circle**: After establishing a sense of lovingkindness for loved ones, the practice expands:
  + **Self-Lovingkindness**: Imagine loved ones gazing back with the same well-wishing, then direct these wishes to yourself, perhaps by placing a hand on your heart [15].
  + **Benefactors**: Include someone in your life who has loved or cared for you [16].
  + **Wider Circles**: Gradually extend the meditation to friends, community members, neighbors, people everywhere, animals, and all beings [16-18].
  + **Difficult People**: Eventually, the practice can include difficult individuals and even enemies [18-20].
* **Integration**: Lovingkindness can be practiced formally, as a dedicated portion of daily meditation, or informally, by carrying the intention to keep one's heart open and see goodness throughout the day [21-24].

**Challenges and Benefits:**

* **Challenges**: The practice may sometimes feel mechanical or awkward, and can even bring up feelings of irritation or anger. It is important to be patient and kind towards oneself in these moments, allowing whatever arises to be held with friendliness [25, 26]. If agitation or upset arises, the phrases might seem discordant, but acknowledging "May this too be held in lovingkindness" can be helpful [22]. Starting with others is often easier for those who find self-love difficult, especially if there's a history of trauma or self-aversion [9, 27].
* **Benefits**: Cultivating lovingkindness helps to **reduce negative states of mind and increase positive ones** [28]. It can lead to sweeter dreams and easier sleep [29], and fundamentally helps one realize that **the heart is vast enough to hold all of life** [30].

### Compassion Meditation

**Compassion** is defined as **what happens when love meets pain and sorrow** [31]. It is a **quivering of the heart** that acknowledges suffering [31], embodying the wish that others do not suffer and a desire to help them if possible [32]. Finding a way to make peace with human imperfections is considered the ground of all healing [33, 34].

**How to Practice:**

* **Setting the Foundation**: Begin by sitting in a centered and quiet way, breathing softly, and feeling your body, heartbeat, and the life within you [35-37]. Acknowledge how you treasure your own life and guard yourself against sorrows [35-37].
* **Starting with Loved Ones**: Similar to lovingkindness, it is often suggested to **start with someone close to you whom you dearly love**, picturing them and feeling your natural caring [9, 35-37].
* **Acknowledging Suffering**: Become aware of their struggles and sorrows, allowing your heart to open with a wish to extend comfort and share in their pain [38-41].
* **Reciting Phrases**: Inwardly recite phrases such as:
  + "May you be held in compassion" [38, 40-42].
  + "May your pain and sorrow be eased" [38, 40-42].
  + "May your heart be at peace" [38, 40-42].
  + Words can be altered to best express your care [38, 40, 41, 43].
* **Receiving Compassion**: Imagine this dear one gazing back at you with the same loving care and offering these compassionate wishes back to you, acknowledging your own sorrows [43-46]. Take in these wishes, then direct the same compassion to yourself with phrases like "May I be held in compassion" [44-46].
* **Expanding the Field**: Extend compassion to other loved ones, friends, and eventually to strangers, difficult people, and all beings, picturing them and wishing them well [19, 44-49].
* **"Just Like Me" Practice**: A specific method to cultivate compassion for others, including enemies, is to recognize that they are "just like me" by silently repeating phrases such as: "This person has a body and a mind, just like me. This person has feelings, emotions, and thoughts, just like me. This person has, in his or her life, experienced physical and emotional pain and suffering, just like me" [47, 49-51].
* **Tonglen**: Another style of compassion practice where you **breathe in suffering (contacting vulnerability, fear, hurt, shame)** and **breathe out a sense of coolness, space, tenderness, kindness, or love** [52-55]. The more deeply you contact vulnerability with the in-breath, the more fully you sense the unconditional tenderness and space it arises out of with the out-breath [54]. This can be for oneself, specific others, or the suffering world [53, 55-57].
* **RAIN (Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Nurture)**: This acronym is a tool for practicing mindfulness and compassion with difficult emotions [58-62]. The "N" in RAIN stands for **Nurture with self-compassion**, which involves regarding your experience with kindness and imagining what it would be like to feel your needs met [62-66]. This can involve gentle touch, like placing a hand on your heart [67-70].

**Benefits:**

* Compassion **reduces negative states of mind and increases positive ones** [28].
* Being in a compassionate state provides **more resources to give to others** [71].
* It allows one to **care deeply without being overwhelmed by caring**, maintaining a balance with equanimity [72, 73].
* The practice is deeply healing and can **reveal an inner spirit** and foster a sense of being held and safe [58, 74].

### Forgiveness Meditation

**Forgiveness meditation** is about **cultivating a forgiving heart** [75]. It is a practice that brings **self-judgments into awareness so they can be seen and released**, serving as an especially cleansing way to end the day [76, 77]. It aims to help individuals **make peace with their human imperfections**, which is seen as the very foundation of all healing [33, 34].

**How to Practice:**
**1. Self-Forgiveness:**

* **Preparation**: Begin by becoming still and relaxing any obvious areas of tension, taking a few long, slow breaths to fully arrive in your body [76-81].
* **Inquiry**: Ask yourself, "Is there anything between me and being at home with myself?" [78, 79, 81]. Pause and notice what arises in your body and mind, such as stories of wrongdoing, falling short, or not meeting personal standards [78, 79, 81].
* **Acknowledging and Offering Forgiveness**: As something arises, simply acknowledge it and offer forgiveness. You might gently place your hand on your heart and whisper, "Forgiven, forgiven" or "It's okay" [33, 34, 82].
* **Deepening the Practice**: Recall your intention not to push yourself out of your own heart [33, 34, 82]. Continue to inquire for other self-judgments until they are identified [33, 34, 82]. For deeper suffering, hold the underlying vulnerability with compassion, sending a sincere message of self-compassion to places of fear, unmet needs, and pain [67-69, 83]. You might mentally whisper, "I see how I’ve caused myself suffering, and I forgive myself now" [67, 68, 83, 84].
* **Meeting Resistance**: Meet whatever arises—fear, judgment, shame, grief, numbness, or even resistance to forgiving—with the message of forgiveness, repeating "Forgiven, forgiven" [84-87].
* **Intention as Seed**: If you feel unable to forgive yourself or believe you don't deserve it, acknowledge these doubts with compassion and state, "It is my intention to forgive myself when I am able" [85-87]. This intention serves as the seed that will gradually relax and open your heart [85-87].

**2. Forgiveness of Others:**

* **Preparation**: Sit comfortably, with eyes closed and breath natural, allowing your body and mind to relax [88, 89]. Breathe gently into your heart area and feel any barriers or emotions carried from unforgiveness, and the pain of keeping your heart closed [88-90].
* **Asking Forgiveness**: Begin by asking forgiveness from those you have harmed, acknowledging ways you have hurt, betrayed, or abandoned others out of your own pain, fear, anger, and confusion [91-94]. Visualize these instances and feel your sorrow and regret, then inwardly repeat, "I ask your forgiveness. Please forgive me" [91, 92, 94, 95]. Recognizing one's own shortcomings makes it possible to open to extend forgiveness to others [93].
* **Extending Forgiveness**: Then, turn to forgiving those who have hurt or harmed you out of their fear, pain, confusion, and anger [96-98]. Repeat phrases such as: "I have carried this pain in my heart too long. To the extent that I am ready, I now offer you forgiveness. I forgive you" [96-98].
* **Patience**: Forgiveness cannot be forced or artificial. For deeply painful experiences, you may only feel the burden or anguish. In such cases, gently touch this feeling and be forgiving of yourself for not being ready to let go. Continue the practice, allowing the words and images to work gradually [96, 97].

**Benefits:**

* Forgiveness meditation offers a **gateway to freedom** [99].
* It brings self-judgments into awareness to be **seen and released** [76, 77].
* It is described as an **especially cleansing way to end the day** [76, 77].
* Ultimately, the practice helps to loosen identification with thoughts and feelings, revealing the **emptiness of self** and shifting one's identity towards **vast awareness** [100]. This fosters inner peace and freedom that is not dependent on external conditions [100].
