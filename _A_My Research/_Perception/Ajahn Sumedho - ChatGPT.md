Ajahn Sumedho often talks about perception (saññā in Pali) as something deceptively simple but actually slippery when you start watching it closely.

### His Main Points

- **Perception isn’t reality itself**: He points out that what we perceive is not the thing-as-it-is but a mental impression or designation. You never get “the tree,” only your mind’s snapshot labeled “tree.”
- **Conditioned and habitual**: Perceptions are shaped by memory, language, culture, and mood. He’ll often use examples like hearing a sound — depending on conditioning, you might perceive it as music, noise, or a threat.
- **Not-self**: Since perceptions arise and pass depending on conditions, Ajahn Sumedho encourages seeing them as *anattā* (not-self) — “just perception, not me, not mine.”
- **Mindfulness practice**: He emphasizes noticing perception without clinging: when you label, recognize it’s only a label. The freedom comes in not being trapped by the story perception spins.
- **Conventional vs. ultimate truth**: Perceptions are useful conventions for living in the world, but mistaking them for ultimate truth leads to suffering.

### How He Uses This in Teaching

He often invites students to reflect: “Notice how the mind perceives this experience.” For example, in meditation, you might notice the perception “pain in the knee.” Instead of running with the story (“my knee is bad, I should move”), he teaches staying with the bare perception and observing how it changes.

### Alternative Angles

- **Theravāda Abhidhamma**: Perception is a universal mental factor — it marks and remembers features so the mind can recognize objects again.
- **Western psychology parallel**: Similar to how cognitive science frames perception as constructive, not direct — the brain predicts and interprets input.
- **Zen comparison**: Zen teachers might call perception “conceptual overlay” or “naming mind,” which obscures direct experience.

### Immediate Takeaway

Next time you catch yourself naming or labeling something, pause. Ask: *is this the thing itself, or just my mind’s perception?* That little gap is where Sumedho points to freedom.
