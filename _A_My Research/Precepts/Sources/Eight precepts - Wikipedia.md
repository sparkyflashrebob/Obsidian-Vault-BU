In [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism), the **Eight Precepts** ( [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): *aṣṭāṇga-śīla or aṣṭā-sīla* , [Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_language): *aṭṭhaṅga-sīla or aṭṭha-sīla* ) is a list of moral precepts that are observed by Nuns, or Upāsakas and Upasikās ( [lay Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upasaka)) on Uposatha ( [observance days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uposatha)) and special occasions. They are considered to support [meditation practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_meditation), and are often observed when staying in monasteries and temples.
They include ethical precepts such as refraining from killing any living being, but also more specific ones, such as abstaining from entertainments. The tradition of keeping the Eight Precepts on weekly observance days is still widely practiced in all [Theravadin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravadin) Buddhist countries and communities worldwide. Based on pre-Buddhist *sāmaṇa* practices, the eight precepts are often upheld on the Buddhist [observance days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uposatha) ( [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): *upavasatha, poṣadha, pauṣadha* , [Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_language): *uposatha, posaha* ), and in such context called the **uposatha vows** or **one-day precepts** . In some periods and places the precepts were widely observed, such as in 7th–10th-century China by government officials. [\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson198813-1) In modern times, there have been revival movements and important political figures that have observed them continuously.

The first five of the eight precepts are similar to the [five precepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_precepts), that is, to refrain from killing living beings, stealing, damaging speech, and to abstain from intoxicating drink or drugs, [\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-BBC-2) but the third precept is [abstinence of all sexual activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmacharya) instead of refraining from sexual offenses. [\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-3) The final three precepts are to abstain from eating at the wrong time (after midday); to abstain from entertainment such as dancing, singing, music, watching shows, as well as to abstain from wearing garlands, perfumes, cosmetics, and personal adornments; and to abstain from luxurious seats and beds. [\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeown200422-4) [\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGetz2004673-5)
To summarise, following anthropologist [Barend Jan Terwiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barend_Jan_Terwiel)'s translation from the [Pāli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali) language used in Thai ceremonies:
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from taking life
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from taking what is not given
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from sexual misconduct
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from false speech
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from intoxicants which cause a careless frame of mind
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from taking food at the wrong time
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from dancing, music, visiting shows, flowers, make-up, the wearing of ornaments and decorations
1. I undertake [to observe] the rule of abstinence from a tall, high sleeping place. [\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETerwiel2012191-6)
In Thailand, when the eight precepts are taken, it is believed that if one of them is broken, they are all broken. [\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETerwiel2012201-7) In the Pāli tradition, the precepts are described in the *Dhammika Sutta* , part of the [Sutta-Nipāta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutta_Nipata). [\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETachibana199265-8) In many medieval Chinese texts, the order of the last three items is different, with numbers 6 and 8 switched. [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcBride2014Buddhist_Rituals-9) [\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETeiser2003150,_n.40-10)

#### Purpose

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eight_precepts&action=edit&section=2)]
The *maechi* in Thailand observe the eight precepts all the time as part of their way of life.
In the context of *uposatha* practice, observing the eight precepts is described by the Buddha in the early texts as "cleansing of the sullied mind through expedient means" ( [Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_language): *upakkamena* ). [\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTESchonthal200655-11) The [Pāli texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_Canon) describe that one undertakes the eight precepts on the observance days following the example of the [enlightened disciples of the Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arhat). [\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWitanachchi2003555-12) [\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETachibana199267-13) In the early texts, the Buddha is described as drawing a distinction between the Buddhist and [Jain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain) ways of upholding the *uposatha* . The Jain way is criticized as being more focused on outward appearance than substance, and the Buddhist practice is dubbed as genuine moral discipline. [\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTESchonthal200654-14) The eight precepts are meant to give lay people an impression of what it means to live as a monastic, [\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellLopez2013A%E1%B9%A3%E1%B9%AD%C4%81%E1%B9%85gasamanv%C4%81gata%E1%B9%83_upav%C4%81sa%E1%B9%83-15) [\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETachibana199266-16) and the precepts "may function as the thin end of a wedge for attracting some to monastic life". [\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWhitakerSmith2018Ethics_(s%C4%ABla)-17) People who are observing the eight precepts are sometimes also addressed differently. [\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETerwiel2012192-18) [\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGombrich199578-19) The objective of the eight precepts is different from the five in that they are less moral in nature, but more focused on developing [meditative concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samadhi), and preventing distractions. [\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey200087-20) Indeed, in Sri Lanka, lay devotees observing the eight precepts are expected to spend much time and effort on meditation, focusing especially on [meditation on the parts of the body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patikulamanasikara). This is intended to develop detachment. [\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGombrich1995314,_329-21)

#### Practice

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eight_precepts&action=edit&section=3)]
Buddhist lay devotees observe the eight precepts often during yearly festivals such as [Vesak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesak).
On regular observance days, Buddhist lay devotees often observe the eight precepts. In that context, the eight precepts are also called the *uposatha vows* (Sanskrit and Pali: *upavāsa* ; Sanskrit: *poṣadhaśīla* , Pali: *uposatha-sīla* ). [\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeown2004Uposatha-22) [\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellLopez2013A%E1%B9%A3%E1%B9%AD%C4%81%E1%B9%85gasamanv%C4%81gata%E1%B9%83_upav%C4%81sa%E1%B9%83-15) When laypeople stay in a Buddhist monastery [\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-BBC-2) or go on a meditation retreat, [\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTESurinrutAuamnoySangwatanaroj2017650-23) they also observe the eight precepts often; they are also upheld during yearly festivals such as [Vesak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesak). [\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVithararta1990230%E2%80%9331-24) [\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellLopez2013Baguan_zhai-25) Presently, the *uposatha* vows are mostly associated with [Theravāda Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) in South and Southeast Asia, [\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-BBC-2) but it was a widespread practice in China as well, [\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellLopez2013Baguan_zhai-25) and is still practiced. [\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey200088-26) In practice, in Theravāda traditions, the precepts are mostly observed by faithful devotees above 40 years of age. [\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey200087-20) Since the eight precepts are often observed for one day, they are also known as the *one-day precepts* . [\[27\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellLopez2013Upav%C4%81sa-27) Sometimes a formula is recited confirming the observance for one day (and one night):
"I undertake to observe in harmony during this day and this night these eight precepts that have been designed by the wisdom of the Buddha." [\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETerwiel2012191-6)
Observance does not need to be temporary, however: some lay devotees choose to undertake the eight precepts continuously to improve themselves in morality. [\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGetz2004673-5) The eight precepts are also undertaken by people preparing for [ordination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upasampada) as a monk, sometimes called *anagarika* in Pāli or *pha khao* in Thai. [\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGosling198462-28) Furthermore, many nuns in Buddhist countries, such as the *mae chi* in Thailand or the *dasa sil mata* in Sri Lanka, observe the eight or ten precepts all the time as part of their way of life. [\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-29) [\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-30)
Among the eight precepts, the first precept is about not killing animals. As recorded in the [Edicts of Aśoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka), there was a custom that he had established not to kill animals on the *uposatha* days, which indicates that by this time observance of Buddhist *uposatha* had become a state institution in India. The custom was most strictly observed on the full moon and the day following. [\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrzyluski1936387-31) The third precept is about maintaining chastity. Buddhist tradition therefore requires lay people to be chaste on observance days, which is similar to the historical Indian tradition of being chaste on *parvan* days. As for the sixth rule, this means not having food after midday, with an allowance for fluids, in imitation of a [nearly identical rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patimokkha#Pacittiya) for monks. [\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETerwiel2012201%E2%80%9302-32) [\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey200087-20) Physician Ming-Jun Hung and his co-authors have analyzed early and medieval Chinese Buddhist Texts and argue that the main purposes of the half-day fast is to lessen desire, improve fitness and strength, and decrease sleepiness. [\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHungKuoChen2002-33) Historically, Chinese Buddhists have interpreted the eight precepts as including vegetarianism. [\[34\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson198813%E2%80%9314-34)
The seventh precept is sometimes also interpreted to mean not wearing colorful clothes, which has led to a tradition for people to wear plain white when observing the eight precepts. [\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey200087-20) [\[35\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellLopez2013Ugraparip%E1%B9%9Bcch%C4%81-35) This does not necessarily mean, however, that a Buddhist devotee dressed in white is observing the eight precepts all the time. [\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETerwiel2012187,_n.16-36) As for the eighth precept, not sitting or sleeping on luxurious seats or beds, this usually comes down to sleeping on a mat on the floor. Though not specified in the precepts themselves, in Thailand and China, people observing the precepts usually stay in the temple overnight. This is to prevent temptations at home to break the eight precepts, and helps foster the community effort in upholding the precepts. [\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-37)

#### History

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eight_precepts&action=edit&section=4)]
According to ethicist [Damien Keown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damien_Keown), the eight precepts were derived from the regulations described in the *Brahmajala Sutta* , an [Early Buddhist Text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Buddhist_Texts). Since in this [discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutra) the Buddha describes his own behavior, Keown argues that the eight precepts and several other moral doctrines in Buddhism are derived from the Buddha as a model figure. [\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeown201628%E2%80%9331-38)
Religion scholar J. H. Bateson and Pāli scholar Shundō Tachibana have argued that the eight precepts may be partly based on pre-Buddhist [brahmanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmanical) practices ( *vrata* ) during the fast on the full and new moon, [\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTETachibana199265-8) [\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBateson1912836-39) but more recent scholarship has suggested that early Buddhist and Jain *uposatha* practices did not originate in Brahmanism. The brahmin *poṣadha* was held in preparation for a sacrifice, whereas the Buddhist and Jain practices were not. Also, according to some scholars, Brahmanism did not migrate to the early Buddhist region till some time after the advent of Buddhism. Instead, Asian religion scholar Benjamin Schonthal and religion scholar Christian Haskett suggest that the Buddhist and Jain practice originate from a common, informal *sāmaṇa* culture, *sāmaṇa* (Pāli; [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): *śrāmaṇa* ) referring to the non- [Vedic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veda) religious movement current at the time of [early Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-sectarian_Buddhism) and Jainism. They base their argument on textual evidence that Jain and other *samana* also upheld *uposatha* practices. Finally, an earlier, less well-known theory by Indologist [Jean Przyluski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Przyluski) proposes a Babylonian origin. Przyluski argued that the lunar calendar followed in Buddhist *uposatha* practice was more likely to be based on [Neo-Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Babylonian) influence than Vedic, based on the distribution of observance days. [\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-40)
Early Buddhist texts relate that the Buddhist *uposatha* originated as a response to other contemporary mendicant sects. Specifically, in the Pāli texts of [monastic discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinaya), King [Bimbisāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimbisara) requests the Buddha to establish an *uposatha* practice, to keep up with competing sects. [\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTESchonthal200656-41) [\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrzyluski1936386-42) The Buddha then has the monks assemble every [fortnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortnight), and later he also has the monks teach lay people and recite the [monastic code of discipline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patimokkha) on the same days. Many of these practices were consciously borrowed from other *sāmaṇa* sects, as the *uposatha* ceremony became part of a wide program by the Buddha to make the spiritual practice of his followers "unique, disciplined and sincere". [\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTESchonthal200657,_60-43)
In 6th-century Korea, the eight precepts came to be associated with worship of [Maitreya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maitreya), due to the work of Hyeryang, a Korean monk that wrote a tract about these matters. [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMcBride2014Buddhist_Rituals-9) In 7th–10th-century China, government officials would often observe the eight precepts for one or more months a year, during which they often invited monks to teach them at home. On the same months that were designated for such religious observance, called the *chai* , the government also refrained from executing death penalties. [\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWatson198813-1)

##### Modern history

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Eight_precepts&action=edit&section=5)]
In 19th-century Sri Lanka, there was a revival of observing the eight precepts due to the influence of [Anagarika Dharmapala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anagarika_Dharmapala).
In the late 19th century in Sri Lanka, there was a renewed interest in the tradition of observing the eight precepts, during the time of the [Buddhist revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism_in_Sri_Lanka#The_Buddhist_revival). This was mostly because of the influence of [Anagarika Dharmapāla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anagarika_Dharmapala) (1864–1933), who observed the ten precepts (similar to the eight) continuously, maintaining a status between lay person and monk. [\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey2013378%E2%80%9379-44) The interest was further fostered by campaigns emphasizing Buddhist religious practice on [traditional observance days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poya). [\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBloss19878-45) The Thai politician [Chamlong Srimuang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chamlong_Srimuang) (b. 1935) has been known for observing the eight precepts continuously, even during his life as a politician. He has been a member of the Buddhist [Santi Asoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santi_Asoke) movement, which interprets the eight precepts as eating one vegetarian meal a day. Srimuang's strict life following the precepts has led his friends to call him "half monk–half man". [\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeyes1989319%E2%80%9320-46) Just like the Santi Asoke, the Thai [Dhammakaya Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhammakaya_Temple) emphasizes eight precepts, especially in their training programs. [\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFuengfusakul1993157-47) In Sri Lanka, in the 2000s, the eight precepts were still observed with great strictness, as was noticed by Religion scholar Jonathan Walters in his field research. [\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWalters2010131-48) In Theravāda traditions in the West, the eight precepts are observed as well. [\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_precepts#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGomes200449-49)
