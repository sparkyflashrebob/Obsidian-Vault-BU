##### Key concepts

* The dasa sila (ten precepts) refers to a code of conduct to help members of the sangha live in an ethical way. The word ‘precepts’ can be understood variously as ‘guidance’, ‘vows’, ‘trainings’, ‘good practice’, ‘aspirations’ etc., and the way in which they are observed depends on what members of the sangha are involved. The dasa sila can be viewed as a practical application of the Eightfold Path.
* Buddhism encourages personal ethical responsibility through avoiding or choosing actions that are (un)skilful, (in)appropriate and (un)fruitful. Therefore, Buddhists interpret and apply the dasa sila in different ways. Hiri (shame) might be felt when breaking a precept (this is not the same as guilt). Ottappa (complete awareness) with regard to intentions/actions needs to be used in applying the sila.

##### Key quotes

‘Healthy self-esteem comes from living up to a set of standards that are practical, clear-cut, humane, and worthy of respect; the five precepts are formulated in such a way that they provide just such a set of standards.’ (Thanisarro Bhikku)
‘Buddhist ethics are not codified into a rigid moral code; nor are they about making judgements and arousing sin and guilt.’ (Snelling)

* The ten precepts are taken by the monastic sangha and are seen as training rules particularly for novice bhikkus and bhikkunis. Lay Buddhists might choose to observe the first eight precepts – precept seven and eight are combined as one precept – as a part of intensive Buddhist meditation and practice and during Uposatha Days (festival days determined by the lunar calendar). All Buddhists would undertake to follow the first five precepts which are called the panca sila. They are: (1) I undertake the precept to refrain from destroying living creatures; (2) I undertake the precept to refrain from taking that which is not given; (3) I undertake the precept to refrain from sexual misconduct; (4) I undertake the precept to refrain from incorrect speech; (5) I undertake the precept to refrain from intoxicating drinks and drugs which lead to carelessness; (6) I undertake the precept to refrain from eating at the forbidden time (i.e., after midday); (7) I undertake the precept to refrain from dancing, singing, music, going to see entertainments; (8) I undertake the precept to refrain from wearing garlands, using perfumes, and beautifying the body with cosmetics; (9) I undertake the precept to refrain from lying on a high or luxurious sleeping place; (10) I undertake the precept to refrain from accepting gold and silver (money).

##### Issues for analysis and evaluation

Key arguments/debates
The general focus is on whether the dasa sila - in its different forms - are useful as ethical principles for the community of Buddhist believers both monastic and lay.
Key questions
To what extent should the principles of the dasa sila be seen as rules/commandments or as general human principles guiding conduct?
How far may the dasa sila be seen as uniting the monastic and lay sanghas through the observance of the 5/8/10 precepts?

* The monastic sangha consists of novices, bhikkus, and, in some schools of Buddhism, bhikkunis. The monastic sangha, when found in viharas and temple complexes, is particularly important for heritage Buddhists and in some parts of the world is an integral part of local communities. Here, for example, bhikkus might provide prayers at ceremonies connected to weddings and funerals, guidance both spiritual and practical, and teaching. The monastic sangha would be seen by many Buddhists as the conserver and upholder of Buddhist beliefs and practice.
* The lay sangha consists of the laity/non-ordained Buddhists. Depending on the school of Buddhism and whether they are mainly heritage or convert Buddhists, lay Buddhists contribute to the upkeep of the viharas and temple complexes, and the life of bhikkus/ bhikkunis. Lay Buddhists would be involved in collecting donations and funding the monastic sangha.
* The relationship between the two sanghas is a complementary one – they are interdependent and each supports the other. They share the same code of conduct but in different ways.
