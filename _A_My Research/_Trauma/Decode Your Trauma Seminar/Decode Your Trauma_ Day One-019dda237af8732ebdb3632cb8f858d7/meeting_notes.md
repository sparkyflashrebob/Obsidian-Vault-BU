### Definition and Nature of Trauma
- Trauma is not defined solely by the events that occur, as two people can experience the same event and have entirely different outcomes.
- Neither is trauma defined purely by internal responses, as these vary across individuals and over time.
- Trauma is understood as a four-stage sequential process, beginning with events and ending with outcomes, with critical stages occurring in between.

### The ECHO Model
- The acronym ECHO represents the four stages of trauma and also reflects the concept that past experiences echo into present life.
- Each stage of the model will be explored across the five days, with one stage addressed per session from day two onward.
- Events, the first stage, encompass both overt traumas such as abuse or parental addiction, and covert traumas such as growing up neurodiverse in a neurotypical family without recognition or support.
- Context, the second stage, refers to how well three core emotional needs are met during childhood, specifically the needs for boundaries, safety, and love.
- Boundaries relate to the capacity to assert personal limits and commitments, forming the foundation for willpower, personal strength, and the ability to persist through difficulty.
- Safety refers to the experience of co-regulation with caregivers, which becomes the internal foundation for self-regulation and the ability to settle when triggered in adult life.
- Love in this context means being valued for who one is rather than for achievements, forming the basis of self-esteem and self-worth.
- Homeostasis, the third stage, describes the shift in nervous system balance that occurs when events overload a person and core emotional needs are unmet, leading to a maladaptive stress response.
- Fight, flight, freeze, and fawn responses become normalised over time, with fawning described as an ultra people-pleasing pattern adopted to seek safety.
- A healthy stress response involves a temporary activation followed by a return to calm, whereas a maladaptive stress response keeps the nervous system in a state of chronic dysregulation.
- Outcomes, the fourth stage, include anxiety, depression, numbness, loss of meaning, self-medicating behaviours, and attachment difficulties such as anxious or avoidant patterns.
- Conventional medical and therapeutic approaches are noted to often focus on outcomes while overlooking the role of unmet core emotional needs and nervous system dysregulation.

### Trauma Across the Lifespan
- Trauma is not limited to childhood and can occur at any point in life, including through loss, grief, or chronic illness.
- How an individual responds to adult trauma is largely shaped by patterns and defences developed during childhood.
- A case example from the YouTube series "In Therapy" illustrates how unresolved childhood grief compounded an adult's experience of loss, resulting in emotional shutdown rather than the ability to grieve.

### Possibility of Healing
- Change is presented as entirely possible because the responses associated with trauma are learned behaviours that have become habituated over time.
- Just as maladaptive patterns were learned, new ways of regulating the nervous system and meeting core emotional needs can also be learned.
- A structured map, practical tools, and a clear framework are identified as the essential components needed to support this process of change.
