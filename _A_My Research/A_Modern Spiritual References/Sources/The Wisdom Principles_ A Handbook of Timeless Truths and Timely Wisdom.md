by Deepak Chopra
If you stand back for a moment, you will see that we are living now on a shaky twig branching off from the tree of wisdom. Modern people naturally don’t generally see it that way. The word “wisdom” is loosely used without investing it with the importance it deserves. In this regard Ervin Laszlo comes at a pivotal point, rescuing the honor of wisdom by revealing what it actually is.
Wisdom isn’t the same as education, learning, expertise, skill, long experience, or any other synonym. The best way to describe it is through direct experience. You touch the core of wisdom when you act on intuition and insight, when you have an “aha” moment, and when you suddenly know, without a doubt, what is true. Imagine that the active mind is like the surface of a river tossed by the wind into waves and eddies. Beneath the surface the currents grow calmer, and at the river’s lowest depth, the water is almost still. This is the level where wisdom is encountered.
To put it simply, knowledge is the product of thinking, but wisdom is a state of awareness. The reason we live on a shaky twig today is that the secular world recklessly tossed out wisdom as somehow undesirable. It became superficially identified with tired, old, sententious talkers, self-styled sages expending hot air. (Perhaps it took the decline of sermonizing to clear the way for renewing what wisdom really is.)
There are distilled gems of wisdom in this book, the fruit of the author’s decades of reflection, with the underlying purpose of reaching a vision of life. In this vision, wisdom is the best use of the human mind, and the guidance of wisdom creates the best life anyone could lead. I am writing this foreword during the dire days of the coronavirus pandemic, when two movements have sprung up around the world. One is the movement fueled by fear and panic. The other is fueled by wanting to create a new and better world.
Ervin Laszlo is speaking here and has spoken on behalf of the movement for renewal in all of his books. What sets his vision apart is that it isn’t based on issues. Crises face us on all sides, and all have been subjected to the
knowledge of experts, vast pressure for change, and the glaring need for such change. Yet so far, all the understanding, outcries, goodwill, and the rightness of a cause have been ineffectual. If we ask ourselves why, the mind answers at the three levels of the river mentioned above.
The restless active mind struggles against obstacles, blames the bad faith of reactionaries, prays for the good to prevail, and forever laments that the problems are too overwhelming to solve. Beneath the surface, the calmer mind sets about with diligence and sobriety to find solutions and act on them. At the very depth of the mind, however, wisdom has a different response. It recognizes that the world is going through an identity crisis. Until we ask “Who am I?” and come up with a better answer, the same old stories will be recycled between victim and oppressor, the forces of light and dark, the lost battle of the powerless against the powerful.
“Who am I?” is the critical wisdom question that determines your state of being. The wisest answer, based on the highest level of your being, is this: “I am a finite field of pure consciousness, unbounded, eternal, blissful, and self-fulfilling.” The words that issue from this state of being are not divine, unworldly, impossibly pure, or suitable only for a saint or yogi. Because every mind is fundamentally wise once you reach the right level. Wisdom is a universal condition, and by far the most joyful one to live in.
The fact that we are perched on a shaky twig right now will change when we stop identifying with the restless active mind. One way to grasp the meaning of this comes from reading this book, because the writing takes you to the level where your own wisdom exists. You are put into contact with yourself. You will experience “Who am I?” differently almost at once.
I am reminded of a lovely anecdote from the life of a modern Indian guru in Mumbai. He led a simple life above a shop, and although he was revered by his devotees, the same devotees felt free to vent their resentments and complaints. One day the guru was entertaining a devotee who became impatient, because their lunch hadn’t been brought up yet. “I don’t see why you and I are so different,” the devotee grumbled. “We’re just two hungry old men sitting above a shop wanting our lunch.”
“Here is the difference,” the guru calmly replied. “You are just a hungry old man waiting for your lunch. Your world is bounded by your moods and whims, your ever-shifting desires and dissatisfactions. Your world is private, and no one else can enter it.
“But my world is unbounded. I see openness and light in all directions.
My being is the Being of all living things, of existence itself. All are welcome in my world, and when they enter, they experience the bliss of pure awareness.”
I find it hard not to shed tears reading this story, as I have done dozens of times. Bliss can touch us like heartache, because it must push aside the sorrows created by the ego. But the story is beautiful at the same time. We’ve all heard it said, “What you don’t know can’t hurt you,” but in the case of wisdom, the saying should be “What you don’t know can change your life.” Wisdom is that precious thing inside us that we don’t know until we touch it, and then it changes us completely.
In that spirit, I invite you to read Ervin Laszlo’s new book The Wisdom Principles in a particular way. Relish the writer’s thoughts and words but at the same time be open and willing to meet yourself. Wisdom can inspire you when it belongs to someone else; it can transform you only through direct experience. By opening this Handbook of Timeless Truths and Timely Wisdom, you open the door to your new identity, because across the threshold there is only the lightness of Being, and infinity in all directions.
INTRODUCTION
by Neale Donald Walsch
What you are about to experience is a deep look at how we might meet some of the biggest challenges and solve some of the major problems facing humanity today. Because this could have a major impact on your own personal daily experience, not just the world at large, I hope you will jump into this exploration immediately.
You will find more than wisdom here. You will find a way out of the maze, a pathway from the box canyon in which we seem to have marched ourselves.
I wrote in a book that I published several years ago that 98 percent of the world’s people are spending 98 percent of their time on things that don’t matter. The book you are now reading reminds us in wonderfully direct ways of what does matter, and how we can step into the living of that.
Let me put this all into context, if I may. The challenge we are now facing on Earth is that nothing is working. I’m sure you’re seeing this. The world’s presently-in-place political systems, economic systems, social systems, and even our spiritual systems are failing us right and left. None of them have been successful in producing for the largest number of us the outcomes they were intended to produce. And yet the people in power within those systems continue to seek support for their systems from the largest number of us.
We can’t continue to offer it. We can’t. It is no longer viable for us to support, much less perpetuate, an approach to the creation of our future that we can see is leading us not into the days of our dreams, but deeper into a collective nightmare from which most of the world doesn’t seem to know how to awaken.
I perceive that Ervin Laszlo is a person who does. He’s an awakener. A way-shower. A guide. An escort on the journey of life upon which billions have embarked without studying the map.
I believe it is important to listen to thinkers such as this. Not because I think that Ervin Laszlo and others to whom he points may know more than we all do. (They’d be the last ones to suggest that.) But because with the road
maps they offer, we can find our way back to our own innermost awareness, our own highest understandings, and our own most expanded level of consciousness. Therein, of course, lies the treasure.
Whether we agree or disagree with all that is said and proposed here, we can know why and how we agree or disagree—and that, by itself, can be important markers on our own trek through the woods.
Ervin Laszlo inspires us, in the remarkable excursion into clarity that is this book, to see that the opportunity before us to advance in realizing our highest potential as a civilization is now exhilaratingly available and totally within our reach. The assertion here is that this is true during this time of— and actually because of—the calamitous events being visited upon us in this epoch of our history.

He does not present this as an indictment, but as a gentle invitation to daringly and dynamically move forward in creating the way we choose to be, in part by looking back at the way we used to be. He suggests that we knew something inherently—something important—that we seem to have forgotten. He then explores, in wonderful depth, exactly what this is.
What we’re given in this book, then, is not just a look at the wisdom of our yesterdays, but also at the promise of our tomorrows.
What we need now are some expansive new ideas, and that’s what Ervin Laszlo and his friends Maria Sagi and Kingsley Dennis offer here. “New” not in the sense of never having been heard before, but in the sense of never having been tried before. Not on a wholesale, global basis. Not with complete and unwavering, species-wide commitment.
Chief among these not-yet-undertaken approaches is to embrace, conceptually and functionally, the conviction that we are all made of the same stuff. That we are all one, and indeed, that all things are one thing, manifesting differently.
What is fascinating about this idea is that we don’t have to agree on what the one thing is. We could call it God. We could call it the Higher Power or life’s Organizing Principle. We could call it the Essential Essence, or simply Pure Energy. We could call it the Cosmic Attractor—the universe’s intrinsic impulse toward wholeness and oneness. Or call it, simply, Love.
There is an implicit question found in the folds of what is offered in this book. What if we jointly decided—that is, what if humanity as a family collectively agreed—that we all really are nothing more (and nothing less) than an individuation of that One Thing? And what if we could find a way to inspire that idea to be the evolutionary driver of our species?
Do you think it would make any difference in the way we treat one another on our planet today? Do you think it would better equip us, and offer us more effective tools with which to meet the tumultuous events of this moment in our collective lives?

PRINCIPLES OF TIMELESS TRUTH: A GUIDE TO WISE
There are principles of wise thinking: principles that are of timeless truth. They have been known to countless generations of sages and philosophers and spiritual people. They pose and suggest answers to the biggest questions of life. Their relevance to our own life has never been as great as it is today, when we face the challenge of finding our way beyond the crisis of the old world, toward a new world where we can all live and thrive.
THE BIGGEST OF THE BIG QUESTIONS: WHERE DID WE COME FROM? WHY ARE WE
These are perhaps the biggest of the big questions thinking people have ever asked about themselves and the world around them. Today we can offer a better, more reliable answer to them than we could before.
WHERE DID WE COME FROM?
When it comes to inquiring into our deepest origins, evolutionary science joins mystical intuition to give the same basic answer. We were not created as we are now: we have evolved into it. But the concept of where we had evolved from is different in science and in mysticism. It is different in explication and formulation, though not in meaning.
According to quantum cosmology, we have evolved from the primordial “ground state” of the universe. In quantum cosmology, this state is best seen as a sea of coherent vibration. The manifest universe is the ensemble of the vibrations that emerged in this sea. The emergent vibrations “excited” the quantum sea: they brought it into what physicists describe as the excited state. The excited state is the basis for all possible vibrations in the quantum sea. These are given in potential; and in the excited state some of the vibrations become actualized. The clusters of vibrations that constitute the observable universe are vibrations in the excited state of the quantum sea: they are the manifest state of the universe.
This abstract physics-assessment of the origins of “things” in the universe has been intuitively anticipated in the wisdom traditions. The creation stories of the world’s spiritual systems speak of a primordial domain out of which the manifest had emerged. In some Eastern metaphysics, this domain is a cosmic egg, whereas in the Old Testament it is a dark and formless sea—a void. “In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth; and the earth was without form, and void; and darkness [was] upon the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters.” (Genesis 1:1–2)
From this spaceless and timeless void God created light, the firmament, day and night, and all the things that have come to exist the universe.
This account of our cosmic origins is also given in Taoist cosmology. There it is said that all things originate in the Tao, and all things return to the Tao. The Tao is both the source and destination of all things. It is not observable, and not even nameable. It can be defined only negatively, by what it is not. As the literature of Tao emphasizes, Tao cannot be seen by our eyes. Tao cannot be heard by our ears. Tao cannot be touched by our hands.
The recognition of a primordial ground occurs and recurs also in the history of philosophy. Plato called the ground the sphere of Forms and Ideas, and identified it as the seat of the Soul. The Hellenic philosophers gave it various names: Pythagoras called it “Kosmos,” and Plotinus “The One.” In our time, quantum physicist David Bohm identified it as the “implicate” (enfolded) order, the seat and origin of the manifest world: the “explicate” (unfolded) order. We live in the explicate order, but this order, and all things in it, is shaped and molded—as Bohm would say, “in-formed”—by the implicate order.
The implicate order is the long-intuited and now rediscovered deep dimension of the world. We, as all things in the manifest “explicate” order, originate as in-formed clusters of coherent vibration in the excited state of the quantum sea that manifests as the universe.
This is the answer of cutting-edge cosmology to the query regarding our origins. In the perspective of spirituality, we come from the nothingness that is also a fullness that preceded and still underlies the manifest universe. This nothingness is the “void” of the Bible, the “Tao” of Eastern mysticism, the “quantum vacuum” of mainstream physics, and the “implicate order” of quantum physics.
The above answers have a major feature in common: they speak of progressive “creation,” or self-evolution, from common origins. The evolution of the world is not a mechanical, preprogrammed sequence of events, but a creative process inspired and in-formed by a fundamental spark: the seed of life, of mind, and of the order of nature. These seeds are potentials, and they were present already at the beginning and became actualized—and continue to be actualized—over the course of time.
The beginning of this process of creative evolution is traced by most cosmologists to the cosmic singularity popularly known as the big bang. This event took place an estimated 13.8 billion years ago. It injected staggering
energies into the preceding ground state of the universe, “excited” it, and gave rise to the formative processes that created quarks and quanta, atoms and crystals, molecules, stars, stellar systems and galaxies, and living organisms and ecologies of such organisms on Earth—and conceivably on milliards of other planets orbiting energy-radiating active stars in this galaxy and in countless others.
WHY ARE WE HERE?
I am alive, I live day after day. Until one day I live no more. Why did I live— why do I live now? Is there a meaning or reason for my life—for life altogether?
Of course, it may be that there is no need to spend time on the question of the meaning of life, since the answer is simple and straightforward. There is no higher or deeper meaning: the meaning of life is just to be alive. That would accord with what we surmise about the experience of other living things around us. When spring comes, grass grows and birds sing and they don’t worry about deeper meaning. They don’t ask why they are alive. Perhaps they are alive just to live. Life itself is a great miracle and being alive could be an end in itself. My best friend, Nikki, greets me in the morning, we look each other in the eye, and we go for a walk. He is happy. He doesn’t ask what his life means. Perhaps he would if he could, and he would say: to live and to be happy. But in his present condition as a dog, he cannot ask.
But you and I, who have been born as humans, we can ask. Is there a deeper meaning for our being alive, and if there is, what is that meaning? I cannot with good conscience disregard and dismiss this question. It may be that it is by more than by a lucky fluke that I have been born somebody who can ask this question. Perhaps the capacity for asking it is the answer. Could it be that I have been born a human being so I could ask what it means to live?
On a purely pragmatic level, asking about the meaning of life is an exercise in sophistry. It doesn’t contribute to my physical existence on the planet. To stay alive as a biological being I don’t need to contemplate sophisticated questions posed by an evolved consciousness. But I have such a consciousness, and I also have the capacity to use it. Is this just a fortunate (or perhaps not so fortunate?) consequence of my being at a relatively advanced stage of evolution? Pragmatic thinkers often say that consciousness
is an accidental by-product of the workings of the brain as it seeks to ensure the organism’s survival and reproduction. Could they be right—could my evolved consciousness be a mere side effect of my biological existence?
Here we come to the end of the wisdom offered by the physical and biological sciences. If my consciousness is something more, or at any rate something other, than a side effect of the workings of my brain, that something is no longer a physical and biological phenomenon. Consciousness beyond the level where it serves the functions of survival and reproduction must be more than the by-product of a survival-and-reproduction-serving brain. If there is such a “something more” it would not be produced by my brain, and would not be limited to my brain. It would be something that my brain receives and processes, and perhaps transmits. But not something that it creates.
In contemplating this possibility, we pass from the physical-biological concept of wisdom to the level of a higher wisdom. The wisdom we can derive from a consciousness that is a side effect of survival-and-reproduction-oriented cerebral functioning is strictly pragmatic: How do I ensure that I have enough to eat, have a place where I can rest, and find a mate with whom I can reproduce? Important, even essential as these queries may be, my brain-created consciousness cannot produce anything beyond them; the rest, in its perspective, is but imagination and sophistry.
There may be a higher wisdom in the world. And that wisdom derives from a consciousness that exists beyond the brain—one that my brain can capture and process, and ultimately transmit. This is the wisdom offered by the great religions and spiritual systems of the world, and it is supported by the new paradigm emerging at the cutting edge of the contemporary sciences.
THE MIRACLE OF “EVERY-THING”
There are just two ways to live your life, Einstein is said to have told us: as if nothing is a miracle, or as if everything is.
The wise way to live your life is as if everything is a miracle. Because everything is. This is the logical and valid conclusion from the insights we gain from the quantum sciences regarding the ultimate nature of the world. Everything—more exactly, every “thing”—is astoundingly coherent. It could not exist if it was not. A “thing” is not a bit or a combination of bits of material things. It is a coherent cluster of vibration in the ground state of the universe. If it was not coherent, it would not be a thing: it would not exist. It would be an indistinguishable part of a non-thing-like sea of oceanic vibration. What there is is a cluster of coherent vibration where the component vibrations share the same frequency, are in phase, and have a compatible magnitude. Only on this condition can the vibrations constitute “things.” Whether they are truly things is not the question. The fact is that only in this condition do they appear thing-like to us.
Where is the miracle in this? The miracle is that every-thing we can perceive is coherent: the universe is full, actually composed, of coherent “things.” This is the miracle that sages have been contemplating for ages: the miracle of there being “some thing” in the world rather than “no-thing.”
The universe is a coherent “thing” made up of other coherent “things.” More precisely, it is a coherent supermacrovibration composed of coherent meso- and microvibrations. This is a miracle. If the universe were a passive, soulless mechanism, as the mainstream physics of the modern age has envisaged, it would be a random sea of incoherent vibrations. There would be “no-thing” in it. But now there are many “things” in it, and if not arbitrarily subverted, they prove to be highly, indeed amazingly, coherent. This truly is a miracle. Ours is a miraculously coherent universe.
THE MIRACLE OF COHERENCE
Coherence is a state of being throughout the sphere of life; it hallmarks all species and populations and ecologies. It is not unique to the sphere of life; it also characterizes the physics of the universe. The universe is coherent from the smallest assembly of quanta to the vastest structure of galaxies. If the universe was not so coherent, it could not have evolved from its initial semichaotic structure into the supreme architecture it exhibits today.
We have gained a better understanding of the nature of the universe, and of the things that emerge and evolve in the universe. We now know that the universe is not a mechanical aggregate of atoms and molecules. The systems that appear in it are not lifeless chunks of matter but coevolving, interlinked systems of energy and information. In all its domains, the universe is coherent, and ultimately, it is one. This is a timeless insight, and its relevance to the world around us is becoming more and more evident.
We are an integral part of a coherent universe, linked in countless ways to one another and to our planet. In a remarkable resurgence of interest in timeless wisdom, more and more people are rediscovering this perennial insight.
With the new discoveries in physics, cosmology, and the life sciences, our very identity is changing. We are recognizing that we are not skin-enclosed, mechanistic parts in a vast machinelike universe, but highly evolved elements in an organic, evolving, quasi-living cosmos. Nature’s penchant toward structure, order, and complexity has reached a pinnacle in us, unparalleled on this planet, and possibly in this entire region of the galaxy.
We may not be the only, or even the most evolved, beings in the universe, but we are surely one of the comparatively evolved ones. Thanks to our brain and consciousness, we are an eye through which the universe is coming to know itself. This is a unique privilege: it allows us to rediscover ourselves in the natural order of things. And when we rediscover ourselves, we can comprehend our role in the order of things—we can seek to regain our coherence with one another, and with the whole universe.
HOW COHERENCE COULD EMERGE IN THE WORLD
The miracle is that the universe we inhabit is coherent—coherent enough to be comprehensible. Why is it not just an unordered heap of unrelated things? How did coherence emerge in the world? That the coherent order we find in the universe would have come about casually is extremely improbable. We don’t need to resort to that serendipity: there is an explanation in reference to the data and the observations that surface in science. This is not a full and final explanation, but as far as an explanation based on the now available data and observation can go.
The probability that the kind of order we find in the universe would have come about through random interactions among the things that emerged in space and time is nearly nil. Yet this is precisely what mainstream physicists have been asserting until quite recently. In recent years, however, with the advent of powerful computation and more wide-ranging and precise observation, it turned out that the order exhibited in the universe is so complex and precise that random processes would take longer than the age of the universe (which is presumably about 13.8 billion years, the time that elapsed since the big bang).
The order exhibited in the universe is highly sensitive to alteration. A change amounting to no more than one-billionth of the value of the mass of the proton would change the rate of the expansion of the universe and make impossible the evolution of enduring systems such as stars, and thus prevent the creation of enduring flows of energy from “hot” stars to their planets. This would make the universe a lifeless sphere of physical processes.
But if mere chance isn’t responsible for the coherence that makes the universe comprehensible—and life in it possible—then what is? Must we assume that it is due to the design of a higher intelligence? Not necessarily. There is design behind the evolution of the universe, but it may not be design exercised by an extra-universal, transcendental Being. It could be, and most plausibly is, not a consciously injected design, but a system of laws that together introduce nonrandomness into otherwise random processes. In
science, this nonrandom effect of the laws of nature constitutes what is called a systemic “attractor.”
Attractors are assumed to be present in the behavior of complex systems that manifest some form of order. Systems “governed” by attractors tend toward some specifiable state or condition in preference to others. These can be recurring states, governed by periodic attractors, or a goal state that suggests the presence of a point attractor. Elements of randomness can be mixed with nonrandom elements, as in the case of systems governed by “strange” or “chaotic” attractors.
The attractor at work in the universe appears to be a formative influence that orients the state of the universe toward forms and elements of integration, coherence, and order. This attractor is best described by the term Stanislav Grof used in regard to the orientation of the subconscious mind toward order: “holotropic” (made up of holos—meaning wholeness or whole in Greek—and tropein, meaning oriented in some direction). This attractor works statistically and not as a mechanical principle. Whenever there is an element of freedom in the selection among alternatives, there is a bias built into the universe that selects toward those alternatives that enhance coherence and wholeness.
As a consequence of the action of the holotropic attractor, the systems that evolve in the universe tend to be integral ensembles of their parts: “wholes” and not “heaps.” A whole is distinct from a heap: it is a dynamic entity as contrasted with a random agglomeration of unrelated items. The diverse elements of the whole function together to maintain it in its environment. The paradigm for such a whole is the living organism. Here cells that are often highly diverse among themselves form multicellular systems that complete and complement one another. The manifest course of evolution in the universe indicates that there is an underlying trend in evolution toward the integration of diverse elements in integral cooperative wholes.
This is the evolution Bohm called “holomovement.” It is a progressive movement toward the formation of wholes from parts through coherence among the parts, and between the wholes constituted by the parts and the rest of the universe. The coherence-building process occurs and recurs on multiple levels, on higher and higher scales of order and dimension of size.
The emerging whole systems jointly form supersystems, building the manifest universe into an embracing super-supersystem.
This evolutionary process is based on the minute bias toward coherence that is built into every element of the interconnected system. Just as a butterfly flapping its wings can create a storm through the minute alterations from randomness created in the weather system, so every minute “bias” in the quanta that constitute the basic architecture of the world can create a coherently ordered universe. All that is needed for this to occur is a strongly interconnected system, with not-entirely-random connection among its elements. The dynamic impetus that introduces bias into the system is what we call the holotropic attractor—and the religions and spiritual systems call divine will and cosmic intelligence.
THE EVOLUTION OF LIFE AND THE EVOLUTION OF THE UNIVERSE
The latest advances in science indicate that there is a level and form of coherence in the universe that enables it not only to exist, but to evolve. This is very different from the idea that in the course of time the universe inevitably breaks down and moves toward the final, inert state of maximum entropy. The universe we know today is a dynamic and directionally evolving quasi-living entity. Coherence hallmarks its macrostructures, as well as its microstructures, as they evolve here on Earth.
Could random, purposeless processes have brought the order we find in the universe? This is highly unlikely. Yet it is precisely what Darwinists argue. Oxford biologist Richard Dawkins, for example, said that whereas the living world gives the impression of having been created for a purpose, this is but an illusion. Cheetahs, for example, give every indication of being designed to kill antelopes. The teeth, claws, eyes, nose, leg muscles, backbone, and brain of a cheetah are all precisely what we should expect if God’s purpose in creating cheetahs was to maximize deaths among antelopes. At the same time, antelopes are fast, agile, and watchful, seemingly designed to escape cheetahs. Do these and similar facts argue for intelligent design? They do not, according to Dawkins and other Darwinists. Nature was not designed: its seeming purposefulness is the adaptive evolution of specific “utility functions.” Cheetahs have the utility function to kill antelopes, and antelopes to escape cheetahs. Nature itself is indifferent to their fate. This is a world of blind physical forces and genetic replication, where some get hurt and others flourish. It has precisely the properties we would expect it to have if at bottom there was no design, no purpose, no evil and no good, only blind and pitiless indifference.
However, current calculations indicate that the processes exhibited by living systems are far too complex to be just adaptive responses to the challenges they encounter. There is cooperation, harmony, and order throughout the domains of life, just as it is in the universe at large. Selective bias toward the formation of coherent systems is present already in the
evolution of stars. Recent findings indicate that even at an early evolutionary stage organic molecules form around active stars; entire chains of carbon link up to produce the structures that form the templates in the evolution of living systems.
The universe is coherent as a whole, and it is the ground or template for the evolution of a vast array of complex systems, ranging in size and complexity from atoms to galaxies. The universe had 13.8 billion years to evolve the complex and coherent systems that now meet our eye. It was born in the cosmic singularity known as the big bang, and the evolution of complex and coherent systems began when the primordial fireball cooled off sufficiently for quantum particles to appear. These particles were (and still are) relatively enduring clusters of vibration. They are called leptons (electrons, muons, tau particles, and neutrinos), mesons (pions), and hadrons (baryons, including protons and neutrons). In the course of time they clustered into more complex and ordered structures: the atoms of the elements. They filled the periodic table of the elements, ranging in size and complexity (in “weight”) from hydrogen to uranium, and beyond.
In time, the atoms clustered into molecules and molecular assemblies, as many superclusters of in-formed vibration. On the astronomical level, stars and stellar systems and entire galaxies emerged, and these are clusters and super-superclusters of in-formed vibration.
However, 13.8 billion years for the evolution of physical systems, and four billion years for the evolution of living systems as integrated ensembles of physical systems, are not sufficient to account for the presence of stars and galaxies in space and time and, above all, for the web of life on this planet. The complexity of the DNA-mRNA-tRNA-rRNA transcription and translation system precludes the probability that living systems would have been produced by random processes within the known time frames. We know that in a long-term process almost anything that could happen may actually happen, but the temporal dimension of evolution in the universe would have to be entire dimensions more extensive than the dimension we arrive at through calculation based on observation. Serendipity does not explain the existence of the atoms of the elements, much less the DNA of even the simplest organism.
According to mathematical physicist Fred Hoyle, the probability of a living system having been produced by a random mix of the elements of its genetic pool is similar to the probability that a hurricane blowing through a
scrap yard would assemble a working airplane. That these complex and coherent systems would have come about by a random mixing of their components is astronomically improbable.
The universe we observe and inhabit is both remarkably complex and remarkably coherent. The bottom line is that it is highly improbable that complex and coherent systems such as ourselves would have come about in a random mix of their components. The statistical analysis of the complexity of even relatively simple biological systems tells us that to produce them by a random interaction among their components would take longer than the age of the universe. The universe we observe could not have been created by the serendipity of random interactions.
There is an ordering factor present in space and time. As we said, this factor is best identified as an attractor emerging as the combined effect of the laws of nature. The “holotropic attractor” transforms a passive, randomly functioning universe into a dynamic system that manifests coherence and order, and is therewith comprehensible to the human mind. This is the miracle to which Einstein is said to have referred—and at the same time the simplest explanation of the evolution we observe, and we ourselves exemplify.
Living systems are integrated, coherent systems and not by-products of chance processes. Wherever there is an opportunity to create coherent systems, the universe seizes on it. Life emerges wherever physical-chemical conditions permit the formation of complex coherent structures.
Life is not limited to this planet, or to any other planet; it is the manifest outcome of a universal process. The evolution of stars and galaxies in the universe and the evolution of life on Earth are the same basic process. We are its most tangible, and on this planet also its most remarkable, manifestation.
THE SPIRITUAL IS NOT NECESSARILY FANTASY: IT COULD BE REAL
Phenomena that cannot be explained in reference to the data of our bodily senses are often called “spiritual.” The reality of their existence has been questioned—and in the modern world, mostly dismissed. Now new findings in the physical sciences prompt us to reconsider this out-of-hand dismissal.
So-called spiritual phenomena could be real—a part of the real world. The concept of what the real world actually is has changed fundamentally in recent times. The new concept of reality is not that of a material world: of a world that only exists if and when composed of a physical substance called “matter.” If that were the true nature of the world, phenomena that cannot be explained in reference to matter could not exist—because, as we held, matter is perceivable, and only matter and nothing but matter is perceivable (or at least accountable in reference to its relations to matter) and only the perceivable is real. What we have called “spiritual” phenomena, not being traceable to matter, do not exist. Therewith the debate on the reality of the spiritual, we have said, is closed. The spiritual is merely illusion.
Now we need to rethink this conclusion—for the simple and basic reason that the new world-picture of the sciences does not restrict reality to the material world. There could be events and “realities” in the world that have no material basis. These nonmaterial phenomena exist, even if we do not perceive them in the same way as we perceive material phenomena.
There is a widespread fallacy today in the world: if we do not perceive something, and if we cannot account for it in relation to the perceivable world, we say that it doesn’t exist. This is a fallacy because the real world is far wider and deeper than we have thought. There could be entire dimensions of reality we do not perceive, and yet are real. What we have known as the “spiritual world” could be such a dimension.
The spiritual world may not be intrinsically unperceivable. It may be perceivable, or at least inferable from the perceived world, if we enter a suitable state of consciousness. It is clear that sages and prophets, and insightful people of all kinds, have claimed that they know realities that are
not perceived, and may not be perceivable, by our physical senses. This could be the case, because our senses decode only a small segment of the reality that surrounds us in the real world. The fact that we do not perceive all of this reality does not preclude that we could perceive it if we entered a different, nonordinary state of brain and consciousness. The whole gamut of spiritual experiences, and the entire panoply of beings that inhabit the spiritual world, could prove to be experienceable. This is not to say that it would be perceivable in the orthodox materialist sense, only that it is experienceable in the deeper sense in which psychologists and consciousness researchers speak of experiencing. For this experiencing to occur, we need to enter what is known as an altered state of consciousness. Even if we experienced but a tiny reflection, a veiled indication, of that reality, a whole new dimension of the experienced world would open for us.
The physical reality of this dimension is now warranted by the new paradigm emerging in the quantum sciences. The nonexperienced but in altered states potentially experienceable world is a world that vibrates at a frequency different from the world disclosed in ordinary perception. This possibility has been affirmed by yogis such as Vivekananda and confirmed by psychologists and psychiatrists such as Carl Gustav Jung and Stanislav Grof.
A catalogue of wisdom principles would not be complete unless it allowed for the hypothesis that there is more—much more—to the real world than meets our eye and ear. And that the “more” includes the phenomena we have called “spiritual.”
The universe is indeed miraculous. More and more of its miracles find scientific explanation. This does not make the universe any the less miraculous, only more comprehensible. And it makes our existence in the universe wiser—more informed. The realization that the avant-garde sciences rediscover that formerly inexplicable phenomena are part of the real world is now part of the scientific literacy of our time. It deserves to be part of our everyday literacy as well.
THE REALITY OF CONSCIOUSNESS
There is a real but nonphysical force in the universe. If the universe were nothing but a Newtonian machine moving inevitably toward its ultimate heat death, this something would be an inexplicable mystery. We and all living things would be anomalies—freakish accidents in the workings of a giant machine. But if the nonmaterial something present in the universe is truly a part of the universe, we are not freaks but quite the opposite: we are highly evolved expressions of the universe’s nonmaterial evolutionary force.
This factor has been recognized by sages and thinking people throughout history. It has been given various names: mind, spirit, soul … In the perspective of the new paradigm emerging in science and society, the best name for it is “consciousness.” But what is consciousness? The answer we propose here places the question in a wider, truly cosmic context.
The universe demonstrates a trend or tendency toward coherence and wholeness; and if this is the case, we must recognize a nonmaterial factor in its evolution. This factor is a universal impetus and, as we have said, in the context of science this factor is an “attractor.”
But there is also something else in the universe that cannot be explained as a purely physical factor. That is what the religions and spiritual systems call spirit or soul, and the secular cultures recognize as mind. It is immediately present in human beings: it is the ultimate phenomenon. As Schrödinger pointed out, we cannot go beyond consciousness: everything we experience is an aspect or manifestation of consciousness. Consciousness is present in all normal human beings, and it may be present in other species as well. Perhaps even in quite simple ones.
In the mainstream paradigm of recent times, scientists tried to explain consciousness as a product created by the firing of neurons in the brain. The phenomenon of consciousness has the same grade of reality as the electric charge does, generated by a magnet rotating around a wire coil. In that case electricity is the product, and in the case of neurons firing in the brain, consciousness is the product. It is not the “real” reality, but a product of that reality. Taken in itself, it is but an epiphenomenon.
This is the classical concept, and it is now antiquated. More and more evidence is coming forth to show that consciousness cannot be reduced to the workings of the brain. We have encountered a score of anomalous phenomena indicating that consciousness can exist even when the brain is flatlined. And that in that “discarnate” condition, consciousness can convey information that is veridical, although it could not have been generated by the brain, since it is beyond the range of the signals the brain could process on its own.
How did such a “beyond-the-brain” phenomenon come about? How could it appear in the physical universe? As just noted, the standard answer is that it emerges when there is a sufficient level of complexity in the brain. At that point consciousness appears: it is generated by the complex brain. The standard answer is that there is no consciousness prior to the critical level of cerebral complexity, and there is no consciousness when that level is infringed. In plain language, consciousness is a product of the brain. If so, there cannot be consciousness in the absence of a functioning brain.
The standard answer is faulty. There is solid evidence that consciousness can exist beyond the brain. As shown by the much-researched and now well-established near-death experiences (NDEs), consciousness can persist even when the brain is seriously damaged, and even when it is wholly inoperative. And there are scores of less unambiguously established but still convincing phenomena, such as out-of-body experiences, mystical experiences, and even so-called ADEs (after-death experiences) that testify to the presence of consciousness independently of brain function. They raise the further, truly mind-boggling possibility that consciousness could exist even in the absence of life in the organism.
The indicated and meaningful hypothesis is that consciousness is not generated by the brain: it is only transmitted by it. Consciousness is part of the reality of the universe. The consciousness present in the universe is transmitted by, and so it manifests in, the human brain, and in the body associated with the human brain. It manifests according to the level of development of the brain—a more complex and coherent brain manifests a more articulate form of consciousness. This particular affirmation was considered proof that it is the brain that generates consciousness. But consciousness persists even when there is no observable brain function.
Given the hypothesis that the brain transmits rather than generates consciousness, there is no need to be mystified by the so-called big question
of consciousness research: “how something as nonmaterial as consciousness could emerge in something as material as a system of neurons.” The answer is that this nonmaterial something emerges in, but is not generated by, the brain. The brain is a transceiver, it receives and transmits consciousness. The fact is that consciousness is a local manifestation of a universal phenomenon. Consciousness is universally present in space and time.
Individual consciousness is not something separate, belonging only to the brain of the individual who manifests it. Individual consciousness is a cluster of information deriving from information that is universally present in space and time. Schrödinger was right when he said that there is no such thing as consciousness in the plural. There is one consciousness, one supreme set of information in the universe. Individual consciousness is a projection of that set, its features selected by the particular needs and capacities of the individual brain and organism.
In the final count, consciousness is one in the universe; only its manifestations are manifold. Our consciousness is not just ours: it is an aspect of the consciousness present in every system that can receive it. In the case of human beings, that system is the functioning brain.
The consciousness that appears in association with the living brain is only one aspect and manifestation of consciousness. It is the “incarnate” manifestation of consciousness. Consciousness exists also in the nonembodied, discarnate state, persisting life after life, and between lives. This is the doctrine of the Eastern spiritual systems. The new paradigm of consciousness can subscribe to it. Consciousness is a universal and infinite phenomenon. Its continued existence no more depends on being received than the existence of a TV program or a website depends on being received by a TV set, a computer, or a smartphone.
OUR IMMORTAL CONSCIOUSNESS
We are conscious beings in a consciousness-infused universe. As individual entities, we emerged in an instantly interacting, seamlessly whole cosmic field. Our body is a cluster of vibration in that field, and it is mortal; it came into space and time with the fertilization of the ovum of our biological mother by the sperm of our biological father. But the clusters of vibration that manifest what the spiritual traditions call soul or spirit (and we call consciousness) are ubiquitous in space and infinite in time. They remain present even when there is no living brain to capture and transmit them. Our body is mortal, but our consciousness is immortal.
We live in a tragic period of our existence, suspended, as Mohammed’s casket, between heaven and earth. Our consciousness is now evolved enough to realize that it is part of the consciousness of the universe, but not evolved enough to realize that it is immortal. We live as mortal beings, although there is nothing entirely “mortal” in the universe. All the life that ever emerged and will ever emerge is conserved as part of the consciousness that pervades the universe. This timeless and now rediscovered truth is vital for a being who “re-cognizes” that he or she has consciousness. Failing this re-cognition, his or her immortal consciousness is ignored, and substituted by a finite consciousness that emerges out of nothingness and vanishes again into nothingness.
This failure is a tragedy of cosmic dimensions. It makes our life an interlude between birth, a stage seemingly prior to consciousness, and a stage that we suspect will come when our consciousness has completed its trajectory as an existent entity in the world.
It is time to realize that this is an illusion. Owing to our immortal consciousness, we have always been here, and will always be here—a real, existent entity in the universe. Our life need not be a preparation for death; it can be a celebration of eternal life.

When you choose consumer products for your personal use, do you choose fancy items that use a great deal of energy or simple, functional devices that do the job with a minimum of waste and fuss? When you choose your work or profession, do you strive to amass the most money in the shortest time, or prefer to engage in an activity that is meaningful in itself and beneficial to others? When you make use of your free time to enjoy your life, are these enjoyments compatible with the right of others to seek their own enjoyments?
In regard to giving an answer, you can consult a simple checklist of aspirations and behaviors. These outline those aspirations that concord with the task of safeguarding, and if possible promoting, the flourishing of life on the planet. If you live well, you will:
Seek simple, natural foods, materials, and lifestyles, rejoicing in nature and rejecting uncleanliness, waste, and pollution. Avoid ostentation in personal appearance, at home and in the workplace; express instead genuine human and cultural values. Derive satisfaction from making choices that enhance the chances of life and well-being of other people, whether they live in your immediate environment or in distant places. Be careful not to consume in ways that prevent the options of others to satisfy the basic needs of their own life and well-being—not just out of a cool calculation of planetary resource availability and ecological carrying capacity, but because of your solidarity and sense of oneness with our community, nation, and culture, and with the global community of all peoples, nations, and cultures.
DISCARD THE OLD TO EMBRACE THE NEW
For the wisdom you adopt to be effective in guiding and inspiring your life, you must take an important step: you must make sure that acting on it is not blocked by faulty beliefs and obsolete aspirations. This means cleaning the deck: sweeping away beliefs and aspirations that are contrary to wise thinking and responsible acting. You may be lucky, or perhaps are already wise, and do not entertain such beliefs and aspirations. But given the shape of contemporary humanity, while this is possible, it is not likely. We are all likely to be saddled with incorrect and now outdated beliefs and aspirations.
The beliefs and aspirations you hold today—even if you do not hold them consciously, with clear purpose—deserve a second look. The wisdom principles that follow help you take this look. This is a necessary, although possibly painful exercise, as some of your cherished beliefs may be among those you need to question, and perhaps discard. But this step is necessary. In regard to the good way to live your life, the same as in every other regard, you cannot embrace the new until you have rid yourself of the old.
REVIEW YOUR BELIEFS
Here is a short list of cherished beliefs that you need to review and perhaps rethink:
My country, right or wrong. Come what may, I owe allegiance only to one flag and one government; all others are none of my business. The cult of efficiency. I must get the maximum out of every person, every machine, and every organization regardless of what is produced and whether or not it serves a useful purpose. The technological imperative. Anything that can be done ought to be done. If it can be made or performed, it can be sold, and if it is sold, it is good for us and the economy. The newer is the better. Anything that is new is better than (almost) anything that we had already a year ago. Economic rationality. The value of everybody, including neighbors,
partners, clients, and customers, can be calculated in money. What I want is what everybody wants: it is to amass money. The rest is idle conversation and mere pretense. The future is none of my business. Why should I worry about the interest of the next generation? Every generation has had to look after itself, and the current and the next generations will have to do the same.
These beliefs are outdated. Why so, is not difficult to see.
The chauvinistic assertion “my country, right or wrong” has played untold havoc both domestically and internationally, calling for people to fight for causes a new government later repudiates, to espouse the values and worldviews of a small group of political leaders, and to ignore the growing cultural, social, and economic ties that evolve among people in different parts of the globe. Yet there is nothing in the healthy human psychology that would forbid the expansion of loyalty above the level of the country one happened to have been born into. No individual is obliged by his or her psychological and moral makeup to swear exclusive allegiance to one flag only. We can be loyal to several segments of society without being disloyal to any. We can be loyal to our community without giving up loyalty to our province, state, or region. We can be loyal to our region and feel at one with an entire culture and with the human family as a whole. As Europeans are English, Germans, French, Spanish, and Italians as well as Europeans, and as Americans are New Englanders, Texans, southerners, and northwesterners as well as Americans, so people in all parts of the world can possess multiple identities and can develop allegiances to each of them. Efficiency without regard to what is produced and whom it will benefit leads to mounting unemployment, catering to the demands of the rich without regard to the needs of the poor, and polarizes society into “monetized” and “traditional” sectors. The technological imperative has resulted in a plethora of goods that people only think they need; some of them are not only not necessary but actually harmful. That the new would always be better is simply not true. Often, the new is worse than the “old” that it replaced—more expensive, more
wasteful, more damaging to health, and more polluting, alienating, or stressful. The naive reduction of everything and everybody to economic value may have seemed rational during epochs in which a great economic upswing turned all heads and pushed everything else into the background, but it is foolhardy at a time when people are beginning to rediscover deep-rooted social and spiritual values and to cultivate lifestyles of voluntary simplicity. Finally, living without conscious forward planning—though it may have been fine in days of rapid growth, when each new generation could ensure a good life for itself—is not a responsible option at a time when the decisions we make today have a profound impact on the well-being of those who come after us.
In addition, there are some deeply rooted beliefs that are now outdated and dangerous to entertain. Here is a brief catalogue.
The Environment Is an Inexhaustible Storehouse of Essential Resources
For all practical intents and purposes, the planet’s biosphere is an infinite source of resources for our life, and an infinite sink of the wastes we produce. This was evident to prior generations: the roots of this belief go back to the archaic empires. It would hardly have occurred to the inhabitants of ancient Babylonia, Sumer, Egypt, India, or China that their environment could ever be exhausted of the basic necessities of life—edible plants, domestic animals, clean water, and breathable air—or could be fouled by dumping into it our wastes and garbage. We still cherish the assumption that nature is far too vast to be tainted, polluted, or defiled by what we do in and around our cities and settlements.
The belief that the environment is an inexhaustible fountain of resources gave rise to the irresponsible treatment of nature that hallmarked the modern age. This attitude has deep historical roots. Indigenous people in Africa, Asia, and pre-Columbian America had a deep respect for the environment and used only as much as nature could regenerate. However, from the time of the “Neolithic Revolution” (the beginning of the period when humans have been domesticating plants and animals for their own purposes) some communities began not only to exploit but to overexploit their surroundings. The
Mycenaean and Olmec civilizations and those of the Indus Valley are notable examples. In the Fertile Crescent, the cradle of Western civilization, human settlements were not content with living by the perennial rhythms and cycles of nature, but sought ways to harness them for their own purposes. The assumption that the resources provided by the environment are inexhaustible made the Fertile Crescent of biblical times into the Middle East of today—a region with vast areas of arid, infertile land. But in earlier times people could move on, colonizing new lands and exploiting fresh resources. In the Nile Valley, the environment was more benign. Great rivers irrigated the land, brought in silt, and washed away wastes. Favorable conditions reigned also on the shores of the Yellow River, the Tigris and Euphrates, and the Ganges.
In some part of the Middle East and Asia, the overexploitation of nature’s resources produced pernicious consequences, but people could move on. Today we have nowhere left to go. In a globally extended industrial civilization, belief in the inexhaustibility of nature is irrational and dangerous. It gives rein to the overuse of the biosphere’s self-regenerative capacities. Persistence in such practices leads to an impoverished and inhospitable environment incapable of supplying needed resources for a vast and still growing population.
Nature Is a Giant Mechanism
Nature is a vast mechanism that can be engineered to fit our needs and demands. The validity of the mechanistic view of nature was amply demonstrated by the technologies of the early modern age. Watermills and windmills, pumps, mechanical clocks, and animal-drawn plows and carriages show that we can create mechanisms that fit our needs, and these mechanisms act on nature and shape it. The success of modern agriculture in converting wilderness areas into productive lands underscores this assumption.
The assumption that nature can be engineered like a machine has persisted to our day. Yet it doesn’t work when it comes to nuclear reactors, networked computers, and genetically engineered plants and microbes. Sophisticated technologies are not Newtonian machines, and they do not obey mechanical laws.
The mechanistic concept works even less well when man-made technologies are interfaced with processes in nature. The way a transplanted gene is expressed in a given species of plant is foreseeable for that plant, but
it is not foreseeable when it comes to the interaction of that plant with other plants in its environment. The same gene that produces the foreseen and desired effect in the transgenic plant can produce unforeseen and undesirable effect in other species. Such effects and consequences have proved disastrous for the integrity of nature and the productivity of lands in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. The side effects included progressive degradation of water, air, and soil, and the impairment of both local and continental ecosystems.
Life Is a Struggle Where Only the Fittest Survives
Darwin discovered that in nature the fit is that which survives. His followers applied this discovery to society. Generations of Darwinists have been telling us that if we want to survive, we have to be fit for the existential struggle— fitter than our competitors. In the human context fitness is not determined by the genes of our species, but by the qualities of the members of the species, such as smartness, daring, ambition, and the will to pursue one’s ends undeterred by extraneous consequences.
Transposing nineteenth-century Darwinism into the sphere of society is dangerous, as the “social Darwinism” of Nazi ideology demonstrated. Hitler’s regime justified the conquest of territories in the name of acquiring adequate Lebensraum (living space), and warranted the subjugation of other peoples in the name of racial fitness.
In our day, the consequences of social Darwinism have gone beyond armed aggression to the more subtle but in some ways equally merciless struggle of competitors in the marketplace. All-out competition has been producing widening gaps between rich and poor, and concentrated wealth and power in the hands of the winners: corporate managers and international financiers. States and entire populations have been relegated to the role of clients and consumers and, if poor and powerless, were dismissed as marginal factors in the equations that determine success in the marketplace.
The Market Distributes All Benefits
“The market ensures optimal capital allocation through the efficient incorporation of all available and relevant information into share prices.” This, in the language of economics, is the principle behind Adam Smith’s “invisible hand.” No matter how inequitable may be the benefits of what we produce, the market straightens out the distortions. It assures that if we do well for ourselves, we also do well for all others. Wealth trickles down from
the rich to the poor. After all, a rising tide, John F. Kennedy said, lifts all boats.
Belief in the invisible hand of the market is comforting, and it is cited by the winners to avert reproach for not caring for the losers. Unfortunately, the idea of the free market leaves out of account the fact that the market is never entirely free. It is assumed to distribute benefits under conditions of perfect or near-perfect competition. There has never been perfect or even near-perfect competition in the real world. The playing field is never level. Inevitably, it is distorted in favor of the winners to the detriment of the losers. This produces the kind of income distribution we had in the pre-pandemic world: the poorest 40 percent of the population has been left with 3 percent of the global wealth, while the wealth of a few hundred billionaires equaled the income of half of the world’s peoples.
The More You Consume the Better You Are
There is a direct relationship between the size of your wallet and your personal worth as the owner of the wallet—as demonstrated, among other things, by the power of your car and the size of your house. Everybody wants the latest consumer goods and the most luxurious ways of living, but only those who are smarter than the rest manage to acquire them. This reflects credit on them—on their value as resourceful and successful members of society.
The equivalence of personal worth with financial worth has been consciously fueled by business. In former years, companies did not hesitate to advertise unlimited consumption as the ideal. Victor Lebov, a U.S. retailing analyst writing shortly after World War II, put forward the consumerist philosophy in clear terms. “Our enormously productive economy,” he said, “demands that we make consumption our way of life, that we convert the buying and use of goods into rituals, that we seek our spiritual satisfaction, our ego satisfaction, in consumption. The economy needs things consumed, burned, worn out, replaced, and discarded at an ever-increasing rate.” The belief that consumption is good both for the consumers and for the economy in which they consume has been a powerful engine driving growth in the economy. In a finite environment, ever more people consuming ever more is not, of course, sustainable; it leads to the inequities, resentments, and stresses we have experienced in the pre-pandemic world.
Everyone Is Unique and Separate
We are all unique and separate individuals enclosed in our skin and pursuing our own interests. The same as our country, we have only ourselves to rely on; everyone else is either friend or foe, at best linked to us by ties of mutual —but only provisional and temporary—interest. This is a commonsense observation that only poets and mystics could question.
We are indeed unique, but we are not separate. We are unique parts of various communities, including our family on one end of the spectrum and the human family of all families on the other. Seeing ourselves as separate from the rest of the world converts our natural impulses to seek our advantage into a win-lose struggle to achieve our aims regardless of the cost to others. This is a shortsighted strategy for individuals as well as for states and business companies. It often produces an unexpected and highly unwelcome backlash.
Every Problem Is Reversible
The virus pandemic, as other crises before it, is a temporary perturbation after which everything will go back to normal. All we must do is manage the problem with tried and tested methods. Business as unusual has evolved out of business as usual, and sooner or later will reverse back into it.
This is a widespread belief, but it does not stand to reason: it has strongly negative consequences. If we hold to it, no experience of shocks and crises will change our thinking and our behavior. The world could change around us, but we remain the same. This makes us miss the opportunity to be a better person, and build a better world.
Order Through Hierarchy
Order in society can be achieved only by rules and laws and their proper enforcement, and this requires a chain of command recognized and obeyed by all. A few people on top (usually males) make up the rules, legislate the laws, give the orders, and ensure compliance with them. Everyone else is to obey the rules and take his and her place within the social hierarchy.
Male-dominated hierarchies have worked in the army and also in the church, but they have proven ineffective in business and in education. Successful managers and wise deans have learned the advantages of cooperative structures, whereas social and political institutions still operate in
a largely hierarchical mode. As a result, state administrations as well as schools and universities tend to be slow to respond, their workings cumbersome and inefficient.
If we are not to fall back into the inequitable and crisis-prone structures and unsustainable practices of the pre-pandemic world, such assumptions need to be questioned, together with the beliefs and practices on which they are based.
Safeguarding National Security Warrants Maintaining a Powerful Standing Army
The ancient Romans had a saying: If you aspire to peace, prepare for war. They had a worldwide empire, with rebellious peoples within and barbarian tribes at the periphery. Maintaining it required a constant exercise of force. The use of force continued to be exercised in modern times. Modern people agree that there is good reason for maintaining a standing army and keeping it in readiness for war. Every country that managed to safeguard its interests has been ready to back its interests with the force of arms. If a country does not do so, it risks becoming the prey of competitors who do not hesitate to use their own arms to encroach on its territories, resources, and interests.
When we believe that our national security calls for it, recourse to armed intervention becomes legitimate. This belief entails a high risk: armed interventions could escalate to wars and spread to the international and then to the global level. In a world where stockpiles of weapons of mass destruction are on hair-trigger alert, taking this risk is not reasonable. Armed responses could bring into play weapons that can not only neutralize the immediate threat, but destroy the higher forms of life on Earth. There are tried and proven methods for safeguarding national interests beyond “calling in the marines.” The recourse to arms is a measure of last resort, when everything else fails. And even then, it is questionable whether it is reasonable. As a matter of principle, taking human life is never reasonable, whether by an individual or by the military.
RETHINK YOUR ASPIRATIONS
Your aspirations guide your behavior, and your behavior is a vital factor in the equations that determine whether we now head toward a better world, or toward chaos and collapse. Your aspirations need to change the conditions
that exist in the real world. The real world today is an inequitable and unbalanced world, with growing gaps between people and societies, and between human communities and the ecology of the planet.
For several decades now, the gap between rich and poor has been expanding. If this trend were to continue, by the middle of this century over 90 percent of humanity would live in the poor regions of the world. The rich would be a small enclave, surrounded by a sea of poverty. This would be, and in fact it already is, an explosive situation.
In the context of this situation, what counts is not how many people are poor in the world and how many are rich, but how the rich and the poor use the essential resources of human life. The world has enough, Gandhi said, to provide for everybody’s need, but not enough even for one man’s greed.
In the industrialized parts of the world, greed has been dominant. In the name of freedom and laissez-faire capitalism, irresponsible values and assumptions gave rein to selfishness and ostentation. Living by the dominant aspirations entailed excessive consumption of nonrenewable resources and excessive waste. Those who are said to enjoy the modern world’s high living standard use 80 percent of the world’s energy and raw materials and contribute the lion’s share of its pollution.
The rich have been overusing the planet’s resources, but their high material standard of living did not create a better quality of life for them. Their lifeways and consumption patterns have often led to ill health—to hypertension, stroke, heart disease, cancer, and stressful obesity. The very ideal of luxury is flawed. The modern world’s paradigm of luxury—sitting in the sun, smoking a cigarette, sipping a daiquiri, munching on a hamburger, and talking on a cell phone—is misguided and even dangerous. It leads to a number of chronic diseases, such as skin cancer, lung cancer, cirrhosis of the liver, high cholesterol, and brain damage. Leading a life of luxury has not been much of an improvement over working in a high-pressure job, taking smoke breaks every hour, having a drink after work to relax, and falling asleep in front of the television.
Are there not better ways to live your life? There are meaningful and important tasks one can undertake in every job and profession. There are scores of healthy and rewarding ways to spend your leisure time. Helping neighbors, creating a better community, visiting sites of natural, historical, or cultural interest, hiking, swimming, biking, reading, listening to music, and taking an interest in literature and culture are satisfying pursuits that do not
involve a high level of material and energy consumption and do not require a great deal of money. They are healthier and easier on the environment than living according to the modern world’s ideal of luxury.
Better aspirations on your part could free a significant portion of the planet’s resources for human consumption. For example, it takes 190 square meters of land and 105,000 liters of water to produce 1 kilogram of grain-fed feedlot beef, but to produce 1 kilogram of soybeans takes only 16 square meters of land and 9,000 liters of water. On the same amount of land where farmers catering to the preferences of the rich have been producing 1 kilogram of beef, they could produce nearly 12 kilograms of soybeans or 8.6 kilograms of corn. And they would save 96,000 liters of water to produce soybeans and 92,500 liters to plant corn.
Eating fresh produce, living closer to nature, using public transportation when available, and walking instead of sitting in cars are healthier than eating red meat and junk food, and sitting in cars in overcrowded cities and jammed highways, breathing toxic exhaust fumes.
Here is a short checklist of more adapted aspirations. It is in the form of a response to a basic question. Is it not true that it is better, more reasonable, for you to:
Consume resources to satisfy real needs and not superficial wants, whether by us or by others; Live in a way that satisfies our needs without reducing the option of others to satisfy their needs; Aspire to leadership in a group of equals rather than to being a lone hero above the others; Seek simple natural foods, materials, and lifestyles, rejoicing in nature and rejecting waste and pollution; and to Overcome the need to be ostentatious in the way you appear to others, cultivating instead your own culture and your own values?
CHALLENGE TODAY’S IRRATIONAL PRACTICES
In the year 2004, the Wisdom Council of the international think tank the Club of Budapest issued a declaration that questioned a number of practices that hallmark the contemporary world. These practices are accepted and influential, but on a deeper look they are paradoxical and even irrational. The
Club of Budapest’s Wisdom Council asked: Where is the wisdom in a system that—
Produces weapons that are more dangerous than the conflicts they are meant to solve? Creates an overproduction of food, but fails to make it available to those who are hungry? Allows half of the world’s children to live in poverty and millions of them to suffer acute hunger? Fails to appreciate and make use of the sensitivity, care, and sense of solidarity women bring to the family and the community, and could bring to politics and business? Faces a gamut of tasks and challenges, yet puts more and more people out of work? Gives full priority to maximizing the productivity of labor (even though millions are unemployed or underemployed) rather than improving the productivity of resources (notwithstanding that most natural resources are finite and many are scarce and nonrenewable)? Requires unrelenting economic and financial growth just to function and not to crash? Faces long-term structural and operational problems, yet bases its criteria of success on three-month accounting periods and the day-to-day fluctuation of stock exchanges? Assesses social and economic progress in terms of the gross national product and leaves out of account the quality of people’s lives and how well their basic needs are fulfilled? Fights religious fundamentalism but enshrines “market fundamentalism” (the belief that unrestrained competition on the market can right all wrongs and solve all problems)? Expects people in their private sphere to abide by the golden rule “treat others as you expect others to treat you,” yet ignores this elementary principle of fairness and equity in politics (to treat other states as you expect other states to treat your state) as well as in business (to treat partners and competitors as you expect partners and competitors to treat your business)?
Where indeed is the wisdom in such practices? And even in failing to
question such practices?
Expand Your Identity
As a typical member of today’s humanity, you have a narrow identity. You are a skin-enclosed, flesh-and-bone being who is separate from the world beyond its skin. It exists in a pitiless world where only those who are fit and fight survive. The world around you is populated by “others”: other living things living in other settings and environments.
To create a better world, you need to shift your identity. You are a unique being, but not a separate being. Separation in the world, Einstein said, is an illusion. Humanity has lived with this illusion for centuries and created the social and economic systems of the twenty-first century in its light. In this world individuals and nations are separate from each other and from their environment. Not surprisingly, this is an inequitable, unbalanced, and unsustainable world—a world where the rich and the powerful come first, and there is little or no concern for the rest.
The world we could create together must be based on the realization that individuals, nations, businesses, and organizations of all kinds are not separate, either from one another or from the world around them. People need to have a wide identity. There are no “others” in the world. We are all members of the human family.
Expanding our identity to encompass all members of the human family is a perennial ideal of humanistic thought. It is a large order, and we are not sure when it will be realized. In any event, this level of expansion is necessary, but it is not enough in itself. Even when we expand our identity to encompass all members of the human family, we could still be creating divisions in the world. The human community is “us,” and the tribes, societies, and groups of other living beings are “others.” Maintaining this distinction, a result of our insufficiently expanded identity, we remain alienated from the web of life on the planet, and continue to exploit it for our narrowly conceived ends. We need to identify ourselves not only with other humans, but with all living things on the planet. This means expanding our identity to embrace the entire web of life—with Life, with a capital L.
Expanding our identity to embrace Life creates a powerful motivation for engaging in the task of creating a better world. When we are one with all life around us, we take as good care of the other forms of life as we do of ourselves. With this expanded consciousness, we can become architects of a
new and better world. Here is an important consideration regarding the realism of the seemingly
unreal aspiration to become one with the planet’s web of life. Life, the system of life, is not a mere given, the result of an unfathomable act of creation. It is the manifestation of a stupendously powerful impetus, the impetus for the dissemination and evolution of all the forms in which life can appear in the universe. On Earth, life appeared 3.8 billion years ago, and it persists and continues to proliferate to this day. There must be many places that harbor life in the universe. We now know that organic molecules, the seeds of biological life, exist even in the vicinity of active stars, where scientists thought that nothing as complex as these structures could exist.
The evolution of life is more than a search for fitness through adaptation to the environment. If it were just that, the earth would be mainly populated by blue-green algae, amoebae, and other unicellular, colonial, and simple colonial organisms. Most of these organisms have achieved a nearly perfect adaptation to their environment—they are amazingly well “fit.” Nothing short of volcanic eruptions, sudden climatic change, and natural catastrophe could lead to their extinction. Yet life on Earth has not stopped evolving on reaching this level of fitness. Living species continue to explore all niches that could support them, even those that offer the barest minimum of support for organic life. Some so-called extremophiles tolerate extremely high (and others extremely low) temperatures, pressures, radiations, and levels of acidity. They invade and colonize such unlikely niches as active volcanoes, deserts, and the deep sea. They learn to live in conditions that are lethal for other forms of life.
The physiology of our own species likewise exhibits features that are not likely to have been simply the result of a search for fitness. We have chromosome 2 in the brain (the second-largest human chromosome, spanning more than 242 million base pairs and representing nearly 8 percent of the total DNA in our cells), produced by a fusion that gives us the capacity for empathy, sympathy, compassion, and the self-regulation of the organism. We also have sensory neurites in the heart that ensure connection and alignment between the heart and the brain, and we have a brain that is at least twice as large as would be needed to operate the functions required for physical survival. Fitness, as shown by the ability of the organism to survive, could be achieved at a far less complex and coherent level of cellular and organic organization.
The evidence speaks clearly: the evolution of life is not toward fitness. The emerging insight is that evolution is a drive toward higher and higher levels of integration and coherence, toward a form of organization that produces complex systems that cohere with other systems and together form complex and coherent suprasystems. To thrive in this world, we need to be aligned with this powerful drive—the force behind the evolution of life on this planet, and presumably throughout the universe.
How do we align with the force of evolution? There are many ways. We can follow the guidance of the quantum sciences, or follow the insights of spiritual masters, or achieve our own insight by searching our own deeper self. Scientists point the way with their observation-and-experiment-based theories; sages point the way with their spiritual insight; and creative people develop their own intuitions and insights. Their findings are diverse in particular expression, but they all point in the same direction: to the recognition of the coherent oneness of all forms of life. It is this oneness that you can participate in when you align with “the force”—and you can align with the force when you expand your identity.
Let us sum up the principal points. We have a chance of creating a better world, because through the virus pandemic the old world has been disrupted: it is ready to be renovated, and in some respects it may be ripe to be replaced. We can create a new world starting with ourselves. The key is the expansion of our identity. We are not just skin-enclosed, finite beings, here today and gone tomorrow, but intrinsic parts of a larger, dynamic whole: Life on Earth.
For the first time in the history of humanity, we can orient our evolution consciously and purposefully. We can do so because we can consciously and purposefully connect with the impetus that drives evolution on this planet. This calls for the expansion of your identity—and that expansion is in your power to achieve. If you do, you will find the key to your own well-being in the context of the well-being of others around you—of “others” who are not really other than you, but are part of you, as you are part of them.
BIFURCATION: YOUR CHANCE TO SHIFT THE WORLD
The sciences of systems and information are telling us that the crisis we are living through is a “bifurcation”—a sudden forking in the evolutionary trajectory of a dynamic system. The human community on the planet is such a system. There are many dynamic systems in the universe, ranging from simple multicellular organisms to embracing ecologies. At one time or another they could all have undergone a bifurcation, a leap in the trajectory of their evolution. But we humans alone can bring consciousness to this process, and exploit the possibilities it offers. This is a unique advantage, and it is not to be missed.
We have learned a few things about bifurcations. They are one-way; the way they evolve cannot be reversed. But their outcomes are not predetermined—they allow a choice. Living in a bifurcation, we can choose the way we go. This is a unique opportunity. For the first time in history, we can consciously and purposefully choose our destiny. This could be a bright destiny; the dawn of a new era of sanity and flourishing. But whether it will be that is not decided yet. It is still up to us.
Bifurcation creates crisis, and crisis, as we know, is not just danger—it is also opportunity. It is a prelude to change. The challenge we face is to bring about the change that leads to a sane and flourishing world. This is a real but nonrecurring opportunity. Failing to seize it means facing the prospect of returning to an unstable world beset by recurring crises.
For the last several decades we have been exposed to a plethora of crises. They include crises as bad as the pandemic. Millions are dying of starvation and penury. Hordes of refugees are searching for a way and a place to survive and are tearing apart the fabric of societies. Droughts turn fertile verdant land into arid, lifeless plains, rising sea levels flood ever more habitations on the planet, and violent storms destroy the abodes of rich and poor alike. There is a distinct prospect of local conflicts escalating into regional wars and turning into a global nuclear confrontation.
The catalogue of the crises we experience makes it clear: this is an
unsustainable world. We either learn to live sanely and sustainably in harmony with one another and with nature, or we leave the stage of history. This is a lesson we have heard but not learned for decades if not for centuries. Now we are facing the consequences.
Returning to business as usual, to the norms and practices and values and assumptions of the past, would be suicidal. Fortunately, the tipping points we have already reached in both the global economy and the global ecology make this difficult, if not impossible. This is just as well: another way is opening for us. The economic, social, and political systems that have framed your life have been shaken to their roots. Disruptive change happened, and it is a prelude to fundamental change, whether constructive or destructive. Making it constructive should be possible. It is the opportunity that opens for us at this critical juncture of our shared history.
THE PANDEMIC: A BLESSING IN DISGUISE
The crisis we are experiencing is driving us to change, whether we like it or not. The choice is not whether, but how to change. You now have a real chance to change for the better, and the pandemic is affording you this opportunity. Forcing you to change is a blessing in disguise.
The COVID-19–provoked transformation is more profound than any of the previous transformations of human life and organization. We thought that the economy is the sphere of global transformation, citing the globalization of local, regional, and national economies through technology, finance, communication, and trade. The radical separation of a thinking, feeling human being from an unthinking and unfeeling world came only with the modern age, and came mainly in the West. It prompted the uninhibited exploitation of presumably unfeeling nature by the thinking and feeling, and therefore superior, human species.
Insightful people have never accepted this narrowly anthropocentric view, whether they were artists, poets, mystics, or scientists. Giordano Bruno, Leonardo da Vinci, Galileo Galilei, Isaac Newton, Nicolaus Copernicus, and in more recent times Max Planck and Albert Einstein, were among the scientists who testified eloquently to their belief that the world around us, although in many respects still mysterious, is intrinsically meaningful and integrally whole.

The behavior of business enterprises is a crucial factor in deciding the outcome of today’s transformation, and the culture of the enterprises is the key factor in deciding the enterprise’s behavior. Today’s business culture—at least at the leading edge—is changing. It is no longer defined by the shareholder philosophy: the belief that the interest of the shareholder is primary and overrides every other consideration. In the culture of leading corporations, short-term profit seeking has been mitigated by a search for sustainability and value. However, the culture of business is as yet far from aligning with a healthy norm—the norm in which the enterprise can coexist and coevolve with the communities in which it is embedded and that it serves.
Leading enterprises are already shifting from exclusive concern with customers and shareholders to concern with society, the environment, and
their stakeholders; they are aware of, and try to live up to, the triple bottom line “people, planet, and profit.” The culture toward which they would need to thrive is not these three P’s on a par, however, but planet first, as the essential precondition for the life of people; people next; and profit last. The latter is not an objective in itself, but the means for achieving the true objectives. This approach would align the flourishing of the enterprise with the thriving of people, and the health of all living things.
You can insist on a healthy norm for the workings of business. This means that business people respect the responsibility of the company for creating healthier relations with its employees, clients, and shareholders, as well as its business partners, with the local community, and with the environment.
RESPONSIBILITY TOWARD EMPLOYEES
Employees are allowed to contribute to the formulation of the company’s vision and values. Annual performance evaluations, compensation systems, and career progression criteria are integrated into the company’s vision and values. The company strives to be actively engaged in the lives of its employees, learning their concerns and understanding their needs.
RESPONSIBILITY TOWARD CLIENTS AND CUSTOMERS
The company accurately represents the costs and benefits of its products and services, including their safety, social consequences, recyclability, reusability, and environmental toxicity. The company makes every effort to enlighten its customers as to the social and environmental desirability of its products and services from cradle to grave. The company’s innovation and product development strategy shapes the industry toward greater sustainability, social responsibility, and corporate citizenship.
RESPONSIBILITY TOWARD SHAREHOLDERS
The company meets the expectations of its shareholders without
impairing the social and the ecological environment. Annual reporting is provided to shareholders on legal and regulatory compliance as well as on social and environmental initiatives beyond compliance. The company’s investment strategy favors activities that involve desirable social, environmental, and business practices.
RESPONSIBILITY TOWARD BUSINESS PARTNERS
The company does not do business with companies that knowingly degrade or otherwise cause significant damage to the environment or behave unfairly toward employees, customers, business partners, or local communities. The company offers preferential status whenever possible to business partners evidencing ethical leadership.
RESPONSIBILITY TOWARD THE HOST COMMUNITY
The company does not do business in markets or support regimes that violate basic human rights. In addition to established corporate giving or patronage programs, the company is actively engaged in the life of its local communities, learning their concerns, understanding their needs, and contributing to their development. There is open dialogue between the company and the communities in which it operates. Employees of the company are encouraged and given opportunities to devote part of their time to socially responsible activities, doing volunteer work or contributing to the conservation and revitalization of the community. The company respects the diversity of the economic, social, cultural, and political conditions in the communities in which it operates.
AND LAST BUT BY NO MEANS LEAST: RESPONSIBILITY TOWARD THE ENVIRONMENT
The company complies with environmental regulations and laws. The company consistently seeks pollution prevention and waste minimization in its supply chain. It anticipates environmental regulation by taking the initiative in reducing negative impacts on the environment. The company establishes its own environmental management system. The company actively pursues eco-efficiency and de-materialization of its value-added services to customers. The company is continually designing itself for environmental sustainability, including recycling nonrenewable resources, consuming renewable resources at a rate that allows them to regenerate, and avoiding a further reduction of biodiversity.
CHAMPION A NEW PHILOSOPHY FOR POLITICS
The influential and until recently seldom seriously challenged philosophy of national and international politics is obsolete: it has seriously negative consequences. It creates separation and self-centeredness, encourages all-out competition, and warrants the pursuit of one’s own interests without concern for the consequences for others. Persisting with the dominant philosophy creates an international political system that is replete with conflict, separation, all-out competition, and violence. Contemporary politics needs a new philosophy.
The operative principle of the new philosophy can be summed up in a single sentence: what is good for society as a whole is good for every person and for every structure and enterprise in society.
The currently dominant political philosophy differs from this conception. In its theoretical humanistic expression, it maintains that the good of the individual coincides with the good of society. This is the philosophy of a liberal ethics where the individual is free to pursue his or her own interests without concern for the rest—as long as he or she acts within the formal or informal morality espoused in society. The updated political philosophy leaves the above coincidence open as a possibility, but does not affirm it as an invariant rule. The invariant rule is the converse: good of the whole of society is always and necessarily good for every individual, structure, and enterprise in society. This is the principle of holism applied to society: the
backbone of the philosophy urgently needed in politics. Traditional cultures have been holistic: they put the good of their tribe
and community first. Their society comprised individual humans as well as their kinship and socioeconomic communities and structures. It included nature: their life-supporting environment. They knew that what is good for their tribe or community in the embrace of nature is good for all members of their tribe or community. Contemporary politics disregards this principle. It extols the individual enterprise, the individual state or nation, and the individual ethnic group. It is not primarily concerned with the whole in which individuals pursue their aspirations.
The operative philosophy of contemporary politics is the exact opposite of the principle of holism. It is based on a simple and basic assumption: what is good for the individual (or the individual enterprise, community, state, or nation) is good for the system in which that individual (or enterprise, community, state, or nation) is situated. Well-meaning politicians consider that if something is good for any given individual, enterprise, or social structure it is also good for the whole country. And very likely for other countries as well. Authoritarian politicians see no need to spell out why this should be so, but if pressed they may cite Adam Smith’s doctrine of the invisible hand. This is the hand of the market; it harmonizes the interests of the individual with the interests of society.
However, the equitable working of the market presupposes a level playing field where all competitors have equal chances and opportunities. Such a playing field is not a given in today’s world, and in consequence the invisible hand often turns into an invisible foot that kicks the poor and powerless but spares the rich and powerful. Resources—whether money, influence, or arms—tend to accumulate to the rich and powerful and create inequality and injustice. Contemporary nation-state politics “suboptimizes” the world economic and political system, breaking apart the coherence of the system. It creates separation, conflict, and unbridled competition.
The philosophy underlying contemporary nation-state politics has been clearly enunciated in a U.S. congressional hearing by Charlie Wilson, then president of General Motors. Wilson famously told the senators, “What is good for General Motors is good for the country.” Thus GM can pursue its own interests without concern for other people and other companies. Its good is the good of the United States, and in the final count the good of the United States is the good of all the countries of the world.
The above philosophy was adopted by Nazi Germany. It was expressed in the slogan “Deutschland über alles” (“Germany above all”). German politics was uniquely concerned with the good of the German nation, and not concerned whether this was the good also of other nations. In a less aggressive but equally potent form the same principle was expressed by Donald Trump when he declared, “America first.” Acting on it had led the Trump administration to withdraw the United States from the Paris Agreement on Climate Change, reject wide-based multilateral cooperation with other states in the Western Hemisphere, engage in trade wars with China, and reduce and even stop support for international organizations such as UNESCO and the World Health Organization.
Sooner or later, the pursuit of the currently dominant principle produces a backlash. At the end of World War II, it created disastrous conditions for Germany, and if pursued (which at this time seems unlikely) it would produce disastrous consequences for America as well. A new paradigm is needed in nation-state politics—one that respects the interest of the whole system of societies, and recognizes that doing so is in the best interest of every society in the system.
Adopting the philosophy of holism in politics is a daring move, but if major breakdowns are to be averted, it needs to be contemplated. It is in your most enlightened shared interest to realize that when it comes to nation-state politics, it is not America, not Germany, and not Britain (as even the pro-Brexit advocates are beginning to realize) that comes first. The whole system of human societies on the planet, together with the natural ecology in which it is embedded, is what comes first. Because then the system of life on the planet can flourish, and when it does, all its elements, human as well as nonhuman, flourish.
FIND A MORALITY TO SHARE WITH ALL
Values and beliefs determine the way we perceive the world and suggest the way we prioritize our responses to our perceptions. They affect all areas of our judgment and behavior. However, in the wider context of society, individual values and beliefs are unlikely to conform to a common standard on their own, and attempting to impose them “from above” would be misguided and, as historical experience shows, is likely to misfire. Self-regulation is just as important in the moral sphere as it is in any other sphere
of society. Self-regulatory mechanisms are urgently needed in all areas of civil society. In regard to values and perceptions, the need is for a mechanism based on the acceptance of a shared moral code or principle—ultimately, a planetary ethic. A planetary ethic does not prescribe the nature of private and public morality; it only ensures that they do not give rise to behaviors that are damaging to the wider community that is the vital context of our lives.
How could a morality shared the world over arise and spread in society? Traditionally, setting the norms of morality was the task of the religions. The Ten Commandments of Jews and Christians, the provisions for the faithful in Islam, and the Noble Eightfold Path of the Buddhists are examples. Today the dominance of science has reduced the power of religious doctrines to regulate human behavior, and many people look to science for practical guidance. Yet scientists, with some notable exceptions, discover few principles that would provide a basis for universal morality. Henri de Saint-Simon in the late 1700s, Auguste Comte in the early 1800s, and Émile Durkheim in the late 1800s and early 1900s tried to develop “positive” scientific observation-based and experiment-based principles for a meaningful and publicly acceptable ethic.
In the final decade of the twentieth century, scientists as well as political leaders began to recognize the need for principles that would suggest universal norms of behavior. In April 1997, in “A Universal Declaration of Human Responsibilities,” the InterAction Council, a group of twenty-four former heads of state or government, declared, “Because global interdependence demands that we must live with each other in harmony, human beings need rules and constraints. Ethics are the minimum standards that make a collective life possible. Without ethics and self-restraint that are their result, humankind would revert to the survival of the fittest. The world is in need of an ethical base on which to stand.”
The Union of Concerned Scientists, an organization of leading scientists, concurred. “A new ethic is required,” claimed a statement signed in 1993 by 1,670 scientists from seventy countries, including 102 Nobel laureates. “This ethic must motivate a great movement, convincing reluctant leaders and reluctant governments and reluctant peoples themselves to effect the needed changes.” The scientists noted our new responsibility for caring for the earth and warned that “a great change in our stewardship of the earth and the life on it is required, if vast human misery is to be avoided and our global home on this planet is not to be irretrievably mutilated.” Human beings and the
natural world, they said, are on a collision course. This may so alter the living world that it will be unable to sustain life as we know it.
In November 2003, a group of Nobel Peace laureates meeting in Rome affirmed, “Ethics in the relations between nations and in government policies is of paramount importance. Nations must treat other nations as they wish to be treated. The most powerful nations must remember that as they do, so shall others do.” And in November 2004 the same group of laureates declared, “Only by reaffirming our shared ethical values—respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms—and by observing democratic principles, within and amongst countries, can terrorism be defeated. We must address the root causes of terrorism—poverty, ignorance and injustice—rather than responding to violence with violence.”
Undoubtedly in this period of reconstruction in the aftermath of the global crisis, the time has come for you to give serious thought to a morality that can be embraced by all people regardless of their creed, religion, race, sex, or secular belief. It must have intuitive appeal, addressing the basic moral instinct present in all healthy individuals. Because the ideals of Marx, Lenin, and Mao for an egalitarian world society were subverted in the practice of communist states, the highest expression of everyday ethics for the great bulk of humanity became liberalism, the philosophical heritage of Bentham, Locke, and Hume. People are not to be prevented from pursuing their self-interest as long as they observe the rules that permit life in civilized society. Ethics and morality have no objective basis: human actions are based on self-interest, moderated at best by an element of altruistic sympathy. “Live and let live” emerged as the principle of liberalism. You can live in any way you please, as long as you do not break the laws. This led to great and growing gaps between rich and poor, powerful and marginalized. It broke apart the texture of the global community.
However, rather than “live and let live,” we need a morality that is just as appealing as the doctrine of liberalism but is better adapted to life on this planet. Such a morality would substitute Gandhi’s “Live more simply, so others can simply live” for liberalism’s “Live and let live.” You need to specify, however, that you mean by simplicity not imposed by poverty, by the lack of real choices. Simplicity means accepting a reduced impact of your life on the biosphere. Your actions must not exceed the capacity of the planet to provide for the needs of all people. The morality we all need can be summed up by this phrase: live in a way that allows others to live as well.
Thomas Jefferson said that if you believe that the people are not sufficiently informed to exercise the power of demos in society, the democratic solution is not to take power from their hands but to inform them. Informing others of the requirement for a morality suited to our time is not a quixotic endeavor. If people realize that there is a real need for a shared morality, and that abiding by it does not entail undue sacrifice, they will not fail to respond.
Abiding by a global morality does not call for serious sacrifice. Living in a way that enables others in the biosphere to live as well does not mean being self-denying: you can continue to strive for excellence and beauty, personal growth and enjoyment, even for comfort and luxury. But in the context of a global morality, the pleasures and achievements of your life are to be defined in regard to the quality of enjoyment and level of satisfaction they provide rather than in terms of the amount of money they cost and the level of resources that they require.
REMINDER: I KNOW THAT CHANGE IN SOCIETY DOES NOT COME FROM THE TOP—IT COMES FROM THE
Population biologists Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould came forth with the principle that the dynamics of human populations are the same as the dynamics of complex systems in general. That dynamics is not the Darwinian dynamics based on chance mutation and natural selection, resulting in the survival of the fittest, and it does not produce a linear fitness-based evolution. Change in society is nonlinear and radical: it is what in the systems sciences is known as a “bifurcation.” This form of change occurs in the world of nature, and it also occurs in human society. The dominant species in a population does not change when the world around it does: it dies out. Change comes from the periphery, where nascent cultures are constantly mutating, searching for the opportunity for their mutations to “nucleate”— spread to the center and create overall systems change.
This is also the nature of the evolution of society. It is unrealistic to expect that the dominant layers of society will change when conditions in society or its environment change. What is likely is that sooner or later the level of tension becomes so high that the entire system threatens to implode. If it does, its dominant layers will lose their privileged position. A new
condition will come about, a condition of turbulence, resembling chaos. In that condition (known in the systems sciences as “creative chaos”) even a small group of people can, and often does, trigger decisive change. This is why anthropologist Margaret Mead was right when she said never to doubt that a small group of people can change the world. And why she was only slightly exaggerating when she added that nothing else ever did.
FOSTER THE SHIFT FROM EXTENSIVE GROWTH TO INTENSIVE DEVELOPMENT
When we seek to see the future in store for us, we do not see what will be the future—for that is not decided yet—but we can take a realistic guess as to what it could be. In the context of the human world, the future could be more than one kind. The road immediately ahead of us forks into at least two major branches. One leads toward chaos and anarchy, and the other toward a sustainable and flourishing world. They both signal fundamental change in the world—a transformation. There is no branch that would allow us to go forward smoothly and continuously, step-by-step.
Discovering the nature of the positive branch of the coming transformation is not an insuperable task. We know that it departs from the kind of development we have experienced for the past hundred years and more. We have been oriented toward a kind of development we can best describe as extensive growth. Its orientation is toward conquering people not as servants and slaves, but as clients and consumers. It moves along a horizontal plane on the surface of the planet: it acquires ever more territories, colonizes ever more people, and imposes the will of the dominant layer of society on more and more people.
The alternative to extensive growth is intensive development. Rather than moving horizontally along the surface of the planet, intensive development moves in a vertical dimension. It affects first and foremost the perception and consciousness of people. It reaches to greater heights in the development of consciousness, and plumbs to greater depths in regard to the relations that form between people, and people and nature.
The ends and means of intensive development and of extensive growth are radically different. A basic end of extensive growth is extending our technologically potentiated power over ever-larger areas. Historically, the means to this end has been conquest: the conquest of nature and the conquest
of other, weaker, and less power-and-domination-oriented peoples. Conquest led to the colonization of the natural environment and of other cultures, subjugating them to the aspirations and interests of the conquerors.
For most of recorded history, extensive growth was accomplished by force of arms. In modern times, it was also accomplished by economic means, using the power of wealthy states and business companies to impose their will and values on ever-wider layers of the population. For nation-states, the goal of extensive growth was the extension of territorial sovereignty, including sovereignty over the human and natural resources of the territories. The corresponding goal for business companies was to generate more and more demand for their products and services, using what has been called “expeditionary marketing” (imposing or promoting the values that fuel the demand).
The ends of extensive growth can be encapsulated in three C’s: conquest, colonization, and consumption. They were served by specially adapted varieties of technologies. First, the technologies that use and transform matter: the technologies of production. Second, the technologies that generate the power to operate matter-transforming technologies: energy-generating technologies. And third, the technologies that whet people’s appetite, create artificial demand, and shift patterns of consumption: the technologies of expeditionary marketing—propaganda, PR, with enticements of coming happiness, wealth, and other forms of reward.
In our time the first of these kinds of technologies built cities and countries with networks of transportation and communication, and increasingly powerful structures for producing an ever-growing quantity and variety of products. The second of these kinds of technologies harnessed the forces of nature to drive the technologies that transform raw and processed materials into finished products. And the third—the technologies of information—channeled people’s wants and aspirations into demand for the products and services of commercial companies.
In intensive development, the ends and means are very different. They can be grasped under three different C’s: connection, communication, and consciousness.
Take connection first. One of the great fallacies of the modern age has been the separation of individuals from one another and from nature. In the economic sphere, this created a disjunction of people’s individual interests from the interests of their society. Individual interests have been served by
applying the principles of classical physics. Like the mass points of Newton and the stones, bricks, and units of mechanical engineering, humans appeared to be self-contained, mutually independent chunks of organized matter, only externally related to one another and their environment. Classical economics reinforced this fallacy by viewing the individual as a self-centered economic actor, pursuing his or her own interests, in the best case harmonized with the interests of others through the workings of the market. But today’s cutting-edge science has overcome the assumption of separateness in the physical sphere. Now a well-founded major aim of intensive development can be to deepen interpersonal connections in the social, economic, political, and ecological spheres.
The second end of intensive evolution is directly linked with the first. It is to raise the level of consciousness in society. In intensive development, communication unfolds on multiple levels. It is also to enable people to relate to one another and to the world around them more spontaneously, thoroughly, and effectively. Intensive development empowers people to evolve their consciousness and recognize themselves as embedded participants in their social, cultural, and natural milieu.
Intensively developed individuals do not aim to receive the material benefits of extensive growth. They have better, higher ends in mind. It is not toward the acquisition of material goods that they are aiming, but toward the evolution of their own consciousness. Intensive development serves as the means to raise the overall level of coherence—and hence of health and well-being—in society. It creates conditions that are better adapted to the flourishing of human life than the self-centered and shortsighted aspirations of extensive growth.
MUTATE YOUR EPIGENETIC, NOT YOUR GENETIC, SYSTEM
When the environment in which a species finds itself changes, for it to survive, the way its members live and reproduce has to change as well. When the species is threatened with extinction, this change is radical. It comes as a brutal fact—to either produce a viable mutation, or face extinction.
In nonhuman species, the change this requires is genetically coded: it is a shift in the genetic pool. For humans, the situation is different. When the survival of a human population is threatened, the change it requires does not
call for a change in the genetic pool. Although some aspects of human behavior are genetically coded, the values and beliefs that decide human survival are under not genetic but epigenetic control. The epigenetic system (the system that controls the functioning of the genes) is sensitive to the will and intention of individuals. How individuals live and reproduce has more to do with the way their epigenetic system operates than with the way their genes are coded.
We are epigenetically coded organisms. According to the way our epigenetic system operates—how it turns our genes on and off—we choose and adopt, or refuse and discard, preferences and ways of living and acting.
Changes in the epigenetic system are far more rapid than mutations in the genetic pool. The epigenetic system is sensitive to human values and intentions, and hence change in it is rapid and can be goal-directed, whereas mutation in the genetic system is random and protracted, and its full range of effects is unpredictable. It relies on trial and error. Today we can bypass that process, because due to the sensitivity of the epigenetic system to our values, intentions, and aspirations, we can act directly on the dynamics of the system that governs our behavior.
The future that awaits us need not wait for an adaptive mutation of our genes. Our future will be the fruit of cultural, and not of genetic, mutation. It will be decided by our culture: how our values and preferences influence our epigenetic system. How they do so comes about in consequence of how we think, feel, and act. Thus the changes reflect how we think, feel, and act.
This gives all of us a degree of control over our future that is unparalleled in the history of life on Earth. We have not used this leverage consciously, although insightful people such as Barbara Marx have been calling for “conscious evolution” time and time again.
It is time that you begin to evolve consciously. You can create the cultural mutation that changes your epigenetic system, so that it changes your genetic operative system. And then the “new man” so often discussed by visionaries and philosophers can arise in you and your children, and open a new phase in the history of humankind on the planet.
CHANGE NOW!
The transformation wrought by the global health crisis poses unprecedented challenges. In the past, a change could percolate through the body of society
little by little, taking years, decades, and sometimes centuries to become tangible. Civilization changed and evolved over several generations. With a slower rhythm of change, people could adjust and adapt their ties to one another and to their environment. Today this is not the case. The crucial period for creating adaptive change is compressed into the span of a few years.
We could afford the luxury of moving forward through trial and error in the past, but we cannot afford it today. The global crisis intervened. It came on top of a number of critical conditions. It is forcing all of us to change NOW.
The overexploitation of resources and the impairment of nature, coupled with the unequal distribution of wealth and power, launched us on a bifurcation, a rapid and irreversible process. In a bifurcation many things are possible, but going back to prior conditions is not among them. We cannot go backward nor can we stay put; we can go only forward. But the direction in which we are going is not predetermined; we have a choice.
But what is this choice? A Chinese proverb warns, “If we do not change direction, we will end up just where we are going.” Applied to the contemporary world, this would be disastrous. As we have seen, the business of business-as-usual was heading us toward a system-wide crisis, and ultimately collapse. Without a change in direction we would be on the way to a world of increasing population pressure and spreading poverty; growing social and political confrontation; accelerating climate change; food and energy shortages; worsening industrial, urban, and agricultural pollution of air, water, and soil; and the thinning of the ozone layer. We also run the risk of megadisasters caused by nuclear accidents and leaking nuclear waste, devastating floods and tornadoes triggered by the unbalanced climate, and widespread health problems due to natural catastrophes as well as to such industry- and consumption-catalyzed processes as the accumulation of toxins in soil, air, and water.
These risks are real. The bulk of humanity in societies both rich and poor, Western and traditional, remains fascinated by material goods, personal wealth, and ostentatious and wasteful lifestyles. Most people hold an egocentric view of nature: we are free to manipulate the environment as we wish, without having to be overly concerned about the consequences. They hold a Darwinian view of life. It is a struggle for survival, with the powerful reaping the benefits. The universe is a passive backdrop to human actions,
governed by deterministic laws. Individual actions and the values that inspire them do not have the power to change the way things are going. There is no need for us to feel responsibility for what happens around us; the world goes to where it is already going; it is not our call to interfere with it.
The complacency and inaction motivated by such beliefs has led to critical conditions in the ecological and social spheres of today’s world. But these beliefs are not valid—they are obsolete, and outright dangerous. The world is not a giant machine evolving mechanistically from the past to the present, and from the present to the future. In the real world, the future is not determined by the past—it is determined by how we perceive the present.
THE NEW IMPERATIVES
Think Globally
There are a number of “imperatives” confronting you today: dictates you either respect, or face dire consequences. Lifting the level of your thinking is one of them.
Global thinking is not thinking in general categories, or in millions and billions, whether of humans, hectares, or barrels of oil. It is thinking in terms of processes rather than structures, in terms of dynamic wholes rather than static parts. Its benefit is not to obtain a catalogue of ready-made blueprints for making proper choices in any and all circumstances; rather, it is to acquire the perspective by which we can make wise choices of our own.
Thinking in new and in better ways is a uniquely human capability. In the higher animals basic survival-related behavior remains guided by instinct, and instinct changes slowly through the processes of genetic mutation and natural selection. The dominance of experience over instinct is what distinguishes the rapid cultural evolution of humans from the slower genetic evolution of animals. You can learn from experience, and your conscious assessment of experience can steer your behavior, transcending your inherited motivations. Global thinking informs the choices you make in the private as well as the professional spheres of your life. When you choose consumer products for personal use, do you choose fancy items that use a great deal of energy or simple functional devices that do the job with a minimum of waste and fuss? When you choose your work or profession, do you strive to amass the most money in the shortest time or choose to engage in an activity that is meaningful in itself and beneficial to others? Global
thinking also enters into the choice of the style you select for your home: Do we want a home designed for ostentation or one that inspires coziness and sociability? How you clothe yourself and your family is likewise informed by considerations of wisdom: Do you dress to be conspicuous and to feed your ego, or for genuine self-expression and to preserve family and community values and your cultural heritage?
Acting in a globally conscious way is not difficult, and it does not entail undue sacrifices. When you think globally, your life becomes richer and healthier, and you have the assurance of acting as you should, to the best of your ability. More than that, nobody can reasonably ask of you.
Rely on Your Inner Authority
The basic imperative of our time is to create a balanced system that allows all life on Earth to flourish. This is a monumental task, but in tackling it you need not only rely on the limited wisdom of our authorities and institutions. Your real guidance does not come from above and beyond—it comes from within and below. It is the guidance deriving from your sense of who you are, and how you relate to others and to nature.
The authority you can and should follow is deep and wise. It is your own deep self. Being a part of the web of life, whatever force or impetus forms and informs that web is “in” you—it is in your conscious and your subconscious. And so, instinctively and intuitively you know what science is now telling us: that life is not a jungle where the strongest survive, but a system of mutual harmonization. This system is guided by the empathy you feel, your sense of belonging, and ultimately by the unconditional love that is the hallmark of an advanced mindset. This is not new: it is what religious prophets and spiritual masters have been telling us all along, and what quantum physicists, quantum biologists, and transpersonal psychologists can now confirm.
The guidance of your inner self is healthy, far healthier than the relentless pursuit of riches and power. We are social beings, and we can live like such beings. Living like that is good for us, and good for the world. Living in harmony with others and with nature is healthier, happier, and more rewarding than chasing after self-centered, short-term satisfactions. Intellectually and rationally, you understand this. But you will not follow it unless you also feel it. And that means turning to your inner guidance: to the code for health and integrity that is present in you, as it is present in every
organism. This is the safe, the reasonable, and in the last count the truly effective way to choose the right path ahead.
Live in Harmony with the Diversity in the World
Living in harmony with other people and other communities is an eternally valid imperative, applying to all times. We now live, and predictably will live in the foreseeable future, in a multicultural, highly diverse world. Diversity is not a drawback: it is an advantage. It ensures greater flexibility and adaptability. It brings into play a larger range of alternatives.
Diversity, however, is constantly threatened in our world. Dominant values overlay the diversity of values and claim universal applicability. Yet the truly universal value is the value of diversity: this is a timeless truth. In order to achieve it, you must safeguard the diversity that exists, and transform the threat of uniformity into the valuation of harmonized diversity. You need to rethink your habitual values and behaviors.
We all need to recognize that not all people are like we are. Other people may think differently, have different values and aims, and different lifeways. We need to coexist with them just the same. This calls for a process of learning, since most people in the modern world tend to think that everybody is like them. Fundamentally, everyone wants the same things: money, power, sex, and having a good time.
This popular belief does not check with reality. While it is true that the values of Western technological society shape many people’s behavior, a great deal of diversity remains beneath. Whether in the Balkans, in the Middle East, or elsewhere in the world, there is a need for better understanding the cultural differences among people and societies. The spread of the internet, McDonaldism, Coca-Colonization, and the emergence of a global consumer culture did not eliminate cultural diversity in the world; they only mask it.
There is persisting diversity in the way people view themselves, their society, and nature. People have different ideas of justice, dignity, liberty, and the good life. Disregarding their diversity produced disastrous consequences—bloodbaths in the Balkans, Ireland, the Middle East, the Arab world, sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, the Indian subcontinent, and Southeast Asia. Even international terrorism has a cultural basis: it is fed by an intense hatred of everyone who is different, holding a different view of right and good, and the nature of a higher reality.
Understanding and appreciating the diversity of values and beliefs in contemporary societies is essential if there is to be lasting peace on Earth. A peaceful world is built not by eliminating cultural differences, but by cooperation inspired and motivated by the differences.
Last but Not Least: Find Meaning in What You Do
The lack of meaning is one of the most disturbing maladies in today’s world. Lacking meaning in the work we do is a particularly noxious expression of it. We have to overcome this malady.
There are various degrees of meaningfulness in what you do, including the job you accepted to make a living. At the bottom is the work you do to get paid—you need to get paid, it doesn’t really matter what you are doing. On the next level, there are things you do because you are interested in them, they are a hobby, or are simply fun to do. That seems to be the ideal case, but it is still not that, because this kind of work could be just indulging a fancy.
To reach a higher level, you need to ask, Is the work I am doing truly meaningful—does it serve some higher or better purpose? If you can say yes, you are wise—or perhaps just lucky. Of course, sometimes meaningful work may be hard work. Sometimes it calls for great effort, even for sacrifice. Think of the martyrs—they don’t sacrifice themselves unless they are convinced that reaching their goals is worth it. Or think of great artists who live in garrets and sacrifice their everyday well-being to fulfill themselves through their art.
What level of meaningfulness you attain depends on how ambitious you are. But certainly, you should aspire to get beyond the lowest level. When you are doing something just to make money, then you forget it as soon as the job is done. You close the door of the office or shop, and live as if your job had no further meaning for you or for anyone else.
Best is to combine your private life and social activity in a way that they are objectively meaningful as well as subjectively enjoyable. Then you can be fulfilled at the same time as you keep body and soul together. And if the meaning you find is truly valid, what you do serves some purpose beyond making money or having fun. Serving that purpose can be, and should be, meaningful in itself, and fulfilling for your life.
THE PARAMOUNT WISDOM PRINCIPLE
For finding your way forward, you need wisdom—better, more reliable wisdom than the wisdom of the day. As this collection of wisdom principles testifies, there are principles that harbor true and reliable wisdom. One such principle is of particular importance, and making use of it could help you and those around you to meet the challenges you face. It is the “paramount wisdom principle.” We have seen it in action: applied to health, business, politics, and lifestyle. Here we distill and restate the core insight carried by this paramount principle.
We can convey the gist of the paramount principle in a single phrase. What is good for the whole is good for the part. We should say, necessarily and always good for the part. This principle has been intuitively applied in traditional cultures, but has been neglected in modern societies. The contemporary world has applied precisely the contrary principle: what is good for the part (that is, me and mine) is bound to be good for the whole (which is everybody and everything else). If this is the case, we don’t need to worry about everybody and everything else; we can pursue our own good with a clear conscience, as ultimately it is the good for everybody.
This is a fallacy, and a dangerous one at that. Because what is good for the part may not be good for the whole—at least, not always and immediately. This could be a problem. Embraced by an economic and political power, this “reverse wisdom principle” leads to unsustainable gaps and stresses in the world. The good of the whole remains at best a pious wish —it is not a real concern. Only the part counts: the part, namely, which is the agent who embraces and applies the principle. This is a dangerous fallacy.
The correctly formulated wisdom principle applies to all complex systems. It applies to the whole community of life on the planet, to all species, populations, ecologies, and communities. We are social beings embedded in communities—in embracing networks of communities. The community of our nuclear family is part of the community of our town or region, and that in turn is part of the larger community of our state and nation. Together these create a community that is the community of all nations and peoples—the planetary community.
The planetary community is still not the highest-level community on the planet: it, too, is embedded in a higher-level community—the community of all life in the biosphere. That community is “the whole” which is the standard and the norm for establishing the good for all the communities that are its parts. What is good for the planetary community of all life in the biosphere is
good for me, and for you and for all the people and things with which you are associated. This is not a chimera of idealistic imagination; it is supported by the concept emerging at the leading edge of science.
One of the most striking validations of the paramount principle comes from the life sciences. It turns out that the living organism is healthy when it is whole—when all its parts are working together to maintain it in the living state. This “working together” is essential for maintaining the system in the living state. The living organism is not a mere aggregate of its parts; it is their functional integration. It is governed by a potent “program” of information. The program that enables the organism to live is the program that makes it whole.
Because the systems that emerge in nature are not the mechanistic aggregates of their parts, they cannot be reduced to their parts. Systems in nature are “irreducibly whole.” When any of their parts are damaged, the whole system is damaged. And when any of their parts are removed or become dysfunctional, the whole system collapses.
While the biological organism has multiple backups providing a great deal of redundancy, it is still the case that when any of its elements fail to function, or be functionally integrated with the rest, the system is not healthy. When this “dis-integration” reaches a critical threshold, it becomes a “dis-ease” in and of the system. At a further point the “dis-integration” becomes a chronic disease, and then it could be fatal. The paradigm case for this is cancer. In a cancer, a group of cells became “dis-integrated” from the rest: it reproduces and grows independently. It ends up by killing the whole of which it was—and no longer is—an integral part.
Wholeness is an attribute of all complex and coherent systems on the planet. What is good for the whole—including the community of life on Earth—is always and necessarily good for every part: every living organism. The paramount principle is universal. Recognizing it and aligning yourself with it is the secret of health and flourishing for you, and for every living being on the planet.
THE WISDOM INSIGHTS: A SUMMARY
WHAT IS THE WORLD?
The world is an intrinsically whole quantum system, where all
things are connected beyond the hitherto recognized bounds of space and time. In this system, the phenomena that meet our eye are not units and ensembles of a substance called “matter,” but sets and clusters of vibration. The vibrations are ordered: they are coherent. The universe’s parameters are precisely correlated: variations of the order of one-billionth of the value of some constants (such as the mass of elementary particles, the speed of light, the rate of expansion of galaxies, and two dozen others) would have resulted in a universe without stable atoms and stable interaction among them. Even a minute variation of some of the basic physical constants would have precluded the evolution of life. The universe is a coherent system, with parameters finely tuned together and consistent with the system’s overall dimensions. Atoms, complex and coherent clusters of vibration, bond into complex and coherent clusters of molecules. Molecules bond into multimolecular, physical-chemical structures, and these are templates for the formation of still more complex and coherent biological structures. Some species of biological structures bond in turn into social and ecological structures. There is a consistent if nonlinear progression in the universe from level to level of structure and organization, where each additional level manifests higher levels of complexity and coherence. Evolution is the consistent and universal buildup of coherent complex systems in space and time. The laws of nature function as algorithms for the clustering of quanta, atoms, and molecular assemblies into complex and coherent physical, biological, and then socioecological systems.
THE DYNAMICS OF EVOLUTION
The universe is the template for the evolution of coherent and complex integrated ensembles of diverse but coordinated elements: biological, and bioecological and biosocial systems. The dynamics of evolution is not progression toward a passive adaptation to the environment, rather, it is the active exploration of the rungs of complexity and coherence in the given environment at the given time.
Accomplishing this evolution calls for an enduring flow of information between the systems and their environments. In comparatively evolved systems this flow is articulated and coordinated by a specialized organ: the brain. The brain maps the internal and external states relevant to the survival of the organism. The human brain is also capable of mapping states that are not directly relevant to the physical and biological viability of the organism. It can respond to subtle signals that supervene on the sensory signals that map the organism’s relations to its environment. These signals are ordinarily masked by those that map the organism’s survival requirements, but they can reach consciousness in states of special receptivity. In these “spiritual” or “mystical” states the in-formation of the organism by the universal evolutionary impetus reaches consciousness. This creates a concious drive in sensitive individuals toward coherence with the world that lies beyond their immediate surroundings.
THE HUMAN ROLE IN EVOLUTION
The basic motivation of the many forms of culture is a response to coherence-seeking in individuals. Religion and spirituality, music, art, science, literature, and philosophy, in their various forms and manifestations do not primarily serve the satisfaction of human survival needs. Basically, they serve the motivation of individuals to align with the evolutionary drive for coherence. The various forms of culture are specific manifestations of the universal motivation for coherence by individuals, and by social and cultural groups and communities of individuals. Achieving coherence in human experience calls for a meaningful ordering of the elements of experience. Such ordering can be physical, in the fashioning of artifacts, or virtual, in the form of creating systems of ideas and images. They produce aesthetic satisfaction, spiritual fulfillment, mystical revelation, scientific or philosophical insight, and the perception of beauty and significance in nature and the world at large. It is achieved through rationality in science and philosophy, and through intuition and insight in art, literature, religion, and spirituality.
THE CRUCIAL TASK: OVERCOMING THE COHERENCE LAG
We have become a planetary species forming a global society. We have achieved a high degree of complexity, but are lagging behind in regard to our level of coherence. The crises and disruptions we experience are consequences of our lack of coherence. But this condition is not irremediable. The chance to correct it is now in your hands. The consequences of the disruptions created by the virus are not negative. They harbor the opportunity to overcome the basic flaw: the lag in our level of coherence. Today’s global crisis is a blessing in disguise. It is a timely opportunity for you and people around you to shift from unsustainability-creating incoherence to coherence-based sustainability in the world.     ANCIENT WISDOM APPLIED TO THE MODERN DIET
    Cooked whole grains, in particular, constituted humanity’s staple food for thousands of years, and, until fairly recently, were eaten as the staple food throughout the world. For example, rice and millet were principal foods in the Far East; wheat, oats, and rye in Europe; buckwheat in Russia and Central
    Asia; sorghum in Africa; barley in the Middle East; and corn in the Americas. In fact, the English word for food is “meal,” or ground grain, while in Japanese, the term used for meal is “gohan,” which means cooked rice.
    Using a natural diet as medicine was a widespread practice in the Far East as well as in the West. In the medical book of the Yellow Emperor, his physician Qui Bo suggests that chronically ill patients should take as medicine whole rice soup for ten days. In the fifth century BCE, Hippocrates himself healed with correct nutrition, among other things with full-grain barley. He is said to have written “Let your nutrition be your medicine, and the medicine your nutrition.” In the classical version, the Hippocratic oath contains the following paragraph, “I will apply dietetic measures for the benefit of the sick according to my ability and judgment; I will keep them from harm and injustice.” Even the term “macrobiotics” was first used by Hippocrates. Other classical thinkers, including Herod, Aristotle, Galen, and Lucian, insisted on a balanced diet as the basis for natural lifestyle and active old age.
    Advice similar to that of the ancients crops up in the modern age. For example, in the year 1713, Kaibara Ekken, a Japanese doctor and Confucian scholar, added to Yojokun, the book of long life and health, the science of applying a diet of whole-grain rice instead of trying to cure the symptoms of a disease with medication or surgery. In 1793, Christoph Wilhelm Hufeland (1762–1836), a German doctor and philosopher, published a book titled Macrobiotics, or the Art of Prolonging Life. He outlined the relationship between nutrition and health.
    The diet called by Hufeland “macrobiotics” was revived in the nineteenth century in Japan. A diet of natural foods was part of the culinary tradition of Japan. The Japanese diet-culture was traditionally based on fresh produce and fish; people did not consume meat until the middle of the nineteenth century. As we shall discuss in more detail below, the Japanese diet was composed of vegetables and grains, especially rice, millet, bean, fruits, algae, and various seafood. Like other Oriental people, the Japanese did not eat dairy products, since the genetic makeup of Asian and African people is not suited for digesting lactalbumin and lactose. Instead of dairy products, they consumed a wide variety of bean paste (“tofu”) based on soybean. The daily diet also included miso and shoyu (made of fermented soybeans); these contain all essential amino acids needed by the organism. Teas (both green and bancha) were likewise part of the diet; bancha, a tea dried on the bush for three years,
    contains eight times the amount of calcium found in cow’s milk. Bancha tea served before and in the middle of meals, as well as green tea served at the end, was and still is an indispensable element of Japanese cuisine.
    The spreading of meat consumption in the last third of the nineteenth century posed a challenge to the digestive system of the Japanese people. Before their organisms could adjust to it, the consumption of meat created serious problems of health. Overcoming this problem was made difficult by the pronounced influence on Japan of European culture and science. Physicians used almost exclusively therapies based on Western medicine.
    Sagen Ishizuka (1850–1909), a high-ranking Japanese army doctor, though trained in Western medical science, criticized the adoption of Western healing and dietary theories, and recommended the Japanese traditional diet. This was due to his own healing experience, as well as his research in biochemistry. He cured many patients by having them use a traditional diet based on brown rice, and a variety of land and sea vegetables.
    Based on his lifelong experiments and practices, Ishizuka created a theory of nutrition and healing based on the traditional Asian diet, complemented by elements from Western chemistry, biology, biochemistry, and physiology. He compiled the information and conclusions of his work in two books: The Chemical Theory of Longevity, published in 1896, and Diet for Health, published in 1898. Both books appeared in Japanese, and have not been translated into Western languages to this day.

Grain is the staple food of a human being. Food should be unprocessed, whole, and natural. Food should be grown locally and eaten in season.

Ohsawa cured himself of a serious disease by changing from the modern diet then sweeping Japan to a diet of whole rice, miso soup, sea vegetables, and other traditional foods. After his recovery he undertook to reconcile Eastern medicine and philosophy with other intellectual and spiritual traditions, including the Vedantic, Jewish, and Christian doctrines.
    Ohsawa developed the philosophy and daily practice that was called “macrobiotics” or the “Unique Principle.” It was based on a new formulation of the laws of change and balance according to the ancient Chinese unifying principle of yin-yang, which saw the paradoxical and dialectical unity of opposites. More specifically, it emphasized the application of yin-yang to food, health, and medicine. In short, he saw macrobiotics as the practical biological and physiological application of the basic principle of Eastern philosophy. For Ohsawa, macrobiotics was emphatically not “a diet” but rather an approach to diet, a comprehensive philosophy of the principles of nutrition, a diet in the modern sense of the word, and a way of living that encompasses all aspects of life. Like the posture of yoga, food was only a vehicle, a sacred vehicle. Eating was a sacred and ritual act.
    After Ohsawa’s passing, his disciples, Michio Kushi and Herman Aihara, carried on his legacy. They continued to teach macrobiotics in Japan, Europe, North America, and South America. The same applies to plants, such as wheat, rice, millet, and oats grown in one and the same location, and of course even more if they are grown in different locations.

    WISDOM FOR THE YOUNG
    Kingsley L. Dennis
    When we look up into the night sky and see the sparkle of stars, we are often awed and enchanted. We recognize the grace, the wonder, and the excitement of the unknown. Everything comes alive with possibility. That possibility is always in potential, if we are ready and willing to acknowledge it. It never goes away—only sometimes it is forgotten amid the daily rush of our lives. It is also all too easy for people to close their eyes—their hearts and minds— and cut off the connection. Yet there remains an enchanted world out there, and it beckons, waiting for humanity to respond to its call. Underlying all life is the quest for meaning. Wisdom provides meaning for our lives, no matter how far along the road of life we are. And “wisdom” does not mean only the grand, sweeping vistas of knowledge about life in the cosmos—it is also, importantly, about the more essential things in our lives. The small acts that create meaning and purpose for our individual journeys through every uncharted path that each person walks upon in their lifetime. No two paths are the same. And we each begin walking our unique paths from the very first baby steps we take in this world. Wisdom is ageless. Wisdom, too, is not only about knowing—it is also about the learning.
    The young need not rush headlong through life in a desperate wish for gaining wisdom. For wisdom is not an end prize but a gradual accumulation. One person who knew this well was Alan Watts. He often spoke about how life should not be lived as a fast journey and that existence in the universe should be recognized as being basically playful. Life is more like music, Watts used to say. And we play music—we don’t “work” music. And in music, the end of the composition is not the point of the composition; otherwise, all conductors would play fast; or some composers would choose to write only the finales. We don’t go to concerts just to hear the final chord being played. We don’t engage in a dance in order to end it (unless we got tricked into it!). And yet as Alan Watts was so keen in observing, much of
    modern life conditions us into grading our lives. As an example, schooling often compels students into chasing grades and making quotas—which then later translates into paying the bills. All the time, we keep on believing, hoping, wishing, for the “great thing” in life to come while we are rushing through our lives with hardly a notice of what we’re leaving behind in our rearview mirrors. In this way, we end up living to retire. And when we retire, we imagine we have “finally arrived.” And yet to where? Do we feel any different? We have a small pot of savings and almost no energy. And then we are told to wait it out. Until what? When? The final curtain? Perhaps only when it is too late do we realize that we were running the whole way through life—we kept racing along with ourselves in order to keep up—aiming for a destination instead of enjoying the journey. Wisdom may help those at the earlier stages of their life to realize that there is no point in rushing to get to the end of the journey only to then ask the question, Did we miss the whole point?
    Wisdom is not a point to gain in one finale. The foods we digest are not enjoyed by finishing their eating but by enjoying and feeling “filled” during the eating. Similarly, life should be about “filling” us while we are living it— not when we are finishing it. Wisdom is not only about human doing but perhaps more about human being.
    BEING HUMAN
    Being human is about being simple. Or rather, it is about recognizing the essential things. Yet this is no simple thing to do. Life gives us an opportunity to get back to ourselves in so many ways. Instead of living life by receiving external impacts, we should consider that life is as much about what we project outward from ourselves into life. Life molds us as much as we mold it. Living is an integrative and mutually participating experience. There is wisdom in participating in the “to-ing” and “fro-ing” of life rather than seeing it as only a one-way street. If we do not experience the varieties that life offers, it is difficult to gain a rounded and fuller perspective on the spectrum of human living. Of course, the perfect pot was proud of its accomplishments, feeling accepted and appreciated. But the poor cracked pot was ashamed of its own imperfection, and miserable that it was able to accomplish only half of what it had been made to do. After two years of what it perceived to be a bitter failure, it spoke to the man one day by the stream.

    load because this crack in my side causes water to leak out all the way back to your house. Because of my flaws, you have to do all of this work, and you don’t get full value from your efforts,” the pot said.
    The man felt sorry for the old cracked pot, and in his compassion, he said, “As we return to my house, I want you to look at the beautiful flowers along the path. It will make you feel better.”
    Indeed, as they went up the hill, the old cracked pot took notice of the sun warming the beautiful flowers on the side of the path, and this made it feel a little happier. But at the end of the path, it still felt bad because it had leaked out half its load, and so again the pot apologized to the man for its failure.

    THE PRIMACY OF INFORMATION IN THE UNIVERSE AND IN THE LIVING WORLD
    The new paradigm emerging in the sciences recognizes that the universe is not random; evolution is a multidimensional and quasi-universal process; and human beings, the same as all forms of life, are an integral part of it.     INFORMATION IN LIVING SYSTEMS: IMPLICATIONS FOR THE MEDICAL SCIENCES
    A recognition of the paramount role of information in the world of life holds major implications for the medical sciences.
    Traditionally, the branches of medicine have been dedicated to the maintenance of health as much as to the cure of disease. Contemporary mainstream medicine is now focused above all on the cure of disease. It attempts to correct cellular and organic malfunctions mainly by biochemical means. Its remarkable achievements have prolonged human life expectancy and eliminated, or produced cures for, a plethora of diseases. However, mainstream medicine is more accomplished in curing or eliminating diseases than in ensuring that the living system persists in a condition of health and vitality. Unlike in traditional societies, in the modern world physicians are called in to cure malfunctions in their patients rather than to maintain them in a condition of well-being.

There is a need to take into account the flows and balances that ensure health and vitality in the whole organism, rather than concentrating mainly on the causes of a malfunction in a part. The realization that information is a major factor in the functioning of the whole organism offers a basis for extending the focus of attention to the whole, without neglecting
    the part. Information governs the processes of life in every part of the organism,

A disease surfacing as a cellular or organ malfunction in a part implies a flaw in the information that regulates processes in the whole. However, mainstream medicine attempts to rebalance vital processes in the part by imparting corrective information to that part. It conveys corrective information by the administration of molecular compounds primarily of synthetic origin. This offers a successful cure to many diseases, but in itself fails to ensure the sustained vitality of the whole.

This requires, first, that we pay due attention to the curative and health-preserving potentials of natural substances. These substances are produced in and by the organism or in its life-supporting environment, as the result of long chains of trial and error. They are likely to contain or complement the information needed to maintain the organism in a condition of health and vitality.

These practices and remedies are mostly dismissed if not actually ignored by mainstream medicine. Yet many of them can be tested and rendered more precise and beneficial by the use of instruments that measure energy and information flows in the whole organism, as well as in a given part.

The rediscovery of the health-restoring and maintaining potentials of body-mind interaction is a major achievement of the neurosciences. The results need to form an integral part of the enlarged scope of modern medicine,

The mind-body system is in fact an inseparable unit able to perceive and give a sense to the information and interact in an intelligent way with it. This ability to give sense to the information is what we call consciousness. Consciousness is a bearer of deep values for the human being, and these values are essential to endow science in general with a “soul” and to humanize medicine in particular. The lack of this realization gives rise to all kinds of abuses and manipulations in society, that also impair the balances of the ecosystem.
