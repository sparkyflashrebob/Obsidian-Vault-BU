GRIEF AND SPIRITUAL HEALING
Surviving Life After Loss
Todd Perelmuter

All rights reserved.
Here’s to the lucky ones. The ones who loved. The ones who had giants in their lives. The ones who were lucky
enough to know those God-like figures who occasionally walk among us. The ones blessed to know kindness and goodness. The ones whose lives were better off because of someone in it, no matter how briefly. To you, I give my
full and open heart. Do not be afraid. You are not alone. You will never be alone. We are one.
In one of my last moments with my father, I quietly said to him, "Take care and travel safely." I remained fully present as he took his final breaths. In that sacred moment, I whispered to myself, "Go exploring with curiosity" recognizing that my words could no longer reach him as he journeyed beyond the horizon.
I repeated to myself, “Go exploring with curiosity.”
A few months ago, my father was diagnosed with pancreatic cancer and given only a few months to live. Over this time, I witnessed his decline from a healthy man to a mere 60-pound skeleton.
One of the most heartbreaking moments of my life was when I saw him in the hospital. He had changed so much that I couldn't even recognize him. His once vibrant eyes were now sunken, and his voice, once filled with warmth, was now faint and distant. It was as if the illness had stripped away not only his physical appearance but also his essence, leaving behind a shell of the person I once knew and loved. The sight of him in that state filled me with overwhelming sadness and a deep sense of helplessness.
During this tragic time, Todd and I were also working on a project centered around themes of grief, death, and spirituality—The Miracle of Death. It means that most of the time I was either by my father's side, witnessing his suffering, or engaging with Todd, asking questions related to the themes we were exploring.
Our conversations delved into deep questions surrounding grief and spirituality. Initially, the project served as a way to momentarily escape the pain of the present and immerse myself in the work. However, as Todd guided me deeper into our discussions, the veil of ego, escapism, and other negative emotions began to fade away.
All the beautiful wisdom I learned from Todd during those days held me steadfast during that difficult time. Instead of merely seeing an end, his teachings and his presence awakened me to recognize that death signifies not just an end but also the start of a new journey—a journey into the unknown.
This book is a collection of the liberating and freeing thoughts that Todd shared with me during my difficult times. The project was crafted to serve as a comforting beacon for those navigating the dark tunnel of grief.
May you find peace during these chaotic times.

INTRODUCTION
There’s the family we’re born into, and then there’s the family we choose. Our soul family. The ones we find in every lifetime. We’ll sail across oceans, climb mountains, and cross any border to find them.
I did not choose to write this book. This book chose me. It was thrust upon me against my wishes, against my kicking and screaming.
July 7th, 2023, 9:54 A.M. That was when I got the call that changed my life forever. I was sitting at my desk in my New York apartment on a typical summer day when my whole world came crashing down.
My soul family in India, who I spent years living with while I was in India during my spiritual journey, had called with unthinkable news. The man who had become more than a father to me, was taken to the hospital where it was discovered that he had stage 4 pancreatic cancer.
For anyone who doesn’t know, as I did not, this type of cancer has a five-year survival rate of 1%. After five years, they don’t even keep statistics for it. The average patient lives only one year after diagnosis.
Two days after the call, I was beside his hospital bed in a little hospital just outside of Delhi. The once larger than life figure, the saint of his family, the yogi who taught me so much, was now frail and weak. “How could this be?” I thought.
This man never smoked, never drank, ate only plants because he wouldn’t even kill a spider, and did yoga every day. He gardened for fun, taking such pride only in his plants and his family. He was a model of a human being.
This loving, kind, generous, patient man did not deserve this. Like me, he left his home when he was young to travel India, living as a holy man with nothing but his robes, learning about the spiritual paths of his country which date back thousands of years. If ever there was a perfect human, he was it.
For the next three months, I lived with him and his family, taking care of anything I could and providing whatever comfort I could offer. Daily we saw his weight go down and his pain increase. But he never stopped caring more about everyone else than himself. Even on his last day, he was worried we didn’t get enough tea.
Those next three months were a nightmare of a rollercoaster, constantly flailing me wildly between despair and hope, hope and despair. The whiplash of uncertainty was too much to bear. It was like coming to terms with his death over and over and over again.
The only saving grace were my spiritual practices that I learned during my nine-year spiritual journey. I was prepared for death. I had meditated for thousands of hours on death and impermanence. I had mastered the art of letting go. I knew he was going to a better place. But what I was not prepared for was bearing witness to the immense suffering of his family. This I did not realize would be so hard, but of course it was nothing compared to the suffering they were going through.
It was through his family’s eyes that I saw the need for this book. Everything this man did was a lesson in how to live, and how to die. I knew that he would want his death to mean something, to give hope to others, and I knew that he would want me to write this book.
If there is one thing that I can be thankful for in all of this, it was that I could go through that most difficult of times so that I could truly understand grief in its fullest form. I could not have written this book without this experience. Though no one would ever wish for this, my hope is that I can help at least one person who is suffering loss.
Grief is something we will all experience. It is like getting kicked in the gut by a horse over and over and over again. But there are some brilliant tools our ancestors left for us. There is light at the end of this darkness. There is a way forward. There is life after loss. Take my hand and let’s walk that path together.
CHAPTER 1 How to Fight the Grief Monster
Grief is not like depression or normal sadness. It is a natural and unique process that has its own elements and stages, working in its very own specific region in the brain.
It's better to think of it like a physical entity in our body, an invader virus that is unwelcome and infects us. This type of virus can't be physically removed or killed; it can't even be beaten. Any fight simply fuels its growth, any resistance against it only energizes it.
So, the key is to let it work its course; this is the fastest way to integrate that entity inside of us, that grief monster. The key is to simply witness its rage, to witness its subtlety when it goes dormant, and to notice when it's still present. We have to keep a watchful eye, seeing all of the emotions, thoughts, and physical sensations.
Sometimes we'll think we're getting better, and then it rears its ugly head. But whether we're in a good moment or a bad moment, or experiencing both at the same time, we simply watch it without feeding it. We embrace it, love it, welcome it, and cradle it. This is how we can transform this unwanted visitor into a sacred companion that may even be with us for the rest of our lives. And it probably will be.
But if we honor it, it will honor us. It can be a bit of a curmudgeon if we fight it; it can get angry. But if we nurture and care for it, it can truly be a peaceful presence and even a source of great strength for ourselves and others. If growth in our lives stems from challenges and difficulties, then there is no greater challenge and no greater growth than that which comes from immense loss.
This grief monster doesn't mean to be mean; it just loved someone so much and it can no longer express that attachment.
Grief is the severed attachment between us and a recipient of our love. When our love can't find its person (or pet) anymore, it becomes grief. But deep down, that grief is the pain and healing journey stemming from a broken attachment.
The process of grief is simply that part of our subconscious mind that became used to a person who’s now gone, and it takes this part of the brain longer to come to terms with the loss. Just like how we know the moon is there whether we are looking at it or not, our minds create people and pets as these permanently existing presences in our lives. This is why grief is so shattering. But there are things we can do to get through it a little easier.
We can create ways for our lost loved ones to live on in our lives. We can integrate their memories and their presence into every moment of our lives in ways they weren't before. And we can transform this new relationship with them from one of distance to one of oneness. In this way, we can truly become closer to those we've lost.
CHAPTER 2 How to Be Okay With Feeling Not Okay
We all will at some point, if we live long enough, to watch everyone we know and love die. People have lost their spouses, children, parents, and have gotten through it. It's nice if there is someone to help us through that process, it's a bonus if we can find someone, but it’s not necessary. There are no preconditions needed to start the healing journey.
At the end of the day, grief is a human process. It is something that hopefully we'll all go through because it means we had a good life and we had good people in our lives. Like how sneezing and coughing help expel viruses and bacteria, the symptoms of grief are signs the healing has begun.
There will be extreme hopelessness.
The question is, can we be okay with witnessing hopelessness?
And the answer is yes.
Whatever feelings we have, whatever pain we suffer, we will survive, and it will be okay.
We can't change that pain. But what we can do is see it in a different way. And that is the most important thing we can do because something people don't often realize is that it's not the pain we feel in life or the loss we face, it is the expectation that we shouldn't have loss. The expectation that we shouldn't have pain causes more pain than the loss itself.
When we are in full acceptance, when we accept the unthinkable, when we expect difficulty, it's never surprising when it happens. It's not scary, and it can be what it is. When we wish it away, we create conflict.
Spirituality is about endling that needless suffering. There will be physical pain, there will be the emotional pain of grief and loss. But there is a difference between feeling grief, avoiding it, and witnessing it.
To feel it or avoid it each have their advantages and disadvantages. Feeling it is going to be difficult but healing. Avoiding it may cause addiction or other suppression symptoms like back pain or loss of temper, and it may never be healed.
But to witness is to be separate from, and we are separate from these passing thoughts and emotions. We are the witness who observes these sensations and thoughts. That separate awareness is our consciousness. There is no physical part of the brain or body from where consciousness appears. It is pure energy, from a nonphysical dimension, that source of all life. It is the spark that makes us who we are and not just bags of meat.
When we can connect with that spiritual dimension of pure awareness within, we connect with the lifeforce beneath every physical person and animal, and we can connect to the life of the person we lost because energy is never lost. It is within us and all life. It’s all oneness.
CHAPTER 3 How to Fight Regrets That Come With Loss
When we lose someone unexpectedly, it's very common to have regrets — things we wish we said, things we wish we did. Wishing we took more photographs or videos and had more conversations. But for most of human history, we didn't have video cameras. And when it's something we wish we had said, our loved ones surely know.
We speak through our bodies so much more than our words. Our energy speaks volumes. They are at peace and we don’t have to be so hard on ourselves for saying the wrong thing or not saying the right thing. They have let go of the need to know.
Many things are said without being said. We can have a deep understanding of people without words. Presence and stillness can show the compassion that words can only allude to..
We can still speak with our loved ones after they've gone anytime we wish. They are at peace; we are the ones who must find a way to live on for them. And speaking to them is a very helpful practice and another way we can live with them and have an ongoing relationship.
So there is nothing to regret because we all come from source, that quantum field, that field of consciousness from which all things emerged and to which all things return. There is nothing to regret because we are all always doing our best based on our knowledge and experience at the present moment..
Everything we do is based on countless factors that came before us that we had no control over. We are just creatures bumbling through this crazy world. If there is a perfect being who’s had it all figured out from the day they were born, that would be news to me.
We all come from source, the field of consciousness, the quantum field where all stuff emerges from. This is why all atoms are made of the same stuff. But there is a non-physical element to us, and that is the unchanging consciousness that collapses into a single point of awareness when physical life is born. We come from oneness and we return to oneness. We have a brief experience of separateness before merging back into everything.
In this way, we can almost be happy about loss. We can be happy for our loved ones. And we don't need to be afraid. We don't need to worry about them. We can turn that energy towards living more fully. And that is the best way to honor those we lost.
When we feel guilt or regret for harsh words we’ve said that we wish we could take back, we have to remember that whenever there are two egos involved, there can be a lot of clashes. When one ego goes away such as in death, all that conflict can dissipate. Suddenly we can see that person in a clearer light. These egos create a huge barrier to reality and to other people. Instead of relating to people as they are; we relate from our ego to their ego.
There are these two walls between us and other people. It's very natural, and it's nothing to blame ourselves for; it is the human condition that these egos always want to be right. When there are two egos involved in a relationship, especially two big ones, it can completely prevent two people from connecting.
That's why sometimes after we lose someone, we can put aside whatever grudges we were holding against them. Holding grudges isn't always personal; it can be self-preservation. All of that resentment can wash away when an ego dissolves. When we lose someone, our ego and defenses can come down as well. This can make the feelings of regret exacerbated.
But it's never too late to appreciate someone, even if it's after their loss. We tend to think of time as this linear progression, and we judge our past based on our present. But if we could look at our past from the past, we would see that we would do every single thing in the exact same way, because we were in it, living in that moment. We cannot truly look back on a moment; we can only look at it through the lens of the present moment.
So, if we look at loss as this final moment at the end of the journey, we will always feel that we should have done things differently. But if we look at life as this journey of growth, then there is no regret. There are only lessons and progress, and we can't blame ourselves for not having the tools we have now before we were able to develop those tools.
All regret comes down to being too hard on ourselves, judging ourselves based on an unrealistic standard. When we can accept the loss and accept ourselves, including our past selves, and forgive ourselves, that's part of the growth as well.
So instead of fixating on a past mistake, we can look to the growth and change that we've made. That is more powerful and more healing than living in that alternate universe that we wish had existed, which will always create that internal sense of incompleteness and longing.
The more we accept whatever happened in this real reality — this true universe we're living in — the less that regret will weigh on us. And when we stop fighting with what is, this peaceful perfect present moment will embrace us with the love of the earth. In the present moment lies freedom from a difficult past.
We don’t have to stuff down our pain. Instead we can become present enough to witness. And we can witness ourselves witnessing anything.
CHAPTER 4 How to Avoid Self-Blame While Grieving
Let’s not unnecessarily pollute this grieving process with blame on ourselves for not being able to save them. We don’t need to wonder, “If only I made him go to that doctor’s appointment...” “If I had called, she would have left the house a minute later and not been in that car accident.”
We can't live our lives in what-ifs. We can't judge our past on what we know today. There are a billion things that could have gone differently, and if any had, we would be living in an alternate universe.
Things happen the way they happen for so many reasons, not one little reason. We literally can't blame ourselves.
Many people blame themselves for how they grieve, or put pressure on themselves to grieve in a certain way. But everybody does it differently. Everybody will go through different stages at different times, and for different lengths of time. Simply don’t resist any of that process.
The more we make peace with this chaotic process, the more bearable it becomes. So we don't need to blame ourselves for not doing enough. We don’t need to hate the world, the doctors or ourselves. Let the love that lies at the core of your grief heal and transform you.
There are a lot of situations where we may feel a medical mistake was made or someone seems responsible for the loss, but we have to look at what that resentment is doing to us. Of course, if there is action to be taken like a lawsuit, or if there was truly some kind of malpractice, then letting go of the grudge does not mean we don’t seek justice. It means we do so without the vengeful emotions of hatred, anger or stress.
Ultimately, no amount of thinking about a past event will bring the person back, and it will do nothing for us but bring us a lot more suffering that we don't need.
It's for our own benefit to accept that these things happened. Everyone has their time. We can't go back and change that. All we can do is learn from it. We have to turn self-blaming into lessons that can help our family in the future.
Grief is very tough. The beauty of a true spiritual practice is that it will heal literally every aspect, every pain, whatever you're struggling with. Make peace — and stay at peace — with whatever arises in grief.
It’s like taking off a tight shoe. The foot was fine all along. It was the pressure and tightness that created the pain. So take off the shoe and peacefully relax into the grief and pain. It is absolutely safe to do.
CHAPTER 5 How to Not Fall into Addiction Trying to Escape Grief
There are healthy ways to process grief, and sometimes that may involve returning to some sense of normalcy, like going to a movie and trying to enjoy it. But if we turn to unhealthy methods and rely on them to distract us, we’ll become dependent and addicted, never allowing grief to process and flow through us. This often manifests as addiction or even illness, chronic pain, or stress-related diseases and disorders.
Choosing healthy coping mechanisms is crucial. Practices like yoga, meditation, and exercise can truly help us navigate through grief. While it's easy to dive into work or alcohol, ultimately, we need to face the stages of grief.
Sometimes, we may temporarily set aside our grief to handle daily responsibilities, but at the end of the day it's important to dedicate some time to truly feel the deep love for that person.
When people experience extreme meaninglessness during such phases, it's often their first introduction into the profound reality of life and death. This can lead them to seek meaning through religion, spirituality, or service to others (often in their loved one’s honor, such as running for a cure).
Life is truly an ever-changing, shape-shifting entity. We cannot grasp onto anything because it's all constantly changing.
In times where life painfully reminds us of its impermanence, religion and spirituality can serve as meaningful anchors, helping us feel centered and grounded amidst the sudden upheaval of everything we thought we knew.
We need to find solace in this new emptiness and discover spiritual tools to navigate through it. For me, meditation has been transformative, and many find that dedicating time to a daily practice can expedite the process of grieving.
By giving ourselves undivided attention, we can confront our thoughts and emotions, allowing us to eventually make peace with our loss. This doesn't mean getting rid of grief, but rather accepting it and moving forward in a new relationship that honors the life of our loved one.
CHAPTER 6 How to Not Get Completely Consumed by Grief
Love is the emotion that transforms every other emotion into itself; because within love is peace, joy, and healing. Whenever we feel overcome with grief and it consumes our whole body and we feel that aching pain in our soul, think of those moments when we experienced pure love and affection for the loved ones we've lost, and for the people we live for still today.
This is not stuffing down or avoiding our grief; it is wrapping it in a loving embrace and holding it, holding the space for that pain and allowing every aspect of it. Whether we are crying constantly, or if we can't cry, allow it, allow it all.
We cannot truly let go until we first accept it. It's very tempting to distract ourselves with our phones or work or intoxicants. But that will only keep it inside of us and it will manifest in detrimental ways. Since we're the only person that has access to our own mind, we have got to be able to look honestly and openly at every single thought and feeling, and to witness this process of grief, not fear it.
Fear and pressure and guilt and regret only complicate grief and add to the pain and loss in needless ways. So we can go easy on ourselves. We don't have to choose anything or decide anything or make any big life decisions. We can witness the process. It's a lot easier to accept the grief, pain, and loss than it is to hate it and be angry at it, or to be scared of it.
The more deeply we turn inward, the quicker and easier it'll be to get through to the other side of grief.
Whatever happens in the grieving process is fine, is totally normal, and we can be at peace with whatever it looks like to each one of us.
If we are struggling with that grief, and if it is disturbing our inner peace and disrupting our lives in ways that are not aligned with our greatest good, it can be challenging to prioritize our well-being. But if we are at peace with our grief, we will not want to escape it, we won't need to escape it; it will be a welcomed companion in our lives.
However, if it is something we wish to be over with, that we wish to get out of our system, then it is the escapist mentality that causes needless suffering. This concept is illustrated in a story taught to me by a Buddhist monk. He said, “Life fires the first arrow at us, we fire the second one at ourselves. The key is to turn that second arrow into an acupuncture needle of healing.”
The first arrow represents an unfortunate event that happens in our life and the pain we experience from it. The second arrow represents our aversion to that pain.
When we seek out distractions or harmful behaviors to escape the pain, we only perpetuate our suffering, like walking around with an arrow stuck in us. When we face and feel through the pain, it can heal us.
A person who has learned to sit with their grief is one who doesn't create needless suffering and recognizes the impermanent nature of everything. They understand that every sensation arises and falls away, and that joy and pain are intertwined. A skilled practitioner knows that pain will eventually subside and that within every pain, there is the potential for joy.
By acknowledging the fleeting nature of both good and bad experiences, we can cultivate a mindset that is less consumed by inevitable changes. Reflecting on the impermanence of blessings and challenges alike allows us to find peace and acceptance amidst the ups and downs of life.
The question of whether it's fine to be consumed by grief or if one should be in grief for their entire life doesn't have a straightforward answer. There are no "shoulds" in this world when it comes to grief. Some people may
remain in grief for their whole lives, while others may find it easier to move on. This variation doesn't reflect on the relationship or the person; rather, it can be influenced by factors such as brain chemistry and individual coping mechanisms.
Societal pressures often complicate the grieving process, with some cultures suggesting that the better someone is the more grief they deserve, or that true love means never moving on. There can be immense pressure to remain in an eternal state of mourning, especially after losing a partner.
However, personally, I hope my funeral is a raucously good celebration of my life, where everyone is smiling and living each moment fully and joyfully. Most people would wish the same for their loved ones.
Living for those we've lost is a noble effort, albeit a challenging one. It's a process and a journey, but by cherishing memories and continuing to express our love in new ways, we can navigate through grief.
Love has a healing power that transcends pain and loss and separation. Embracing it can help us find solace and meaning in life's most difficult moments.
CHAPTER 7 How to Not Rush the Grieving Process
I heard a great quote recently from a grief counselor specialist. He said that someone asked him, "How long will I grieve for?" And his response was, "How long will the person you lost be dead for?" This is essential to understand that we will never get over the loss of a loved one. It is not something we forgive or that we can let go.
These are people who were huge parts of our lives. We wouldn't want to forget them if we could, and there's no way we can reach a place where we say, "Yes, that death was fantastic." But what we learn to do is integrate that grief into a way that brings meaning, purpose, and even joy to our lives.
We do this by truly integrating their memories into our lives. We don't try to forget them; we do the opposite. We create a vibrant relationship with their spirit. If you lose a parent, they are literally inside you (at least 50% genetically speaking). Every memory is inside you.
When we know people on a level like that, they speak through us, and they speak to us. We can speak to them; we can hear their guidance; we can know their mind. They are with us all the time, wherever we go. In this way, there is a deeper relationship that can take place.
The sooner we can make grief our friend, the sooner we stop wishing for it to go away, and the more quickly that grief process doesn’t stay so difficult.
It's not that the grief ends; it may be with us for our whole lives. When we have a close bond with someone, we will feel their absence for the rest of our lives.
We don’t want to kill our grief friend or make an enemy of it. But the pain from our grief can shift into good memories and fond feelings. We can feel their continued presence instead of mourn their absence. Grief can energize us instead of drain us. When we infuse that pain of loss with love for that person, we make the grieving process a little easier and more tolerable.
The way to move forward with grief is to come to terms with it. So, we welcome that pain and loss as part of the beautiful expression of that person and our love for them. Once we no longer suffer grief, all that remains are those cherished times and the legacy they left behind, which is one of immense love and of leaving the world better than we found it, leaving all the people we touched better than we found them, and that is the best that any of us can hope to do with our lives as we shift off this physical plane.
Unfortunately, there's no mathematical equation for how long grief will last. But although grief may be carried with us forever, we can either let it fuel our depression, addiction, isolation, physical disease, and all the other destructive things that grief can cause; or we can let that grief, which is unexpressed love, fuel us into helping and saving all the lives we can.
The greatest way to create that balance in ourselves and in the universe after losing a loved one is to ask ourselves, “How can I save someone else? How can I show the love I have inside to the world, even with this immense loss that is still happening within me?” It can be through charity. It can be through showing more kindness and love to those around us. It can be giving back to the earth, gardening, or planting seeds for future generations’ enjoyment.
As we shift focus from our grief to the universe around us, that traveling companion of ours that continues to stay with us no longer takes up our whole awareness. Our love and concern for the planet and all its inhabitants can become much more important and dominant themes in our life.
That feeling of love, both self-love and universal love (which is the love we have for everything beyond us), is the medicine that will heal the pain that grief caused.
Grief is really just love with no place to go. If we are grieving, it's because that person or animal has touched our lives and we can no longer give or receive that love anymore. But if we do recognize that we are all one, that we all have the same life energy within each of us, then we can learn to place that love anywhere, receive it from everywhere, and we will see that nothing can truly be lost.
The minute we stop longing to give our love to that person or pet we lost, and instead we start thinking, “How can I give that love to someone or something right now,” we will start to feel their loving energy again.
CHAPTER 8 How to Not Be Afraid of Life After Loss
Loss is inherently a scary time because we don't know where the person's going. We haven't seen the other side personally. And we don't know what our life looks like without that person.
If we don't know what the grieving process will look like, how long it will take, if it will ever end, it’s very natural for fear to creep in. But it doesn’t have to.
Sit comfortably knowing that this loss is a universal journey. We will all go through grief. We will all one day journey to the other side. And we can all see how the people who've lost the most often have the greatest empathy for others who are going through hard times. They understand pain on a much deeper level. They can be sources of great strength and wisdom for the rest of us.
So, we do not have to fear the future because the greater the pain, the greater the growth that we can emerge from the pain with. Bruce Wayne lost his parents when he was a child and became Batman.
The amount of pain and loss we feel is always in direct proportion to the love and good memories we created with our loved ones. We only mourn those we loved. Every single memory, every single moment, every word spoken by the people we lost — they will rattle around in our brains forever. Those memories do not go anywhere.
The impact our departed loved ones had on us will live on forever in us, it will impact everyone we come into contact with, and everyone they come in contact with in a neverending ripple effect.
Just as nothing truly ends, so too our relationships do not end. There are many cultures who live with the memory of their ancestors — and their lost loved ones — in everyday life. Their relationships can become even closer because these figures go from being a part of our lives to living inside of us. It is still a relationship that can be cultivated and can be as rich as when the person was alive. It's up to us how much we choose to live with them.
CHAPTER 9 How to Deal With the Loss of Faith That Comes With Death
If we lose a loved one — especially a child or partner — and we experience that kind of loss that is the most unthinkable, unbearable, worst thing imaginable, the best thing to do is not be afraid of the grieving process, to not be afraid of the feelings, so that we are not adding more negativity to the most negative event in our lives. Embrace the grieving process until we can get to a place where the memory of our child brings tears of joy.
On a deeper level than our conscious thoughts, we can recognize that this extreme grief is part of our healing, that it is the release of the spiritual crisis within us, and we can get to a place where we love remembering these people and we love their memories. The journey of making peace with and accepting tragedy is how we can reach that place.
I know it seems impossible right now. I know it seems crazy to even say, but that's why I say trust this grieving process. Trust that human beings have this ability to heal even from that which seems the most impossible.
Humans used to live in times where infant mortality was 50%. Life expectancy was very low in the old days due to untreatable diseases and accidents, war and famine. But the story of humanity is the story of overcoming suffering, staying hopeful and moving forward. As difficult as it is to live with such a big hole in our lives, we do have this amazing healing system within us — physically, mentally and spiritually.
We have seen people time and time again turn their immense pain into hope, support and comfort for others. You don’t have to trust the process, but try to trust the process. Let go of the fear, let go of expectations, and just become the witness of your grief.
This will create a little space between you and it. Over time, that space becomes bigger, and we will have a little distance from that pain. That is enough to take us down the path of healing.
It's important to allow for the feelings that arise and create space for them. We shouldn't resist or push them away; instead, we should accept all the feelings and fully experience them. In this way, we don't get sucked into addiction or other distractions. We allow for what we think is unthinkable to be, and we allow for what is unbearable to be borne.
Over time, we will learn how to live with the pain and make peace with the suffering. The grief may be there forever, but we can still honor their lives by cherishing our own.
