## The science behind why nature feels so good.

### Key points

- Research continues to reveal the mechanisms of nature’s physiological and psychological benefits for humans.
- Time in nature has benefits for various psychoneuroimmunological processes.
- Contact with nature promotes sleep, exercise and microbiome health.

Of all the mood-boosting interventions at our disposal, there is one that may be most native to us: spending time in nature. A stroll through the autumn woods in crisp, fresh air, under a spotless blue sky, serenaded by wistful robins, with a carpet of red and yellow leaves under our feet does something indisputable to the body and soul.

We know, almost instinctively, that nature is good for us. What research is now revealing is just what exactly happens when we step into communion with it.

Three main theories explain nature’s physiological and psychological benefits.

[*Attention Restoration Theory*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0272494495900012) focuses on attention. Our capacity for directed attention is often under strain in our cognitively demanding modern lives. Yet, there is something about being in natural settings that restores this limited and easily depleted resource. In nature, we attend to the surrounding world in a gentler way ([researchers](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613\(25\)00311-0?dgcid=raven_jbs_etoc_email) call this “soft fascination”), thus replenishing our attentional capacities, which in turn benefit [concentration](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/attention "Psychology Today looks at concentration"), working [memory](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/memory "Psychology Today looks at memory"), and other cognitive functions that rely on them.

[*Stress Recovery Theory*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272494405801847) holds that being in nature can help the body recuperate from [stress](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/stress "Psychology Today looks at stress") by activating the parasympathetic [nervous system](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/neuroscience "Psychology Today looks at nervous system") —the branch of the nervous system that is associated with rest and recovery. [Studies](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613\(25\)00311-0?dgcid=raven_jbs_etoc_email), for example, show how the resulting benefits of stress reduction show up in our biology, including improvements in measurements of blood pressure, cortisol, and heart rate.

Finally, the [*Biophilia Hypothesis*](https://books.google.nl/books?hl=en&lr=&id=gNAuEAAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PP6&dq=E.O.Wilson+Biophilia+1986+Harvard+University+Press&ots=xw1rSwhDEQ&sig=N5iCHOvDvlg-pZHnhUVbyrINuAA&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q&f=false) points to the evolutionary origins of our affinity for nature. We seek out nature, and we enjoy being in its presence. This inherent bond with nature has helped us survive and thrive as a species.

Researchers have also been using neuroimaging modalities such as EEG and fMRI to track nature’s effects on brain function. A recent [analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763426000205) of such studies found distinct neurophysiological signatures of nature contact, often involving patterns indicating reduced stress, improved attentional control, and better [emotion regulation](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/emotion-regulation "Psychology Today looks at emotion regulation").

In fact, when it comes to our emotional lives, nature can play a particularly formidable role—reducing [negative affect](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12630), [anxiety](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/anxiety "Psychology Today looks at anxiety"), [loneliness](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/loneliness "Psychology Today looks at loneliness"), depressive symptoms and [burnout](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/burnout "Psychology Today looks at burnout"), and boosting [positive affect](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17439760.2018.1557242), a sense of meaning, [self-worth](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/self-esteem "Psychology Today looks at self-worth"), [awe](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/awe "Psychology Today looks at awe") and wonder.

How do these effects happen?

In a recent [review](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613\(25\)00311-0?dgcid=raven_jbs_etoc_email) published in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, Gregory Bratman and James Gross (2026) identify five pathways as possible mechanisms behind the positive effects of nature contact on affective functioning.

## 1\. Emotion regulation

[Emotion](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/emotions "Psychology Today looks at Emotion") regulation is the [multifaceted process](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613\(25\)00311-0?dgcid=raven_jbs_etoc_email) behind the *what*, *when*, and *how* of our efforts to shape our feelings. According to the [*process model of emotion regulation*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781), this includes five distinct processes: *situation selection* (choosing which situations to enter), *situation modification* (changing the situation to align with our emotional [goals](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/motivation "Psychology Today looks at goals")), *attentional deployment* (where and how we direct our attention), *cognitive change* (reframing the way we think about a situation), and *response modulation* (how we try to shape our emotional response).

Nature contact can [influence](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12630) each of these aspects. For example, as the [authors](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613\(25\)00311-0?dgcid=raven_jbs_etoc_email) point out, people often seek out natural spaces such as parks and watersides, in part because they expect to feel positive emotions there (situation selection). Being in nature is also associated with less [rumination](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.643866/full) (attentional deployment).

## 2\. Psychoneuroimmunology

The mutual influence of mind and body processes is well established. Psychoneuroimmunology frameworks like the [Social Safety Theory](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-032816-045159) describe how our emotions—whether positive or negative—affect immune function and inflammation in the body, while inflammatory cytokines may in turn impact our emotions.

When the body is in a state of stress and senses or anticipates threat, the immunological response ramps up. While heightened activation can be adaptive in the short run, it becomes harmful when chronically switched on. Here, too, nature contact may offer [help](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01093/full). For example, inhaling certain volatile organic compounds that are emitted from trees may have [anti-inflammatory](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ehpm/27/0/27_22-00160/_article/-char/ja/) effects on the body.

Some [studies](https://www.mdpi.com/1660-4601/18/4/1416) have also found that nature exposure can have positive immunological effects through reductions in interleukin-6 (a pro-inflammatory molecule that, when chronically elevated, is implicated in autoimmune and inflammatory disorders), and increases in natural killer cells (white blood cells that destroy virus-infected and malignant cells).

## 3\. Human microbiome

The microbes in our gut (“ [the hidden kingdom](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/abstract/S1364-6613\(18\)30097-4) ”) influence many aspects of our biological health, including [hormonal](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/hormones "Psychology Today looks at hormonal"), immune, and neural processes. Thanks to the workings of the [gut-brain axis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-023-02287-6), they also affect how we feel.

Being in nature, having skin contact with soil or other natural elements, or inhaling airborne microbes can impact the [diversity](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160412024002484) and composition of the [microbiome](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/microbiome "Psychology Today looks at microbiome"). For example, a two-year biodiversity intervention [study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160412021004360) from Finland found that children whose playgrounds were enriched with natural elements gained more beneficial microbes on their skin and lost potentially harmful bacteria compared to controls.

## 4\. Sleep

Most of us have personal evidence of how sleep affects our emotions. How often, for example, has poor sleep played havoc with some or other aspect of our affective functioning—from the kinds of emotions we experience during the day, to our success at regulating them?

Nature has been called “ [an environmental determinant](https://www.cell.com/trends/cognitive-sciences/fulltext/S1364-6613\(25\)00311-0?dgcid=raven_jbs_etoc_email) ” of sleep health. Living near green spaces is associated with better [quality and duration](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013935125009491) of sleep, while nature visits have been associated with fewer nights of [insufficient](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_lookup?doi=10.1016%2Fj.envres.2024.118522&pmid=38403148) sleep.

## 5\. Physical activity

Physical activity benefits many aspects of our subjective well-being, including our [affective health](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-021-00489-9). It does so by increasing positive emotions, decreasing negative emotions, and helping us use more adaptive [emotion regulation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00221-024-06791-1) strategies. As Bratman and Gross (2026) write, natural environments are [predictors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S001393512302409X) of physical activity for a variety of reasons—from removing [obstacles](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0033062021000013) to movement to making exercise more enjoyable.

The paths that nature takes to exert its beneficial effects on humans are many and mysterious. Much remains to be uncovered by science. This makes the stroll through the autumn woods all the more magical.
