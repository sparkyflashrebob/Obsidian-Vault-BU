2001: A Space Odyssey

After finding a strange artifact buried on the moon, a team of scientists and astronauts try to trace its origins.

This classic Stanley Kubrick film, which was written simultaneously with

Arthur C. Clarke

Many of the details match up with reality, from the position of satellites, to the centrifugal propulsion, to the trying duration and eerie quiet of space travel.

Released more than 50 years ago,

raised the bar for hard sci-fi movies to come.

The Martian

Photo Credit: 20th Century Studios

The Martian

is based on a novel of the same name by Andy Weir, and follows Mark Watney, an astronaut left behind on Mars. While he waits for a rescue mission, he is forced to innovate in order to survive.

NASA provided their expertise to help the production replicate accurate equipment for the spacecraft and the astronauts. They also offered images and information about Mars to help the movie capture Martian scenery and conditions in a realistic manner.

Additionally, many major plot points hold up to scrutiny, like the possibility of restoring prior NASA equipment to communicate back to Earth; growing sustenance on Mars to survive; and traveling between planets on a realistic time frame.

Minority Report (2002)

Photo Credit: 20th Century Fox and Dreamworks Pictures

Set in 2054, this futuristic chase thriller centers on a team of special officers who are part of the PreCrime division, tasked with detecting future crimes and preventing them before they occur.

While the movie sparks a debate about determinism and free will, and raises questions about the government's involvement in private lives, its depiction of future technologies was strikingly accurate.

In the process of making the movie, director Stephen Spielberg collaborated with prominent experts to craft a realistic portrayal of the future. Many of the devices Spielberg incorporated into the film now exist.

Minority Report

included technologies with gesture recognition, retinal scanners, self-driving cars, virtual reality systems, personalized ads, and motion tracking computers – all of which are familiar to us today.

linguist Dr. Louise Banks is called upon to communicate with alien lifeforms that have appeared in crafts in twelve different countries. Their sudden arrival alarms governments across the globe, and as the situation escalates, Dr. Banks races to discover the purpose of their presence on Earth.

To communicate with the extraterrestrials, Dr. Banks acts out different words, which the aliens respond to in their corresponding symbol. Building up a vocabulary of basic terms, Banks pushes for more information. Languages are often translated in this manner, by creating a shared understanding of essential terms and the way each respective language represents them.

As Dr. Banks' knowledge of the alien language evolves, her perception of time, and capacity for temporal thinking, shifts to match the aliens'. This depicts

a scientific concept known as linguistic relativity, or the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis
