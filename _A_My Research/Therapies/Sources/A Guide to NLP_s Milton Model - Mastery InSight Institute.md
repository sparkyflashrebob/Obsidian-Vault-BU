### A Guide to NLP's Milton Model

#### The NLP Milton Model, What it is, and Why it Works

##### An Incomplete Guide on How to be artfully vague… on purpose *(and for good reasons)*

*Originally published on Altfeld.com; updated and expanded for 2026.*
Jump to: [ [Presuppositions](https://nlpmasteryinsight.com/blog/nlp-milton-model-language-patterns/#presups)], [ [Embedded Commands](https://nlpmasteryinsight.com/blog/nlp-milton-model-language-patterns/#commands)], [ [100-sentence Drills](https://nlpmasteryinsight.com/blog/nlp-milton-model-language-patterns/#sentences)]
**The Milton Model** is a set of linguistic patterns used in Neuro-Linguistic Programming to communicate with the unconscious mind using **'artful vagueness.'** It was modeled from the work of **Dr. Milton H. Erickson.**
The Milton Model Language Patterns were modeled by John Grinder and Richard Bandler, from the now-famous and innovative Hypnotherapist Milton H. Erickson, M.D., who passed away in 1980.
These patterns enable you to communicate in a way that's strategically and artfully vague, hypnotically memorable, and *(when you do it right)…* surprisingly elegant!
Before we go any further: this material is about trance, and it can be used in ways that are powerful. If you plan to do anything involving hypnosis in an “official capacity,” know your local/state laws, and also—more importantly — know your ethics. The goal here is not to create zombies. The goal is to help people change more easily, communicate with more influence, and do it with responsibility.

##### Start here: outcome first! *(otherwise, you're just doing word-salad, or word-magic)*

If you're going to use hypnotic techniques like Milton Model language patterns, have a clear outcome in mind first.
Not a vague, “I want to influence people” outcome.
A real one:
\* **FIRST:** What outcome(s) do *they* want?
\* **THEN:** What outcome(s) do I want, for them?
\* What's the win-win that combines these?
\* **Ecology:** What are the consequences if I succeed?
\* **Optimizing:** How do I make this cleaner, kinder, and more useful?
This is where the Milton Model gets dangerous in the wrong hands, and **beautiful** in the right ones.
The patterns don't make you ethical. **You do.**

##### The NLP Milton Model, in a practical map

Some trainers chunk the Milton Model down into four categories:
\* The inverse of the Meta Model
\* Presuppositions
\* Indirect elicitation patterns
\* Patterns in metaphor

##### Let's turn that into something you can actually use.

###### 1) The inverse of the Meta Model

The “Meta Model” in NLP helps us “recover lost information” from communication. This means, to clarify deletions, identify (and sometimes challenge) distortions, as well as notice and sometimes break) generalizations.
So knowing that about the Meta Model… the Milton Model can also be thought of as:
“Strategically *introducing* **intentionally-chosen** deletions, distortions, and generalizations… and then letting the listener's mind complete the meaning.”
If you want the full precision-side breakdown first, [study the Meta-Model directly](https://nlpmasteryinsight.com/blog/nlp-milton-model-language-patterns/nlpmasteryinsight.com/blog/nlp-meta-model-language-patterns/) before returning here. And if you'd like to learn even more about the Meta-Model, we do have a **Meta-Model Exercise Workbook** available.
**That completion process is the hypnotic gold.**
Because the listener isn't just hearing your words.
They're searching their own experience to make your words true.
That's trance-adjacent even when nobody calls it trance.
**Example (clean and ethical):**
“You may find yourself noticing the part of you that already knows what needs to shift… even if you can't put it into words yet.”
No specifics. No force. But **their brain starts looking in useful directions.**
**Didn't yours?**

###### 2) Presuppositions

**Presuppositions are the sneaky ninjas of language,** because they're usually found inside of that which you didn't *actually* hear.
**They smuggle assumptions** into the listener's deep structure, like:
**“When you start feeling better…”** *(presupposes: you will feel better)*
**“Have you noticed how quickly you learn this?”** *(presupposes: you are learning this)*
**“Which do you prefer, option A or option B?”** *(presupposes: you'll choose one)*
Here's a fun way to think of it:
**Presuppositions are a bit like beliefs**
**that hitchhike inside grammar!**
Here are a few easy **“high-yield” times to spot** (and practice) presuppositions:
**Time clauses:** Before / after / when / as soon as…
**Example:** “ **After** you've thought about it for a moment, you'll know what to do.”
**Ordinal numbers:** first / second / next…
**Example:** “ **First** , I'll invite you to relax. ”
**Either/Or:** *(hints at false dichotomies)*
**Example:** “Do you want to understand it now, **or** let it sink in and understand it even more later?”
**Awareness predicates:** realize / notice / discover…
**Example:** “You may have already **started noticing** what's different about your thinking already.”
**Adverbs/adjectives:** easily / naturally / comfortably…
Example: “You can naturally begin to feel more at ease.”
**Practice drill (fast and effective)**
**Take a normal sentence** like: *“I want you to learn this.”*
Now rebuild it with presuppositions:
“ **As** you **continue** learning this, you might **be surprised** **how quickly** it becomes second nature.”
Same intention… with a completely different impact.

###### 3) Indirect Elicitation Patterns

These are Milton Model language patterns where you get responses without demanding responses. You're guiding attention, choice, and internal experience.
**(A) Embedded commands**
This is when a command hides inside a longer sentence.
Example:
“You might not even realize how good you can feel when you allow yourself to relax now.”
Notice: you're not barking “Relax now.”
You're embedding it like a suggestion the mind can adopt.
**(B) Analog/Analogue marking**
You “mark” the embedded part with voice tone, gesture, spacing, or emphasis. In writing, people overdo CAPS and it turns into hypnosis cosplay.
In live conversation, it's usually subtle: a slight pause, a nod, a tonal dip.
Tip: If you have to perform it like a stage magician, it's probably not elegant yet.
**(C) Embedded questions**
These are questions hidden inside statements. They “invite” the mind to answer without feeling interrogated.
Example:
“I'm curious what you'll notice first as you begin to understand this more deeply.”
The mind starts answering: What will I notice first?
That internal search is the point.
**(D) Negative commands**
“Don't think about X” forces the brain to represent X.
This is why “Don't be nervous” is the world's worst advice.
Cleaner example:
“You don't have to try to relax… you can just let the chair do some of that work for you.”
**(E) Conversational postulates**
These look like yes/no questions, but they're really requests for action or experience.
**Examples:**
\* “Can you close the door?” (translation: close the door)
\* “Do you know what time it is?” (translation: check the time)
They're polite, indirect… and often more effective.
**MORE indirect patterns: ambiguity**
These are advanced tools best learned by writing lots of examples:
\* Phonological ambiguity *(sound-alike meanings)*
\* Syntactic ambiguity *(sentence structure confusion)*
\* Scope ambiguity *(“all” applies to what, exactly?)*
\* Punctuation ambiguity *(in writing)*
Straight talk: don't use ambiguity to be “clever.” Use it to open meaning, soften resistance, or invite inner search.

###### 4) Patterns in metaphor

This is where trance gets whimsical — and strangely practical.
Two big ones we played with:
**(A) Selectional restriction violations**
That's a fancy term for giving human qualities to non-human things. The effect on human beings can be even stronger than if we directed those qualities towards people! Its like it bypasses conscious resistance directly.
So, naturally, I **love** these. And your desk chair will enjoy supporting you in this exciting journey of discovery, every step of the way!
\* “Your schedule has been arguing with you lately.”
\* “That problem has been waiting for the right moment to disappear.”
\* “I see your confidence is remembering how to stand up straight.”
Why it works: it creates a gentle dissociation.
The conscious mind goes, **“That's not literal…”**
…and the unconscious goes, **“Yeah, but I get it.”**
**(B) Quotes**
Quotes are… WONDERFUL. They let you put ideas in a frame that bypasses argument. It can be a real quote, an imagined quote, or a “someone once said…”
**Ethical use looks like this:**
“Some people discover that the moment they stop trying to force change, change starts cooperating.”
**Notice:** not coercive, not absolute. Just a frame. Using quotes does not necessarily make it coercive (though coercion can occur through quotes).
The big “secret”: complexity can overload the conscious mind
One of the funniest things is that if you stack enough nested clauses, the conscious mind can get overwhelmed — and the person starts absorbing the gist rather than parsing every word.
That can be useful… but it can also sound like you're doing an impression of a late-night infomercial hypnotist.
Speaking of which: **These work best on intelligent minds when** the quotes are genuine. They can be verified.
**These patterns work even when claims aren't verifiable.** Which is why slogans, propaganda, and **'someone said…'** messaging can move people fast. That's exactly why the ethical bar should be higher, not lower.

##### So here's a guideline I actually stand by:

Be clear in your intention.
Be elegant in your language.
Be respectful of the listener.
And if you're going complex, do it because it serves the outcome — not because you want to feel powerful.

##### **A practical way to use Milton Model language patterns, without being weird**

*“Okay Jonathan… how do I use this in real life?”*
Here are three non-creepy, high-value applications:

###### 1) Coaching / change work

```
*  Presuppose capa [](https://nlpmasteryinsight.com/free-gift/) bility and progress
*  Ask embedded questions that invite insight
*  Use metaphor to bypass self-criticism
```

###### 2) Leadership / Communication

```
*  Use conversational postulates to request action cleanly
*  Use presuppositions to reinforce direction
*  Use artful vagueness to let people self-organize meaning
*   **Inspire people with your vision, and invoke a higher calling and heroic journeys.**
```

###### 3) Conflict & Repairing Rapport with Others

```
*  Soften your direct questions into embedded questions.
*  Pace their experience without agreeing with their conclusions
*  Lead toward options (“what would help?” instead of “why are you like this?”)
```

#### The Artistry of the Milton Model: Weaving the Linguistic Tapestry

Knowing the individual patterns of the Milton Model is like knowing the notes on a scale; the **true artistry begins when you stop playing notes and start composing music.**
When you get so good that you can **unconsciously weave these patterns together** — perhaps stacking a presupposition inside of a metaphor, possibly even marked by a subtle embedded command — you start creating a beautiful tapestry of experience for your listener.
Then, of course, this is no longer about “using a technique” on someone; it's about creating a linguistic environment where **new perspectives feel like the listener's own discovery** .
Lets explore an example!
**Sample Context:** Shifting a “Stuck” Mindset. Imagine a coaching client feeling trapped in a career rut. Instead of arguing with their logic, you might weave a tapestry like this:
“As you sit there, **noticing** the weight of those old thoughts *(Awareness Predicate)* , I'm curious if you can **remember a time** *(Embedded Command)* when **a closed door actually led you to a much more interesting hallway** *(Metaphor)* . Because sometimes, **the mind knows** how to find the exit *(Selectional Restriction Violation)* before the eyes even see the handle.”

#### **How to get Really, Really Good at the NLP Milton Model**

**Training drill: the “100 sentences” method (the fastest way to get good)**
If you want this in your bones (not just your brain), do this:
Pick one category per day and write 25–100 examples, fast:
\* 100 presupposition sentences
\* 100 embedded commands
\* 100 embedded questions
\* 100 conversational postulates
\* 100 selectional restriction violations
Then read them out loud, and notice:
*Which ones feel smooth? Which ones feel like you're trying too hard?*
That feedback loop is how you can begin to go from “NLP technique” to “natural language skill.” **Its a limited feedback loop** , **if you're not getting constant feedback from others training alongside you, or from a skilled trainer,** but its certainly a starting point.

#### Final thoughts on the NLP Milton Model for now…

The NLP Milton Model isn't about being vague to be vague.
It's about guiding attention, inviting inner search, and creating language that lands—not just logically, but experientially.
Used responsibly, it helps people change faster, because they're no longer fighting your words.
They're using your words… to access their own resources.
And that's the whole game.
