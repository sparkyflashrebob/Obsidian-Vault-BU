Based on the sources and our conversation, the concept of "Preparing the Mind to Receive Dhamma" is presented as a fundamental purpose and result of cultivating Mindfulness of Breathing (Anapanasati). Anapanasati serves as the primary method for this preparation.

Here's a discussion of what the sources say about this preparation:

1. **Anapanasati as the Essential Foundation and Training:** Mindfulness of Breathing is repeatedly described as the **backbone** and a **very stable and concentrated foundation** of practice [1]. It is presented as essential and important [1]. From the second stage onwards, the Buddha uses the verb ***sikkhati***, meaning **"to train oneself"** [2]. This highlights that Anapanasati is not just observation but an active process of **training, studying, and making known within oneself through one's experience** the results of the practice [2, 3]. This training is explicitly linked to the purpose of preparing the mind.
2. **The Purpose of the Whole Practice:** The overarching purpose of the entire practice, including Anapanasati, is to **create balance and clarity of mind** [4]. This state of balance and clarity is crucial because it **allows the mind to be able to see and understand things clearly, see and understand things as they truly are** [2, 4]. This clear seeing is what ultimately liberates the mind [2]. The sources state that if the mind is not clear and balanced, it **never has that opportunity to see in that way** [4]. Therefore, preparing the mind means bringing it to this state of clarity and balance.
3. **Making the Mind "Ready for Dhamma":** The sources specifically state that calming the body (part of the *kaya sankhara* stage in Anapanasati) and calming the mind make the mind **ready for work** and **ready for the Dhamma** [5, 6]. This readiness involves cultivating specific qualities:

   * The mind needs to be prepared to **receive the Dharma graciously, gratefully, appreciatively, delightfully** [6].
   * It needs **awareness and a kind of brightness** [6].
   * It requires **a kind of softness or gentleness** [6].
   * It needs **clarity** [6].
   * It needs **balance** [6].
4. **How Anapanasati Prepares the Mind:** The sequential training described in the Anapana Sutta lays the groundwork for these qualities:

   * **Knowing the Breath Clearly (*Paññatti*)**: The initial stage establishes basic attention on the breath sensation [7].
   * **Experiencing and Calming Bodily Formations (*Kaya Sankhara*)**: This involves entering into the full bodily experience of the breath, noticing sensations, and calming them [2, 5, 8]. This physical settling **calms the body** and is the **primary condition for alleviating restlessness**, which is a hindrance to clear seeing [9]. Calming the body and mind makes them **ready to receive the Dharma** [5, 6].
   * **Experiencing and Calming Feelings and Mind Formations**: This stage involves cultivating mindfulness of feelings (*vedana*) and mind states (*citta*), understanding their arising and passing [10-12]. Learning to relate skillfully to feelings and perceptions, even unpleasant ones, helps prepare the mind to use them as opportunities for growth and cultivating wholesome qualities [13, 14]. It involves discerning the mind itself from its passing moods [15, 16], which contributes to clarity.
   * **Gladdening the Mind (*Pamoja*)**: This is a necessary part of mental development, involving bringing **delight and brightness** to the mind through the practice itself [17, 18]. This is not seeking external pleasure, but finding contentment in stillness and relinquishment [17]. This gladdening is essential for further reflections and discriminations needed for insight [18], creating a "place of stillness and brightness" [18]. It's part of a natural sequence leading to joy, tranquility, happiness, and concentration [19].
   * **Concentrating the Mind (*Samadhi*)**: Establishing firm concentration settles the mind [20, 21], pulls it together, focuses, and **purifies it of coarser elements** that agitate and stain it [20]. This purification is likened to cleaning windows to see through them [20]. Concentration makes the mind pliable and malleable [20], leading to a **pleasant abiding here and now** [21] and developing the **capacity for knowledge and vision** [22] and **sharp mindfulness and alertness** to feelings, perceptions, and thoughts [23]. This settled, purified, and concentrated state is necessary for seeing things clearly and understanding their implications [4, 23, 24].
   * **Contemplating Insight (*Dhammanupassana*)**: The final stage builds directly on the cultivated state, contemplating impermanence [25], dispassion [26], cessation [27], and relinquishment [28]. These insights into the true nature of reality are the direct "reception" of Dhamma's core truths, made possible by the prepared mind [25, 29].
5. **Overcoming Hindrances:** The training inherent in Anapanasati is crucial for overcoming the Five Hindrances (sensual desire, ill will, sloth/torpor, restlessness/worry, skeptical doubt) [30-32]. These hindrances obstruct clarity and peace [31], making the mind impure and unable to see clearly [24]. Learning to recognize and relinquish them [30, 33] is vital for preparing the mind to be like **clear, still water** where things can be seen as they are [24]. Using mindfulness of breathing to calm and settle is the primary condition for alleviating restlessness [9], and developing awareness and attention helps dispel sloth and torpor [31, 34]. Guarding the mind from unskillful states is a key function of mindfulness [35, 36].
6. **Beyond Mechanics:** The sources caution against simply going through the mechanics of the practice [37]. The preparation involves **learning how to attend, how to train, how to experience things fully, and how to know things clearly** [3]. It's about cultivating the right qualities and intending to make the mind ready to **experience the Dhamma, experience truth, and realize the fruits of the Buddha's teaching leading to liberation** [6, 37]. This readiness is the crucial point [6].

In essence, Mindfulness of Breathing, through its progressive stages of training (*sikkhati*) – from establishing clear attention on the breath, to experiencing and calming the body/breath, working with feelings and mind states, cultivating positive states like gladness and concentration, overcoming hindrances, and finally contemplating fundamental truths – systematically prepares the mind by developing the necessary qualities of balance, clarity, stability, and receptivity required to truly receive the profound insights of the Dhamma and realize liberation [2-6, 37].
