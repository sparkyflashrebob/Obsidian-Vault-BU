INDIGENOUS ACKNOWLEDGEMENT

We honor and recognize the native and indigenous people on the many lands on which we sit in

ceremony together. They are the original stewards of the land, water, plants, and animals. We

acknowledge the painful history and the many crimes against the original inhabitants in this, and other

regions of this continent perpetrated in the interest of western european supremacy and expansion.

It is a profound privilege that we can participate in these rites of passage and rejoin the ceremonies and

rituals that have been passed down through generations by the peoples of many lands. For many, these

ceremonies are more than a personal journey - they are a way of reclaiming an ancestral connection to a

land, spirit, and culture that had been violently threatened. There are countless individuals whose

lineages were fractured by colonization, and whose ancestral practices were disrupted, forgotten, or

forcibly erased. The path to healing and rediscovery is not so easy to tread. The privilege we hold to be a

part of sacred rituals is a testament to the resistance of those who preserved these traditions against all

odds. It is a reminder of the privilege we have to reconnect and the responsibility we have to honor the

ancestors and original stewards of all the lands on this earth, who still guide us from afar.

CULTURAL RELATIONS

As an organization, the School of Lost Borders is committed to introducing participants and trainees to

the important history of First Nation peoples in the many places where we hold our programs. We

acknowledge much of our work, including our headquarters, are in Payahuunadü, the ancestral,

historical and contemporary homelands of the Nüümü and Newe peoples.

We make it a practice to look for ways to say thank you, not only to the land, but to the community.

Some guides contribute time, energy, and funds to indigenous organizations as well as other efforts

focused on social justice, healing, and the restoring of right relations.

We strongly encourage people to learn about their own ancestry and roots, knowing that ceremony

comes through relationship and deep connection with people and place. At the same time, we

acknowledge that it is a privilege to know, and have access to records of one's own ancestry.

With that, our work is focused on offering all people an opportunity to experience belonging on this

earth. To feel a part of, and not separate from nature. As this relationship is remembered, marked and

celebrated, we hope that all cultural identities and traditions can co-exist a bit more peacefully.

We are committed to actionable change, social justice, and regenerative relationships

with all beings and the land.

We honor the Indigenous peoples of all of the lands where we work.

We are committed to and engaged in active listening and learning and our work is

ongoing. We welcome input and feedback and will continue to share as we go.

SOLIDARITY AND ACCESSIBILITY

We at the School of Lost Borders are aware that our work exists within a context of challenging and often

painful issues related to justice, equality, diversity, and inclusion. These issues touch on race, ethnicity,

gender, class, and other categories. They also touch on our relationships with the lands where our

programs take place and the complex cultural relations with the traditional peoples of those lands.

We are in deep exploration about how to understand and contribute to healing from these issues. We

remain committed to welcoming all those who are called to our programs and to doing our part to

remove any barriers to responding to this call.

While the programs at the School of Lost Borders are inclusive to all people, we recognize the need to

have programs anchored within particular communities, such as Queer specific programs offered for the

LGBTQ2IA+ community, BIPOC, veterans, women, men, youth, and elders. In these spaces, there is often

a strong container of shared experience, which provides added safety and can be very healing.

We know that similar identity-specific programs are needed for BIPOC and other identity groups not

currently served in this way by the School. We are working towards wholeness, creating more access and

more representation within our own guide collective, and more inviting spaces for systematically

marginalized communities in our organization and work.

INTRODUCTION

Deep in your heart you know whether or not you want to experience a rite of passage. Already you feel

the ancient stirring within you. The time has come. You must go onto the land, onto the Earth, and seek

vision, understanding and strength for yourself and for your people. Truly, the survival of the social order

depends on your providing visionary leadership, on you as an active force working for the welfare and

growth of the people.

The individual human spirit must be renewed and revitalized by the quest for vision. Deep in your

unconscious, where you hear the voices of your ancestors, you know that the time has come to leave

everything behind and go alone to the sacred mountain. You are ready to accept the responsibility that

accompanies this choice.

The definition of vision is the faculty or state of being able to see, or the ability to think about or plan the

future with imagination or wisdom. It is not necessarily a grand, magnificent, or showy awakening.

SOLB Vision Fast Handbook  Page 8

It most likely will not come to you as a sudden burst of light.

Look carefully at your expectations. The Greek god Prometheus stole fire from the sacred altar on

Olympus and was condemned to eternal torment. What you take without earning it will probably die in

your heart. Do you hope to take a recreational dance through a phantasmagoria of altered states? If so,

you will be unable to find a comfortable place to sit. Are you looking for a vivid hallucination or two? If

so, you will forget what you saw within a few months. Are you expecting a "quickie" self-transformation,

hoping you won't have to work too hard? If so, you will return from the sacred mountain feeling cheated,

puzzled, and no more prepared to serve your people than you were before.

Many there are who quest for vision. Few there are who return to actually demonstrate the reality of

their vision for the people of their world. To every initiate, the charge is the same: to shoulder the vision

of "doing" and to live your myth.

THE CRISES AND TRANSITIONS OF THE INDIVIDUAL IN MODERN TIMES

No human escapes being human. We must all face the ecstasy and the agony of human experience. Life

is filled with transitions; growth is characterized by change. As Octavia Butler says, “All that you touch,

you change. All that you change, changes you. The only lasting truth is change.”

Everyone’s journey through life, including experiences of growth and maturity, unfold at different times

and in unique phases. But it can be useful to use the “traditional” phases of life as a lens through which

to understand our unique journeys.

Humans must pass from one life stage to another. We are born from the womb into infancy. We are

weaned from infancy and brought into the world of childhood. We pass from childhood into

adolescence, from adolescence into adulthood. We make our way through the adult passages, facing

predictable crises at middle age and "retirement." With aging comes preparation for dying, and finally,

the ultimate passage of death.

Since ancient times, individuals have stood at the threshold of these life changes. In traditional cultures,

these transitions were and are celebrated by rites of passage and ceremonies of initiation. Without these

ceremonies, individuals could not have understood or interpreted their life experiences, nor could they

have been capable of assuming the social responsibilities and privileges required by these changes.

SOLB Vision Fast Handbook  Page 9

To acknowledge just a few of the Indigenous peoples whose wisdom can be drawn upon: the Diné

(Navajo), Lakota Sioux, Chippewa (Ojibwe), Maasai, Māori, Sateré-Mawé, Inuit, Apache, Celtic…the list

Nowadays, traditional life passage ceremonies are not a part of the lives of most people. The thickening

of the wall between humans and their natural environment has contributed to the loss of traditional

ceremonies of life passage. The careful, ritual footprints left by our ancestors have been engulfed by the

traffic of modern civilization.

Our modern human experience includes a multitude of "crises,” "accidents," "upheavals," and other

traumatic life changes that ask us not only to survive them, but to understand their meaning and to grow

thereby. How many times in our span of years are we called upon to uproot and transplant, to let go of

the old and embrace the new, to end it and go on, to plow under and sow seed, to cease being ignorant

and find out, to die and be reborn? How many times will we move from one place to another, change

our work or vocation, lose or be separated from a lover or friend, mourn the death of a parent, conceive,

give birth to, miscarry, or abort a child, be devastated by a natural disaster, drop into suicidal depression,

survive a near fatal accident, lose our limbs or sensory functions, do violence to or have violence done to

us, become parents, grandparents or step-parents, enter the armed forces, embark on a long journey,

become addicted to a drug, or burn out on the job?

There is no way to ascertain to what extent the loss of meaningful rites of passage has crippled the

growth of modern individuals through the passages and crises of life in modern culture. Those who have

a strong value or myth system, i.e., a way of finding meaning in their experience, most easily survive

these passages. There are many individuals, however, who do not have, or cannot refer to, an adequate

value or myth system. People often remain in crisis over extended periods of time, unable to make the

passage from the old world (the past) to the privileges and responsibilities of the new (the future). Bereft

of the means of understanding what has happened, or is happening, caught up in a crisis one cannot

leave behind. It consumes them, stuck in the passage and swallowed by it. Bruised, battered and

bewildered emotions rage unrestrained. Because the traditional ways of passing through crises are not

available, individuals must either find their own way, sometimes with the help of others, or succumb to

the perils of the passage.

THE COLLECTIVE CRISIS

This time on planet Earth is marked by the specter of what many call “the metacrisis” or “the polycrisis.”

Everywhere we look, whether that gaze is regional or global, environmental or societal, the challenges

are many and existential. The call for solidarity is profound. Time in wild nature and in community is

indispensable. Much of what is spoken of above can be applied to the struggles of navigating the

broader crises and transitions at work in our communities today.

WHY DO YOU WANT TO PARTICIPATE IN A WILDERNESS RITE OF

It is very important that you are certain why you want to participate. If you are not clear about what

you are looking for, you will not find it.

What passage, crisis, or transition do you find yourself in? What life event do you seek to celebrate,

mark, or leave behind? Have you come to a door marked "fresh beginning" or a door marked

"conclusion?" If you cannot give yourself a clear reason for going to the wilderness alone you should

question again. If your intention is to heal and you do not look more deeply into the source of your

suffering, the trip may be for naught.

SOLB Vision Fast Handbook  Page 11

Your reasons for undertaking this ceremony do not have to be significant to anyone else but yourself. In

fact, others may think you have lost your way. This reaction from others has been felt by the healers,

original wisdom keepers, visionaries and prophets of old and new.

In their wisdom, the sacred ancestors of many peoples designed rites of passage in such a way as to lead

you to discover within yourself the healing vision you seek. The very feelings that tug at you, pulling you

toward ceremony, evidence the ancient way of knowing that exists in you. The ceremony evokes this

knowing, infuses your life with meaning, and prompts you to find the answers that you seek.

If you have turned toward this urge to "get away from it all," to sever from a former world and find a

place of vision and self-empowerment, then you have taken the first step toward participation in this

ancient and sacred ceremony.

It is essential that you take this step with the confident recognition that you are going away only so

that you can return and assume the responsibilities of your new place in the world.

Over the years, thousands have gone out to the desert, the mountains, the plains in a similar way, alone,

fasting, praying, seeking connection to self, others and the earth. Many were in the midst of life crises or

were passing from one life stage to another. Many went to clarify and strengthen who they were in their

lives, their families, their work and their communities.

There is no right reason to embark upon this path.

The reasons why these and other individuals left everything behind and went to the sacred mountain

indicate the continued relevance and adaptability of this traditional rite to the modern world.

THE CEREMONY

On the mythical plane, this ceremony is an initiatory journey, a symbolic ordeal, that occurs in a

sacramental region of natural power wherein the initiate encounters "demons" or "spirits" -reflections of

his/her/their life or karma as mirrored by the natural cosmos.

On the physical plane the participant lives in a sensitized state of bodily awareness and feels the

connection they share with the elements and living things of the natural world. Bodily states of

emptiness and weakness often give rise to emotions and fears concerning personal survival on many

levels. The body /psyche responds to the challenge and furnishes the individual with unexpected sources

of strength and vitality.

Put another way, this is the quintessential transpersonal experience; by spending time alone in the

wilderness our sense of identity or self extends beyond the individual or personal and encompasses

wider aspects of humankind, life, psyche, or cosmos.

This new inner knowledge helps to heal the acculturated split between what we hold sacred (that which

is set-apart, awe inspiring, and/or worthy of devotion) and profane (mundane everyday concerns), and is

the basis for a new recognition of our physical/spiritual place on this land.

Perception of our true place in nature, however, is not possible without taking the first, hesitant, steps

across the margin of our experience. This ceremony provides an ancient, time-tested way of taking those

first steps:

THE PROCESS

This rite of passage is an experience of symbolically passing from one life stage to the next. The

individual moves from the state of "letting go" to the state of "beginning." This passage between the two

worlds is often referred to as a womb or a grave. The rites and ceremonies attendant on this passage

were many and varied, depending on culture. Invariably, they provided for the furtherance and

preservation of the social order among the people or nation.

Despite the variety of rites evolved over thousands of years by diverse cultures, the inherent framework

is common to all rite-of-passage ceremonies, including this one. You will be led through a three-stage

SEVERANCE. Over a period of time, you will prepare to break from the former world, to leave everything

behind. At the appropriate time, you will separate yourself from previous concerns, be removed from

your regular routine and head out onto the land.

THRESHOLD, or the "sacred world." You will enter the threshold (or passage) when you step across the

limitations of your former life and begin the time of testing. Here in the threshold you may be faced with

any number of challenges, or you may feel deeply nourished. You may experience both. No matter your

experience, you will make your "last stand," dying to the old and embracing the new.

INCORPORATION (literally, “taking on the body"). Returning from your journey, you will assume the task

of bringing your self, your vision, your understanding, into the gross body of the world - for the benefit of

your people.

As you stand rooted in the soil, and reach out, like a plant into the light of the sun, you will also seek to

know your place among all the peoples of the earth. You will greet all who have journeyed before you,

regardless of their beliefs. Every thought, emotion, prayer, dream, or song will resonate with the power

of your ancestors. Through you, and the ceremony you perform, your grandparents from the beginning

of time will reach ahead to the fulfillment of our collective vision -- and the ever-evolving

interdependency of all the peoples and beings of the earth.

Although this same three-stage process underlies all such passage ceremonies, this ceremony is as

expansive and diverse as the people who have come to participate. It would be wrong to assume that

there is only one "correct" ceremony. It will be up to you to navigate your needs as they pertain to

severance, threshold, and incorporation.

"Nothing can be sole or whole/That has not been rent," wrote W.B. Yeats. Former limitations must be

torn down in order to establish new boundaries. Death must precede new birth. If we want to make a

new start, we must say farewell to the old way to have a clear road into the new. Now that you have

made the decision to experience an initiation ceremony, you must prepare to leave everything behind,

resolve loose ends, give up old habits, and make it good with your relationships. This severance

preparation is a clear opportunity to "practice your own dying." Indeed, some bright day you will leave it

all behind, and cross the threshold into the sacred passage. This knowing in the gut, that someday you

will die, will guide your preparations.

For some, this is not just a personal healing but a return to the wisdom of the ancestors, a re-amination

of ways that have been lost or stolen, a reclaiming of ways that existed in balance with nature before

civilization and foreign ideologies were enforced upon us.

All ceremonies of passage involve some sort of hardship, risk, or trial. You must prepare yourself,

physically, emotionally, mentally, and spiritually, to survive a period of three or four days and nights of

solitude in the "wilderness," with no convenience other than the contents of your backpack. Even if you

are an avid explorer or backpacker you will probably feel the same apprehension the seasoned sailor

feels before a long voyage. After all, Nature is unpredictable -- and her power is beyond imagining. You

will go to her and place yourself in her hands. By preparing to surrender to and endure whatever she has

to teach you, you are probably confronting some basic fears. Remember that fear itself can be a teacher

of clarity of purpose and wisdom of heart. The pumping of adrenalin becomes a feeling. Feeling becomes

thought; thought becomes enlightened action. The body knows fear, just as it knows the wind, the

darkness, and the silence. This is where transformation occurs. The sacred teachings and inner

knowledge of your ancestors will come out to meet you on your path and you will be shaped by the

earth herself.

You will be asked to participate in a series of preparatory meetings (council) where you will meet with a

small group of others. Prepare carefully and well. These councils are a forum for deep listening and open

sharing. All voices are equal. Your guides are midwives who will help you give birth to new perceptions.

You may not hold to the same value or belief system; nevertheless, they seek your safety, well-being, and

self-educational growth. They cannot and will not take the steps of preparation for you or accompany

you across the threshold.

You and those with whom you meet comprise a community. This community has been formed to insure

the survival of every one of its members. Therefore, you will probably feel close to the others, especially

as the threshold phase draws near. You will become vividly aware of their emotions, feelings, and

thoughts, even as you are of your own. Hopefully, you will find yourself willing to share your tenderest

places with everyone, and they with you.

The survival of any community depends on the mutual trust of its members. This community is a

microcosm of the human world, and is a way of seeing your place in it.

Are you extroverted, introverted, bold or shy? No matter, you will take part in the essential act of mutual

survival. You will confirm your oneness with your kind by laying your body and soul on the line. And so

our role within the human community is no different than that of the chipmunk. Though separate

beings, we exist for the well being of the collective.

THE DAY WALK

The day walk is a journey upon the face of the earth, a ceremony of preparation, a pilgrimage through

the mirror of nature. In the natural world, signs and symbols of your inward journey are reflected. The

walk is a distilled version of the threshold phase of the vision fast.

During your walk, certain spirits or powers of nature, which typify the nature of your own qualities, are

attracted by and reveal themselves to you. With your help, they weave an allegorical or symbolic story

which indicates future direction, your inherent gifts, and the kinds of ceremonies that you might perform

during the vision fast.

The walk is customarily taken one month before the actual initiation (threshold) time commences. You

prepare the night before by packing an emergency kit and a few other items you might need for a

day's journey. These should include adequate water and a cell phone or other communication device

to be used only in the event of an emergency. Inform someone of the location and duration of your

Get a good night's rest. An important dream may arise on this night.

You walk for several hours, or the entire day if desired, in a safe, natural place. It is suggested that no

food is taken on this day -- until after the sun has set (ultimately, the decision is yours). Set forth on a

wandering, intuitive course, without consciously attempting to reach any goal. As you wander, keep your

eyes open. Be aware if you are feeling "drawn" in any direction or to anything. Listen to and sense the

consciousness of all that is around you.

Remember to honor those who lived on the land long before you and to accept their presence, wisdom,

and enduring connection to the earth. In this way you are not only reconnecting with yourself but with

the Indigenous people who have always understood that the land is full of stories waiting to be heard.

The beginning and the end of your day walk are marked by ceremony. The ceremony that you perform at

these points may be elaborate or simple, but it must involve your focused attention and your gratitude

to the land for her teaching and safe-keeping.

SOLB Vision Fast Handbook  Page 19

It is wise to keep a journal account of your walk and to be familiar with its basic plot -- for it is a kind of

"life story" and you have the leading role. Like your life, this story has a beginning and an end. Be

prepared to share this adventure with your guides, or a close friend. They may be able to point out

salient features of the story you may have overlooked or forgotten.

Like longer solo time in nature, the day walk is an exercise of balance and attunement. It is not a

challenge of the elements or heights, nor is it an endurance contest. The walker maintains connection

with the beauty of life and the reality of death as both are reflected by the world of nature.

Pay special attention to loved ones and friends who call strongly to you as you walk. Some of these

individuals may have already died. They can act as your teachers, heroes, and helpers, watching as you

grow and help to strengthen the circle of your purpose.

THE THRESHOLD

When you step across the threshold, you enter what some might call a “sacred” space. In other words, it

is a place worthy of respect. There is no other human here to define your sense of what is real for you.

The apparent, physical body of the land is all around you. You may sense your own insignificance. You

may surrender to the presence of Nature, acknowledging that you exist within it.

The word threshold is derived from the AngloSaxon thrashold (or "threshing hold"); you will be threshed,

like wheat. The chaff will be snatched away by the hungry wind. The seeds will fall to earth, where they

will become food for your people. For three or four days and nights you will be held there, in the eye of

the threshing-storm of memory. You will face whatever nature brings you. You will become one with that

which thrashes you.

Of course the concept of the threshold space comes from a far older wisdom and should be

acknowledged as such. In the Nahuatl native tongue, the Mictlan, or underworld, is the place where

transformation begins, where you will be broken open and renewed. The rays of Tonatiuh (the Sun) will

sweep over you, removing the chaff that is no longer needed and leaving the seeds that will feed the

The Mictlan teaches you to shed the old and embrace the new. When you step across its threshold you

are no longer who you were, but part of the great cycle of life and death, the eternal Quiahuitl (rain) that

nourishes all life.

Some have returned from their threshold time lamenting that they did not have a "vision." When we

asked them what they meant, they invariably imagined "vision" as an event of great magnitude and

power, an intense moment of non-ordinary reality filled with angels, grails, or rapturous light. But this is

only one of many kinds of vision.

Vision is wisdom. Vision is insight into the nature of things. Vision is feeling in balance and harmony with

all creation. Vision is the ability to see the future. Vision is the ability to dream.

Above all, vision is merely opening the eyes to see what already is.

Those who cross the threshold are never betrayed by vision. Sometimes, however, they are betrayed by

their own limited definitions. Take care that you do not enter with your eyes closed!

The crossing of the threshold is a personal yet formal statement, a self-definition, a step in an ancient

ceremony. You do not cross the threshold empty-handed. You carry with you the inherent wisdom of

your ancestors, and the tools or components that were available to them from ancient times. Twelve of

these components are offered below. Study them. Use them as you choose. Combine or change them to

suit your own needs. Consult with your own teachers

and guides.

1. FINDING YOUR PLACE ON THE EARTH

You will usually have one day to focus on finding a place to live during the threshold time. This is often

the day before you go out alone. You will have a companion, a buddy, who will be your unseen partner

during the days and nights of solitude. Your buddy will be close enough to help you if you are in trouble

and you will offer the same support in return. The two of you will find separate places of power situated

within a half-mile of each other. This arrangement is sometimes a little difficult and requires friendly,

responsible coordination between the two of you.

Truly, the place of power which you seek is inside you. As you enter into the wilderness to look for your

retreat, remember that you seek a place where the inner and outer worlds become one. The assent of

your whole being is the sign you are looking for. This abode of power, where you will stand, sit, and lay

your body down, can be almost anywhere, as long as you are there, empowering it with your spirit. In

turn, the beings of your dwelling will bless and nourish you and give you, not what you want, but what

you need. This place you seek, then, will validate and respond to your own personal power.

Watch yourself search for and then discover your place. There is much in your behavior to observe. How

do you set about looking? Do you have an image of the place in mind? Are you easily satisfied or are you

very particular about what your place looks like, or where it is situated? Do you keep personal safety

uppermost in your mind, or not? Do you plan to hole up like a turtle or range on the exposed ridges like a

bighorn sheep? Do you resent being within walking distance of your buddy or do you welcome the idea

SOLB Vision Fast Handbook  Page 23

of having him/her/them as a neighbor? There is no right or wrong way - only your way and your

responsibility to return safely to your community.

When you find your dwelling, spend some time there. Lie down on the ground and relax against its feel.

Will your soul find strength and insight here? If you were a warrior, would you choose this place to make

your last stand? Is this a good place to "die?" When you return to civilization, you will carry a strong

image of this place in your heart. It will symbolize your own personal power. Do not let yourself be ruled

by the fear that you have made the wrong choice -- unless, in your heart, you are convinced you must

look further.

There are many different kinds of good places. Some are high and exposed; some are low and protected.

Usually, there is something distinctive about them, certain features that resonate with the soul: a cliff, a

6. THE PURPOSE CIRCLE

This circle of which the wind speaks is the most ancient and reverend of ceremonial symbols. It is

important that you acquire an understanding of the meaning and use of the "Purpose Circle," or "Circle

of the Self."

The simple, yet sophisticated circle-symbol has been variously utilized by the Australian Aboriginal

people (the Sacred Enclosure), Native Americans (the Medicine Wheel, the Sweatlodge, the Sun Dance,

the Kiva, etc.), the Mayan/Aztec (the Calendar), the Tibetan Buddhists (the Mandala), the Celts (the

Wheel of the Year), the Jewish people (the Wheel of Life), and many others.

Ordinarily, the Purpose Circle (Circle of Self) is aligned to the four directions, or powers, as seen in the

four points of the compass. These four directions represent the four seasons, the four developmental

ages, the four quadrants of the heavens, the four dimensions, etc. The four-directioned circle of purpose,

like the tabernacle, the cross, the sweat lodge, the mandala, etc., is a whole, balanced, symbolic

representation of the psyche, or self, in a regenerative relationship with the cosmos.

Within this ceremony the Purpose Circle "comes to life" when the initiate builds such a circle with the

intention of being its inhabitant.

SOLB Vision Fast Handbook  Page 29

When you enter your circle, you symbolically take responsibility for your connection in the greater web

of life. You acknowledge the ancient truth: "I am what I perceive, and I am what perceives me”.

The circle is built sometime prior to the last night of the threshold period in an area selected with great

care. It is best to lay down the cardinal stones first, aligning the north and south stones to the North Star.

Then the east and west stones can be added. When this basic framework is down, the circle of stones

may be elaborated as you are prompted. The circle need be no more than six feet across and can be

composed of any material symbols you see fit to add to the basic stones. Make the building of your

Purpose Circle a labor of love. Your circle will enclose, protect, and awaken you. It will follow you through

the wilderness of your life.

Completed, the circle becomes a nexus of your power place, a magnet of spiritual attraction. You cannot

ignore it anymore than you can ignore yourself. By sunset of the last day, you might want to formally

enter the Purpose Circle and begin your vigil, staying awake through the long night, crying for a vision. Or

you might not.

There are many ceremonial ways to utilize the power and meaning of the Purpose Circle. Your guides

will be happy to consult with you regarding the many ways in which the circle can be used as an

effective ceremonial tool.

When you leave your place you will symbolically carry your Purpose Circle with you. It will surround and

protect you as you go. Having oriented yourself to the universe in the ancient, sacred manner of your

ancestors, you will never quite be able to divest yourself of your circle-burden of responsibility and

power. As you must erase all other signs of your sojourn in the sacred world of the threshold, you must

do the same with your Purpose Circle, at most leaving a center stone, knowing that it will remain there

for a long time to come.

It is crucial to remember the privilege inherent in being part of these ceremonies, especially when

considering the long history of colonization and forced separation from ancestral practices. Many

Indigenous peoples have been removed from practicing these ceremonies in their original, unbroken

forms due to the violence of colonization. For anyone  joining  a rite of passage ceremony   today,   it

should be approached with reverence that comes from an understanding of this history.

HEAT EXHAUSTION

Precautions: Find or create shade and stay in it during the hottest hours of the day (11:00 a.m. to 5:00

p.m. depending on the time of the year). Keep your head covered with a hat or a damp bandanna. Take it

easy. Drink water as often as you need to. Do not try to climb a mountain. Respect the power of the sun.

Symptoms: Pale, clammy skin, weakness, nausea, dizziness, tiredness, headache, possible fainting.

Treatment: Rest, shade, water, nightfall. A wet bandanna-hat. A sponge bath.

Precautions:  Always carry your sunscreen, sun hat, and sunglasses with you, even on a short walk.

The air is clear, the sun is hot, and there is reflected radiation from the stones or soil. Protect yourself by

covering up and using a broad-spectrum sunscreen of your choice. A severe case of sunburn will produce

oozing blisters, not an attractive tan. Watch your time in the sun. Do not fall asleep in the nude. If you

DEHYDRATION

Precautions: If you are fasting, the most precious element you possess is water. During the threshold

time you should be drinking three quarts a day, especially in dry climates. Desert winds desiccate. Hot air

pulls moisture from your body whether you feel thirsty or not. You can live for weeks without food.

Without water, in the heat, you have three days or less. Do not hike anywhere without water. Carry your

container wherever you go. Water is not only the remedy for dehydration, but also a preventative

medicine for altitude sickness, and both hypo/hyperthermia.

Symptoms: Weakness, exhaustion, dizziness, nausea.

Treatment: Drink water and rest in the shade. A wet bandanna-hat. Drink more water. Take a reasonable amount of electrolytes and/or salt.

EXPOSURE TO COLD

Precautions:  Always carry with you a wool cap (even in the summer months), warm jacket, and a

raincoat if there is any possibility of a storm moving in.  Stay hydrated.

The weather can turn bitterly cold. The wind can rise like a monster with rain and sleet in its teeth. You

must have clothes that will protect you from the dangers of excessive body heat loss (hypothermia):

ample synthetic or down jackets, wool sweaters, socks, and caps. Remember that the greatest source of

body heat loss is through the crown of your head. Mittens also cut down on heat loss. Remember that

moderate exercise produces some body heat, but excessive exertion without food will expose you to the

Symptoms: Weakness, trembling, uncontrollable shaking, feelings of exhaustion, disorientation, lapsing

measures to be sure the patient's body heat is rising, not allowing the person to sink into unconscious

stupor. Look for frostbite. Warm frostbitten members gradually and carefully, to keep from destroying

skin tissue. Return to basecamp.

storm blows over. Get down low. Do not look for shelter under tall trees, prominent boulders, jutting

outcroppings, or caves.

RETURNING EARLY FROM THE THRESHOLD TIME

Does returning before the threshold time is over mean that you have failed? On the contrary, there is no

FEES AND PAYMENTS

To speak of money is an exercise in humility. We need money to survive, to eat, to feel secure and cared

for, and yet, money carries the weight of many shadows – greed, consumerism, and commodification.

Living in a time  where too often  wealth equates with power, where those with less are viewed as less

than, and where shrewdness is valued over relationship, this comes as no surprise. Our society is

obsessed with money, and so we, at the School of Lost Borders, walk as consciously as possible

whenever we enter into this realm.

Since the inception of the School, over forty five years ago, it has been our commitment to keep our

programs accessible to anyone who feels a strong intention to participate. We recognize that inequities,

due to systemic oppression, create disparities in access to our programs. Thus, our bottom line is to

never turn away anyone from our programs.

We at Lost Borders are doing what we can to face inequity. This is an ongoing, evolving process. We have

a sliding scale, scholarships, and payment plans to help make our programs as accessible as possible. And

we ask that those of financial means or access to wealth pay more, inviting an inquiry into the realities of

systemic wealth inequality as they consider what feels right to offer. We also encourage participants to

seek support through crowdfunding and community engagement, not only because it can help generate

the resources needed, but because it weaves a fabric of personal and community investment in one's

experience. This also promotes a sense of reciprocity between the School, participants, and the

communities that our participants serve.

In order to remain a viable organization, we need money to support our guides and administration, as

well as pay the multitude of expenses that go into running a wilderness-based non-profit. To us, the very

practical things we need in order to support our work, such as insurance, permits, and propane, are

considered sacred, as we cannot hold the ceremony without them.

We are not a charity organization. Nor are we a foundation. We are a small non-profit organization with

a very modest budget. But, really, we are not that either. Rather, we are a group of ordinary people who

just happen to have an insatiable passion – a calling, perhaps – for taking people out on the land as a

means for bringing about community and personal transformation.

We have witnessed again and again the life-changing power of the wilderness rites of passage ceremony,

and for this reason, we are committed to making our programs accessible to all who feel called.

WILDERNESS ETHICS STATEMENT

These guidelines and aspirations are based on respect and reverence for the Earth and on our

recognition of the many dimensions of relationship between human visitors and living wilderness areas.

Our being in natural and wild places is a gift which we honor by doing our best to live lightly, leave no

trace, support the preservation and protection of the Earth, and, when possible, help renew the places

we visit.  We see these attitudes and actions not as sacrifices but as opportunities to express our

gratitude in skillful and meaningful ways and as opportunities to learn how to live better through the

teachings of the Earth. Because our trips go to several different ecosystems—high desert, canyon

country, mountain forests—we adapt our ethics and actions to the particular needs of the place.

We acknowledge the original people of the lands that we work on and look to give back to them

in some way, where possible.

We attend to our attitudes and behaviors in the places we visit.  We work to be mindful of our

impacts on the ecosystem, soil, water, plants, and wildlife of the areas we visit.  We do our best

to understand their ways, rhythms, needs, and systems because understanding supports

responsible and caring action.

We practice Leave-No-Trace camping as appropriate for each bioregion we visit.

We keep our groups small for the sake of safety and to reduce our impacts on the land.

When traveling to, from, and in wilderness areas, we choose routes that will cause the least

damage.  We keep vehicles on established roads and ways, and we walk on the most durable

surfaces.  We take special care to avoid damaging cryptobiotic soil and to avoid eroding stream

We select basecamp sites that will not show lasting signs of our presence. In desert and canyon

areas, we do our best to set up basecamps on rock.  We give our sites enough time to recover

between visits so they show no cumulative signs of our use.  When necessary, we coordinate our

visits with other groups to avoid overuse.

When we do have fires, we keep them small, using dead and down wood (in forested areas), or

bring in our own (in desert and canyon areas).  We use a fire pan, mound, or other method to

avoid scarring rocks or soil.  We either carry out our ashes or scatter them widely.

We use stoves for cooking.  If fire danger is high or fires are not permitted, we do not have fires.

We recognize the fragility of water sources in all wilderness areas and take care not to pollute or

damage them.  In arid places, we avoid water sources for our camps in order not to inhibit

wildlife who depend on scarce water.  We do not wash ourselves or dishes near water sources.

We pack out all our trash.   We use the most appropriate and least impactful method for dealing

with human waste.  Usually this means using a self-contained portable toilet (such as Eco-Safe) in

basecamp.  We carry out all our toilet paper, tampons, etc.  We work to keep ourselves current

on the best ways of disposing of human waste and to apply those ways.

We do our best to remove evidence of our impacts when we leave.  This can include erasing our

footpaths, replacing any rocks we have disturbed, raking the area, and sprinkling leaves, twigs,

and branches to naturalize areas we have used.  We remove all signs of our visit such as markers

for the buddy system, altars, purpose circles, and rock cairns we have built.  We do not disturb

historical rock cairns.  If there are anthropological sites nearby, we do not disturb them.

We work with appropriate land-stewards, landowners and land managers for the areas we visit

to ensure compliance with regulations and to foster good on-going relationships.

We understand that meaning well is not enough, so we work to educate ourselves about

wilderness ethics.  This statement of our ethics is a work-in-progress, which will change as we

learn more.
