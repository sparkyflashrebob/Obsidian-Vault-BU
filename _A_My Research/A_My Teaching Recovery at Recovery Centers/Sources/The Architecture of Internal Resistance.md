The Architecture of Internal Resistance

The Illusion of Control and Conditions

In the context of recovery, the sources identify the core barrier to peace as the habitual attempt to manage how we feel by controlling external conditions

. Addicts and alcoholics are described as sensitive beings who constantly engage in the "push and pull" of life—grasping for pleasant experiences and pushing away unpleasant ones

. This endless cycle of wanting things to be a certain way, or resisting when they are not, creates constant internal conflict

. Because external circumstances and other people are always changing and ultimately beyond our control, trying to manipulate them to secure happiness is a futile and exhausting endeavor

The Five Hindrances

The instructor specifically outlines five mental blockages, known as "hindrances," that pull individuals away from spiritual practice and peace

. These are:

Sense Desire:

The addictive craving to feel a certain way or to obtain external gratification

Resentment, anger, and hatred that arise when reality does not match our desires

Sloth and Torpor:

A depressive, sluggish state where the mind lacks energy, likened to a turtle retreating into its shell

Restlessness and Worry:

An agitated inability to be present, often fueled by anxiety about the future

A rudderless lack of faith in the practice or in oneself, causing the mind to spin without direction

Personality View and Identification

A major obstacle to peace is taking life events personally and rigidly holding onto a fixed identity, a concept introduced as

sakkaya-ditthi

or "personality view"

. When individuals believe their thoughts, temporary moods, or painful histories define who they are, they trap themselves in a conceptual "box" that they feel compelled to defend

. Believing the thought "I am a broken addict" or identifying entirely as a victim keeps a person locked in suffering, preventing them from accessing the unconditioned peace of simply observing the experience without judgment

Resistance and the "Second Dart"

The sources frequently teach the formula that

pain multiplied by resistance equals suffering

. Pain itself—whether it is physical illness, emotional grief, or difficult circumstances—is an unavoidable part of human existence

. However, the true barrier to peace is the "second dart" (or second arrow): our habitual reaction of judging the pain, complaining about it, or fighting reality

. The instructor likens this resistance to pouring salt into an open wound or repeatedly "banging your head against the wall," thereby compounding the initial, unavoidable pain with unnecessary, elective suffering
