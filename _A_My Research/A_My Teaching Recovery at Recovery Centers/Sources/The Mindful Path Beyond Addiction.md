The Mindful Path Beyond Addiction
The core principles of mindfulness for recovery presented in the teachings revolve around changing an individual’s fundamental relationship with their own mind and their experience of the world. Rather than using mindfulness merely to relax or escape, the primary goal is
from the delusions and habitual reactions that fuel addiction
Here are the core principles detailed in the sources:

1. The "Knower" vs. The Experience (Non-Identification)
   A central pillar of this practice is learning that
   you are not your thoughts, emotions, memories, or pain
   . Because addicts often believe they are their depressing thoughts or their physical cravings, they feel compelled to act on them.
   The teachings introduce the concept of the
   —the part of you that is aware of the thought or the pain
   This knowing quality is described as pure and unconditioned; it does not age, get sick, or have a gender, and it is not damaged by trauma
   By stepping back to become the watcher of an experience, the individual creates a psychological gap or "pause" that acts as a
   refuge and protection
   , putting the brakes on addictive reactivity
2. The Equation of Suffering (Acceptance)
   The teachings explicitly define suffering through a simple formula:
   Pain x Resistance = Suffering
   Pain is an inevitable part of nature (e.g., getting old, sick, or experiencing loss)
   Suffering, however, is optional. It is created when we fight, resist, or refuse to accept reality as it is
   True recovery requires letting go of the exhausting attempt to perfectly control and arrange external conditions
   . When resistance drops to zero, mental suffering also drops to zero, even in the presence of physical or emotional pain
3. The Engine of Addiction: Wanting and Not Wanting
   The sources frame the human condition—and the root of addiction—as a constant tension between
   "wanting and not wanting"
   . Humans naturally crave what is pleasant (good food, love, comfort) and push away what is unpleasant (pain, difficult emotions, annoying sounds)
   . Addicts and alcoholics take this to the extreme, using substances to artificially manage their inner world because they refuse to sit with uncomfortable feelings
   . Mindfulness trains the practitioner to be present with whatever arises without needing to instantly gratify a craving or escape a discomfort
4. Impermanence (Arising and Passing Away)
   Another core principle is recognizing the changing nature of all phenomena. Every thought, sensation, and emotion is impermanent—it arises and passes away
   . When a recovering individual directly observes that a craving or a negative mood is just a temporary "cloud passing by," they stop panicking
   8, 55, 58-60
   . They learn that they do not need to "fix" the feeling with drugs or alcohol; if left alone without being fed, the feeling will naturally dissipate on its own
5. Abandoning "Personality View" (From Victim to Student)
   The teachings identify
   Sakkaya-ditthi
   , or "personality view," as a major source of trouble
   . This is the ego-driven habit of taking everything personally—believing that the world is happening
   us, or identifying deeply with labels like "I am a broken person" or "I am an angry person"
   The core shift in mindfulness for recovery is moving from being a helpless
   of one's circumstances to being a
   of one's own life
   Instead of asking "why is this happening to me," the practitioner asks, "what is happening right now, and what can I learn from it?"
