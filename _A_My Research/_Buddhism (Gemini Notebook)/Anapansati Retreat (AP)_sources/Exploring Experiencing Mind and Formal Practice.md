# Exploring Experiencing Mind and Formal Practice

Study Guide: Exploring Experiencing Mind and Formal Practice

Quiz (Short Answer)

According to the source material, what is one key result of seeing the changing nature of things?

What is one suggested practice for fostering dispassion?

How is attending to cessation described as helpful in the context of the mind?

What is Ajahn Buddha's preferred translation for "relinquishing," and what does this imply?

According to the analogy of the raft, what does the raft represent, and when should it be relinquished?

Why is maintaining a questioning attitude ("Is this meditation? Is this mindfulness?") important in practice?

What is the primary function of the physical posture in meditation as described in the source?

According to the source, what are the two fundamental constituents of the mind that are always present?

How does the concept of "nourishing and starving" relate to working with hindrances?

What is one image or analogy used to describe the hindrance of ill will?

Quiz Answer Key

Seeing the changing nature of things leads to resting in non-desiring.

Reflection on the 32 parts of the body or reflection on death are suggested practices for fostering dispassion.

Attending to cessation gives a "window of cutting through" the mind that entangles itself and suffers.

Ajahn Buddha translates relinquishing as "giving back," implying that we are returning things (like form, feeling, mental formations) that we have wrongly appropriated as "self."

The raft represents the path of practice or training, and it should be relinquished once one reaches the "other shore," which is Nibbana (safety/freedom from suffering).

Maintaining a questioning attitude keeps the mind alert and prevents meditation from becoming flat or mechanical.

The physical posture acts as a "weather vein" to check for balance, alertness, presence, and mindfulness.

Feeling (vedana) and perception (sañña) are the two fundamental constituents of the mind.

We "nourish" hindrances by giving them attention and what they crave (e.g., attending to attractive things feeds sensual desire), and we "starve" them by cutting off their sustenance and directing attention to qualities that weaken them (e.g., contemplating death attenuates desire).

The hindrance of ill will is compared to boiling water, which is agitated, unclear, and can scald, or being ill with a fever or headache.

Glossary of Key Terms

#### Impermanence (Anicca):

The changing nature of things; seeing this leads to resting in non-desiring.

#### Dispassion (Virāga):

A cool, clear observation of reality, free from clinging and bias. Cultivated through practices like reflecting on the 32 parts of the body and death.

#### Cessation (Nirodha):

The stopping or ceasing of thoughts, feelings, and sensations. Described as a "window of cutting through" entanglement and suffering. Also translated as "quenching."

#### Relinquishment (Paṭinissagga):

Actively letting go of clinging, particularly the tendency to identify with mental phenomena as "self." Also translated as "giving back."

#### Anapanasati:

Mindfulness of breathing. The discourse on Anapanasati outlines 16 stages of practice.

#### Duka (Dukkha):

Often translated as suffering, dissatisfaction, or the unsatisfactory nature of things. The "this shore" in the raft analogy.

The "other shore" in the raft analogy, representing a state of safety, freedom from suffering, and liberation.

#### Jitta (Citta):

The mind; the sphere where we experience things. Described as having a fundamental potential for awareness and knowing, distinct from the "moods of the mind."

#### Kaya Sankara:

Bodily formations or body conditioners. Refers to the physical body and the breath itself. Experiencing and calming the kaya sankara is a stage in Anapanasati.

Feeling. Along with perception, a fundamental constituent of the mind that conditions it. Includes pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral feelings.

Perception. Along with feeling, a fundamental constituent of the mind that conditions it. Influences our reaction to feelings and can be skillful or unskillful.

Joy or rapture. An aspect of mindfulness of feeling and a constituent of the first jhana. Can be subtle or energetic.

Happiness or pleasure. An aspect of mindfulness of feeling and a constituent of the first jhana. Can be cultivated and experienced through skillful practice.

Defilements or afflictions. Refer to states of mind like greed, hatred, and delusion that stain and condition the mind.

#### Hindrances (Nivarana):

Mental states that obstruct awareness and weaken wisdom. The five main hindrances are sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, and skeptical doubt.

Initial thought or the lifting up of attention. A constituent of the first jhana, used as an antidote for sloth and torpor.

Sustained thought or sustained attention. A constituent of the first jhana, used as an antidote for skeptical doubt.

One-pointedness of mind or focus. A constituent of the first jhana, used as an antidote for sensual desire.

#### Samati (Samadhi):

Concentration or establishing the mind firmly. A state of settled, focused, steady, bright, and peaceful mind, cultivated through practices like mindfulness of breathing.

#### Yoni somanasikara:

Wise attention or skillful attention. Attending skillfully to things in practice.
