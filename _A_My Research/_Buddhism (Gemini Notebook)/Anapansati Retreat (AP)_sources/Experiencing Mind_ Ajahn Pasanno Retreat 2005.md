Briefing Document: Ajahn Pasanno - Winter Retreat 2005 Excerpts

#### Overall Focus:

The excerpts from Ajahn Pasanno's 2005 Winter Retreat talks emphasize the practical application of Buddhist meditation, particularly the Anapanasati Sutta (Discourse on Mindfulness of Breathing), as a means to cultivate clarity, balance, wisdom, and ultimately liberation from suffering. A central theme is the importance of directly experiencing the mind and its contents, rather than just intellectualizing about them.

#### Key Themes and Concepts:

#### The Importance of Experiencing (Viharati) in Meditation:

Ajahn Pasanno stresses that meditation is not merely "watching" from a distance but actively "experiencing fully" and "entering into" the sensations and states of the body and mind. He contrasts "watching" with "experiencing," highlighting the difference between observing from afar and being fully present and engaged with the object of attention.

"I think that that that uh um just that that that sense of we can only really know something fully when we when we when we really enter into it and and feel it, experience it. Uh rather than um kind of step back distance ourselves be a bit aloof and watch from afar that breath as it goes in and out..."

#### Mindfulness of Breathing as a Foundation:

Anapanasati is presented as a fundamental practice for developing attention and stability. The different stages outlined in the Sutta (knowing long/short breaths, experiencing/calming bodily formations, experiencing/calming mental formations, experiencing/calming the mind, investigating impermanence/dispassion/cessation/relinquishment) provide a progressive framework for training the mind.

"And this is the the third tetrad in the the uh discourse on on mindfulness and breathing. It's the third foundation of mindfulness and it's a a uh say a necessary foundation to be attentive to because we uh you know the mind is where we experience things."

#### Experiencing the Mind (Jitta) Directly:

A significant portion of the talks focuses on the third tetrad of Anapanasati, which involves experiencing the mind itself. This is presented as crucial for cutting through delusion and recognizing the true nature of reality.

"And this is the the first um step in the the uh uh in this tetrad experiencing the mind. I breathe in experiencing the mind. I breathe out. So that the u recognizing the the uh what is happening within the mind because we we have a we experience things within the mind and more often than not we don't actually see it or recognize it as this is just the mind."

Experiencing the mind involves seeing mental states (with or without desire, aversion, delusion, contracted, distracted, etc.) as

just the mind

, rather than identifying with them as "me" or "my mind." This distinction helps to alleviate suffering.

"And as Ajancha would often times say, the the one who u is lost in the world is lost in their their moods. One who's lost in their moods is lost in the world."

Ajahn Cha's advice to Ajahn Man to discern the difference between the fundamental mind base (jitta) and the "moods of the mind" (objects of the mind) is highlighted as a crucial insight. The fundamental mind has a potential for clarity and knowing, often obscured by passing moods and mental content.

"discern more clearly what's the difference between the mind and the moods of the mind... underlying that is this sort of fundamental mind base."

Developing stillness (samadhi) is necessary to experience the potential of the mind (jitta) beyond its conditioned movements and contents.

#### Working with Feelings (Vedana) and Perceptions (Saññā):

The second tetrad involves experiencing feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral) and understanding how feelings and perceptions (jitta sankhara) condition the mind. This includes recognizing how perception can influence our reaction to feelings, allowing us to "cast it in another light" for skillful practice.

"So that the Buddhists I think that that's a very natural shift in that when we we really attend to the body, relax the body, are very mindful and we become peaceful within the body. Then there is there's pleasant feeling arising. There's a sense of well-being, sense of a rapture..."

"this feeling and perception is what is conditioning the mind what is creating the mind what is building the content of the mind so that when when we when we experience pain Then we tend to also have a perception."

Skillfully working with feelings and perceptions involves recognizing how they can lead to hindrances or, conversely, support the cultivation of positive states and insights into dharma.

#### Identifying and Relinquishing the Five Hindrances:

The hindrances (sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt) are seen as major obstructions to awareness and wisdom. Recognizing and naming them is the first step in dealing with them.

"it's really important to remember that that is its purpose is for guiding the mind to a place of of clarity and balance where it will then be able to really see things and understand them in a way that liberates the mind."

"So that the the attentiveness to and recognizing how they arise if they're actually there and you know to to one first thing is to actually identify them. Yeah. This is a this is the hindrance of sensual desire..."

The Buddha provides vivid similes for each hindrance (debtor for sensual desire, sick person for ill will, boiling water for ill will, choppy water for restlessness, muddy water for doubt) to highlight their negative impact and the sense of relief when they are relinquished.

"when the Buddha talks about the sense of well-being and happiness that arises say in the mind is from the relinquishing of of hindrances."

#### Nourishing and Starving the Hindrances:

The talks emphasize the importance of understanding what feeds the hindrances and what weakens them. For example, attending to attractive objects nourishes sensual desire, while contemplating aging, sickness, and death attenuates it. Cultivating loving kindness attenuates ill will.

#### The Role of Stillness (Samadhi) and Concentration:

Settling and concentrating the mind (samadhi, jhana) is essential for developing clarity and gaining insight. It purifies the mind of coarser elements and makes it malleable.

"concentrating the mind... is actually the word that you sat in in the in the suta talk. talking about some mati concentrating the I you tend to like to rely on the the Thai translation of this word u which means establishing firmly sort of establishing the mind firmly..."

While important, concentration shouldn't become an object of striving or comparison based on levels (jhana attainment). The focus should be on the mind's ease, steadiness, and mindfulness.

#### Insight (Vipassana) as the Culmination of the Path:

The later stages of Anapanasati move towards insight, focusing on contemplating impermanence, dispassion, cessation, and relinquishment. These are the means to understand the true nature of reality and achieve liberation.

#### Contemplating Impermanence, Dispassion, Cessation, and Relinquishment:

These contemplations are active practices aimed at transforming the mind's relationship to experience.

#### Impermanence:

Seeing the changing nature of things leads to resting in non-desiring.

#### Dispassion:

Cultivating a cool, clear observation of reality, free from clinging and bias. Reflection on the 32 parts of the body and death are suggested practices for fostering dispassion.

Actively attending to the stopping or ceasing of thoughts, feelings, and sensations, opening a "window of cutting through" entanglement and suffering. The image of "quenching" is offered as a preferred translation for cessation.

#### Relinquishment:

Actively letting go of clinging, particularly the tendency to identify with mental phenomena and claim them as "self" or "mine." Ajahn Buddha's translation of "giving back" is highlighted as a helpful perspective – giving back what we have wrongly appropriated.

"Then relinquishing, we're putting attention on relinquishing of that mind that's trying to sort of establish itself in some way and an an importance..."

#### The Analogy of the Raft:

The path of practice (the raft) is a means to reach the other shore (Nibbana) and should be relinquished once the goal is attained.

"The Buddha gives that as a say the the say the this shore being the that duka and crossing the the water, the expanse of water as being the path of practice of training and being able to reach the other shore of Nibbana... one leaves the leaves the raft at that at that at that shore and goes goes up onto the bank in in a place of safety."

#### Asking Questions and Investigation:

Maintaining a questioning attitude in meditation ("Is this meditation? Is this mindfulness? What am I doing?") keeps the mind alert and prevents the practice from becoming flat. Investigating the meaning of terms like "Buddho" (the one who knows) and applying that questioning to one's own experience is encouraged.

"if the question isn't there then you see often times it's just this you know the time passes and the bell rings and one does something else... that that that that sense of of really questioning... is helping to bring up this alertness. Because then without that without that questioning, without that querying, without the investigation, then mindfulness and meditation turns into something very flat."

The physical posture is a useful tool or "weather vein" for checking for balance, alertness, presence, and mindfulness in the meditation practice.
