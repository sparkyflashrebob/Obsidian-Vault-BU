# The Four Pillars of Right Speech

These criteria—that speech must be timely, true, beneficial, and kind—serve as the specific, active guidelines for \*\*Right Speech\*\*, which is the third factor of the Noble Eightfold Path. While Right Speech is often defined by what you \*should not\* do (abstaining from lying, slander, harsh words, and idle chatter), these four qualities dictate \*what, when, and how\* a practitioner should actively communicate.

According to the \*Abhaya-raja-kumara Sutta\*, the Buddha provided a strict filter for evaluating whether something should be said, based on its truth value, utility (is it beneficial?), and emotional impact (is it agreeable or endearing?) \[1\]. 

Here is how these criteria are applied in practice:
\*   \*\*Is it beneficial?\*\* This is the ultimate baseline. The Buddha stated that if a statement is unbeneficial, it should never be spoken—even if it happens to be true or agreeable \[1\].
\*   \*\*Is it true and beneficial, but disagreeable?\*\* If a truth is helpful but might be difficult for the listener to hear (i.e., not endearing), the speaker must use their wisdom to wait for the \*\*proper time\*\* to say it \[1, 2\].
\*   \*\*Is it true, beneficial, and kind?\*\* Even if a statement is factual, helpful, and highly agreeable or endearing, the practitioner must still choose the \*\*proper time\*\* to deliver the message \[2\]. 

In other discourses, the Buddha further emphasizes these positive qualities, teaching that practitioners should abandon idle chatter and harsh speech in favor of words that are "in season" (the right time), "factual" (true), "connected with the goal" (beneficial), and "soothing to the ear," polite, and "affectionate" (kind) \[3, 4\]. 

Ultimately, these guidelines ensure that your communication does not cause harm and instead actively promotes harmony, trust, and spiritual progress \[3, 5, 6\].
