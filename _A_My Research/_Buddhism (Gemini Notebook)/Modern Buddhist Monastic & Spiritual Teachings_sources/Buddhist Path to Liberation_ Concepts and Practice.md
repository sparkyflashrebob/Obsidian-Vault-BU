# Buddhist Path to Liberation: Concepts and Practice

Study Guide: Core Buddhist Concepts and Their Application

I. Gāha: The Three Obsessions and Defilements

A. Definition and Origin

refers to obsessions, derived from the image of being seized by a demon. These obsessions are fundamental hindrances to liberation.

B. The Three Principal Defilements

#### Taṇhā (Craving):

Rooted in the delusion of ownership and possessiveness. This defilement manifests as attachment to things, experiences, and even ideas, believing them to be "mine."

#### Māna (Conceit):

Stemming from the subtle conceit of identity. This refers to the egoistic sense of "I am" or "this is what I am," leading to comparisons and self-aggrandizement.

#### Diṭṭhi (Views):

Centered around the concretization of the body and personality. This involves rigid beliefs and opinions about oneself and the world, often leading to clinging to specific doctrines or self-identification with impermanent phenomena.

C. Practical Application (M 144.9)

The text demonstrates how these defilements manifest through the six sense-spheres (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) by asking: "Do you regard the eye... thus: ‘This is mine, this is what I am, this is my self’?" This highlights the pervasive nature of these obsessions in our perception and self-identification.

II. The Tathāgata's Understanding and Non-Conceiving (A 4.24)

A. The Tathāgata's Knowledge

The Blessed One (Tathāgata) possesses complete knowledge and understanding of "whatsoever is seen, heard, sensed, cognized, attained, sought after and pondered over by the mind." This knowledge is total and comprehensive.

B. Non-attachment to Knowledge

Despite this profound knowledge, the Tathāgata "has not taken his stand upon it." This signifies a state of non-clinging, where knowledge does not lead to conceit, dogma, or rigid views.

C. Avoiding Falsehood and Fault

The text emphasizes that denying this knowledge, or claiming to both know and not know, or neither know nor not know, would constitute falsehoods or faults. This underscores the precise and unperplexed nature of the Tathāgata's realization.

D. The Nature of Non-Conceiving

The Tathāgata "does not conceive of a visible thing as apart from sight; he does not conceive of an unseen; he does not conceive of a ‘thing-to-be-seen’; he does not conceive about a seer." This principle extends to all sense spheres, illustrating a radical non-conceptualization that transcends subject-object duality and attachment to perceptions or the perceiver.

III. Contemplation of Mind and the Six Sense-Spheres

A. Contemplating Mind as Mind (M 10.34-35, D 22.12)

#### A bhikkhu practices contemplating the mind:

#### Internally, Externally, or Both:

Observing one's own mind, the minds of others, or both.

#### Arising and Vanishing Factors:

Focusing on the impermanent nature of mental states, their origination and dissolution.

#### Bare Knowledge and Mindfulness:

Establishing mindfulness that "there is mind" simply to the extent necessary for awareness, without further elaboration or attachment.

#### Abiding Independent, Not Clinging:

The ultimate goal of this practice is to cultivate independence and non-clinging to anything in the world.

B. Cessation of Differentiation and the Six Sense-Spheres (A 4.174)

The cessation of the six sense-spheres (fade and cease without remainder) leads to the cessation and allaying of differentiation. This means:

The range of the six sense-spheres defines the extent of "differentiation" or conceptual elaboration.

When these sense-spheres cease, the process of differentiation also ceases, indicating a state beyond conventional perception and conceptualization.

The "do not say that, my friend" response to questions about "anything else" signifies the ineffable nature of this cessation, beyond binary existence or non-existence.

IV. Developing the Mind and Overcoming Obstacles

A. Developing a Mind Imbued with Luminosity

This practice involves cultivating a mind that is "open and unenveloped." It is achieved by perceiving:

"As after, so before; as before, so after" (temporal continuity).

"As below, so above; as above, so below" (spatial interconnectedness, reviewing the body's impurities as a form of detachment).

"As by day, so at night; as at night, so by day" (consistency of practice and awareness).

B. Obstacles to Desire/Energy

#### Desire that is too slack:

Accompanied by lassitude.

#### Desire that is too tense:

Accompanied by restlessness.

#### Desire that is constricted internally:

Accompanied by sloth and torpor.

#### Desire that is disturbed externally:

Repeatedly distracted by sensual pleasures.

V. The Bondage of Conceiving and Liberation (14.23)

A. Conceiving as a Disease

The text highlights "conceiving" as a subtle bondage, even subtler than that of Vepacitti. Various forms of "I am" statements ("I am," "I am this," "I shall be," "I shall not be," etc.) are all forms of conceiving. Conceiving is explicitly called a "disease, a tumour, a dart," emphasizing its harmful and binding nature.

B. Liberation Through Not Conceiving

By "not conceiving," one is freed from the Evil One (Māra). The training involves dwelling with a mind devoid of conceiving, perturbation, palpitation, and proliferation. These are all facets of the self-identification and conceptual elaboration that bind one to suffering.

VI. The Path to Liberation: Not-Self and Relinquishment

A. Treating Elements as Not-Self

Liberation involves treating the six elements (earth, water, fire, air, space, consciousness) as "not self, with no self based on" them. This leads to the "destruction, fading away, cessation, giving up, and relinquishing of attraction and clinging, mental standpoints, adherences, and underlying tendencies."

B. Destruction of Taints and Final Knowledge

A bhikkhu with taints destroyed and completely liberated through final knowledge demonstrates this by the "destruction, fading away, cessation, giving up, and relinquishing of desire, lust, delight, craving, attraction, and clinging, and of mental standpoints, adherences, and underlying tendencies regarding the six sense-spheres." This signifies a complete detachment from all sensory experience and mental formations.

VII. Voidness and the Qualities of Right View (M 122.4, .6 & .7)

A. Abiding in Voidness Internally

The Tathāgata discovers an abiding: "to enter and abide in voidness internally by giving no attention to all signs." This means cultivating a state of mind free from all external and internal mental elaborations or 'signs'. This promotes seclusion and renunciation.

B. Characteristics of a Person with Right View

#### Absence of Unabandoned Obsession:

The mind is not obsessed by sensual lust, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, doubt, or speculation. This leads to clarity and seeing things as they actually are, making the mind "well disposed for awakening to the truths."

#### Internal Serenity and Stillness:

Cultivating right view leads to personal internal serenity and stillness.

#### Uniqueness of View:

Recognizing that this noble and emancipating view is not shared by other recluses or brahmins outside the Buddha's Dispensation.

#### Character of Confession and Restraint:

Immediately confessing any offense and entering upon restraint for the future, like an infant drawing back from a live coal.

#### Keen Regard for Training:

Maintaining a keen regard for training in higher virtue, mind, and wisdom, even while active in various matters, like a cow watching its calf while grazing.

#### Strength of Heeding Dhamma:

Heeding, attending to, engaging with, and eagerly hearing the Dhamma and Discipline when taught.

#### Strength of Inspiration and Gladness:

Gaining inspiration and gladness in the meaning and Dhamma when taught.

VIII. Impermanence and Acceptance

A. Mindfulness of Death (A.8.73)

Contemplating mindfulness of death ardently leads to the destruction of underlying defilements. The text highlights how our lives lead to entanglements that prevent us from facing death with clarity.

B. The Story of Ay Siripongs

#### Background:

A Thai native convicted of murder, denied involvement in the murders but refused to name accomplices, sentenced to death.

#### Spiritual Transformation in Prison:

Utilized incarceration to develop inner resources. He had temporary Buddhist monastic ordination as a youth and practiced meditation consistently in prison. Guards and inmates recognized his peaceful demeanor.

#### Ajahn Pasanno's Role:

Served as Ay's spiritual advisor. Focused on Ay's spiritual well-being and ability to face death with composure, rather than his past crimes.

#### Preparation for Death:

Guided meditation on breath and light, advising Ay to let go of the breath and focus on light at the moment of death. Encouraged detaching from external distractions and focusing internally.

#### Forgiveness:

Ay had forgiven others but struggled to forgive himself completely. Ajahn Pasanno helped him see that he was not his past memories.

#### Acceptance of Karma:

Ay believed in rebirth and accepted his fate as a result of karma, encouraging others not to feel sad but to see it as a chance to terminate negative karma.

#### Peaceful Demise:

Ay died peacefully, with a serene expression, confirming his inner composure.

IX. Core Teachings and Practices

A. Letting Go

Profound teaching requires letting go of one's own "terms and conditions." When one understands profoundness, personal conditions seem less important. Letting go allows for deeper understanding of teachings.

B. Overcoming Misunderstanding

Many difficulties arise from simple misunderstandings, which can escalate into endless complications like hope, fear, and despair. Realizing the utter unnecessity of such suffering can lead to compassion and appreciation for the absurdity of it all.

C. Balance in Practice and Life

#### Meditation:

Avoid being too forceful (rigidity, stress) or too slack (daydreams, lack of focus). Be alert but not straining, like guitar strings that are tight but relaxed.

#### Relationships:

Seek friendship and support, but also maintain independence. Avoid blaming others (especially parents) for emotional problems; instead, forgive and let go of the past. Extreme self-reliance can stunt growth; embrace support from others.

Develop at one's own pace and abilities, not according to others' timetables.

D. Cultivating Qualities

#### Compassion:

Be yourself, expand sympathies, be kindly, refuse to hate. Pity and compassion are proper feelings towards erring humanity.

Do not fear difficulties; make the best of adversity as a stepping stone. Difficulties exist to make us strong.

Be utterly humble for the foundation of peace. Where there is charity and wisdom, there is no fear or ignorance. Where there is patience and humility, there is no anger or vexation.

A heart full of kindness creates an atmosphere of harmony. Peace radiates peace to other hearts.

E. Nature of Happiness

Happiness is the "free expression of life," possible when the workings of the mind are unconditioned by its past. It is not something to be pursued or sought.

F. Dhamma and Scrutiny

The Buddha's teaching (Dhamma) does not require blind faith. Practitioners are encouraged to continuously examine and scrutinize the teachings, as deeper examination reveals more benefit and value.

G. Dhamma in "The Last Breath"

#### We are what we think:

Our thoughts create our world; pure mind leads to happiness.

#### Island of Self-control:

The wise man creates an "island" through vigor, mindfulness, restraint, and self-control, which no flood can submerge.

#### Steadfastness:

The wise man is unmoved by praise or blame, untouched by happiness or sorrow.

#### Surrendering to Dhamma:

Leads to serene being.

The three principal defilements (gāha) are taṇhā (craving), māna (conceit), and diṭṭhi (views). Taṇhā is built around the delusion of ownership; māna around the subtle conceit of identity; and diṭṭhi around the concretization of the body and personality.

The Tathāgata possesses complete knowledge of all phenomena, but unlike ordinary knowledge, he "has not taken his stand upon it." This means his profound understanding does not lead to clinging, dogmatism, or self-identification with what is known.

A bhikkhu can contemplate mind as mind internally, observing their own mental states, or externally, observing the minds of others. They can also contemplate mind in its arising and vanishing factors, focusing on the impermanent nature of mental phenomena.

The text states that "however far the range of the six sense-spheres reaches, that is how far differentiation goes." When these six sense-spheres fade and cease without remainder, it leads to the "cessation, the allaying of differentiation," implying a state beyond conceptual elaboration.

Two types of problematic desire are "desire that is too slack," which is accompanied by lassitude, and "desire that is too tense," which is associated with restlessness. Both hinder the development of a balanced and luminous mind.

"Conceiving" is considered a disease, tumour, and dart because it represents a subtle bondage rooted in self-identification (e.g., "I am," "I shall be"). This conceptual elaboration perturbs the mind and proliferates suffering, hindering true liberation.

A bhikkhu becomes liberated by understanding and treating the six elements (earth, water, fire, air, space, consciousness) as "not self." This practice leads to the destruction, fading, cessation, giving up, and relinquishing of all attraction, clinging, and underlying tendencies associated with these elements.

The first knowledge attained by a noble disciple practicing right view is the understanding that there is no unabandoned obsession within themselves that would hinder them from knowing and seeing things as they actually are. This mind is then "well disposed for awakening to the truths."

Ay believed his execution was a consequence of karma, whether from this life or a past one, and encouraged others not to feel sad but to accept it peacefully. He viewed it as an opportunity to terminate negative karma and avoid entanglement in future lives.

Ay's writings indicate that he initially felt lonely and isolated, believing he had no true friends due to his "intellectual snob" tendencies and difficulty trusting. He realized that by "letting go" of these barriers, he could count more than a "handful of friends" and overcome his loneliness.

Glossary of Key Terms

#### Abhayagiri Buddhist Monastery:

The first monastery in the United States established by followers of Ajahn Chah, known as "Fearless Mountain." A center for teaching and practice for monastic and lay life.

#### Ajahn Chah:

A respected Buddhist Master of the ancient Thai forest tradition of Theravada Buddhism.

#### Ajahn Pasanno:

Co-abbot of Abhayagiri Buddhist Monastery, a highly respected Dhamma teacher who served as Ay Siripongs' spiritual advisor.

#### Añguttara Nikāya (A):

One of the five Nikāyas (collections) of discourses in the Sutta Piṭaka of the Pāli Canon.

#### Ay Siripongs:

A condemned prisoner on Death Row in San Quentin Prison whose spiritual transformation and peaceful death are chronicled in "The Last Breath."

A Buddhist monk.

A member of the priestly class in Hinduism, often associated with spiritual knowledge or rituals. In Buddhist texts, sometimes refers to non-Buddhist ascetics.

#### Conceiving:

The act of forming an idea or concept, particularly the self-identifying thoughts such as "I am" or "I shall be." Considered a subtle bondage leading to suffering.

#### Craving (Taṇhā):

One of the three principal defilements (gāha), built around the delusion of ownership and possessiveness.

#### Defilements:

Mental states that cloud the mind and lead to unwholesome actions, causing suffering.

The teachings of the Buddha; the universal truth or law; righteousness.

#### Dhammapada:

A collection of sayings of the Buddha in verse form, offering essential Buddhist teachings.

#### Dīgha Nikāya (D):

One of the five Nikāyas (collections) of discourses in the Sutta Piṭaka of the Pāli Canon, containing long discourses.

#### Differentiation:

The process of distinguishing or perceiving differences, often associated with conceptual proliferation and the six sense-spheres.

#### Diṭṭhi (Views):

One of the three principal defilements (gāha), centered around the concretization of the body and personality, leading to rigid beliefs.

#### Equanimity (Upekkha):

A state of mental calmness, composure, and evenness of temper, especially in a difficult situation.

#### Five Cords of Sensual Pleasure:

The five types of objects that stimulate the senses and cause pleasure (forms, sounds, odors, tastes, tactile sensations).

#### Four Noble Truths:

The fundamental teachings of Buddhism: suffering, the cause of suffering, the cessation of suffering, and the path to the cessation of suffering.

Pali term for "obsessions," derived from being seized by a demon; refers to the three principal defilements: taṇhā, māna, and diṭṭhi.

#### Karma (Kamma):

The principle that actions, driven by intention, lead to corresponding consequences in this or future lives.

A state of physical or mental weariness; lack of energy. Associated with "desire that is too slack."

#### Liberated (Mind):

A mind freed from taints, craving, clinging, and all forms of suffering, achieved through final knowledge.

#### Luminosity (of mind):

A state of mind that is open, clear, and unenveloped by defilements.

#### Majjhima Nikāya (M):

One of the five Nikāyas (collections) of discourses in the Sutta Piṭaka of the Pāli Canon, containing middle-length discourses.

#### Māna (Conceit):

One of the three principal defilements (gāha), based on the subtle conceit of identity ("I am").

Often personified as "the Evil One" or "the Tempter," representing all that hinders spiritual progress and liberation.

#### Mindfulness (Sati):

The practice of bringing one's attention to the present moment, sensations, thoughts, and feelings without judgment.

#### Palpitation:

A rapid, strong, or irregular heartbeat; in the context of the text, a manifestation of the agitated mind due to conceiving.

#### Perturbation:

A state of anxiety or mental uneasiness; disturbance. In the text, a consequence of conceiving.

#### Proliferation (Papañca):

The tendency of the mind to expand and elaborate on perceptions and concepts, creating complexity and suffering.

The concept of continuous cyclical existence, where beings are reborn into different realms based on their karma.

#### Renunciation:

The act of giving up, abandoning, or relinquishing something, often worldly attachments, for spiritual progress.

#### Restlessness:

A state of inability to relax or be still. Associated with "desire that is too tense."

#### Right View:

A fundamental component of the Noble Eightfold Path, involving understanding the Four Noble Truths and seeing things as they truly are, free from obsession.

#### Saṃyojanā (Fetter):

Mental chains or bonds that tie beings to the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra).

#### Saṃyutta Nikāya (S):

One of the five Nikāyas (collections) of discourses in the Sutta Piṭaka of the Pāli Canon, containing grouped discourses.

The monastic community of Buddhism (bhikkhus and bhikkhunīs).

#### Sensual Lust:

Intense desire for sensory pleasures, a form of obsession.

#### Six Elements:

Earth, water, fire, air, space, and consciousness – fundamental components of existence that, when clung to, perpetuate suffering.

#### Six Sense-Spheres:

The organs of sense perception (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body) and the mind itself, along with their corresponding objects.

#### Sloth and Torpor:

Mental dullness, drowsiness, and lack of energy, which constrict internal desire.

#### Spiritual Power:

Abilities developed through concentration and meditative practice, such as concentration due to energy, mind, or investigation.

#### Taints (Āsavas):

Mental effluents or corruptions (e.g., sensual desire, desire for existence, ignorance) that lead to suffering and rebirth.

An epithet for the Buddha, meaning "one who has thus come" or "one who has thus gone," referring to one who has attained perfect enlightenment.

#### Theravada Buddhism:

The "Doctrine of the Elders," the oldest surviving branch of Buddhism, prominent in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.

#### Udāna (Ud):

A collection of inspired utterances by the Buddha in the Khuddaka Nikāya of the Sutta Piṭaka.

The monastic disciplinary code followed by the Sangha, established by the Buddha.

#### Voidness (Suññatā):

A key Buddhist concept referring to the absence of inherent existence or self in all phenomena. Entering upon and abiding in voidness internally means giving no attention to all signs or conceptual elaborations.
