# Skillful Means: Truth and Right Action in Buddhism

Buddhism - true but not right, right but not true

The saying "true but not right, right but not true" is a common way to express a nuanced approach to truth and right action found in Buddhist teachings. While not a direct quote from the Buddha, it summarizes a key concept, particularly evident in the concept of skillful means.

#### Here's a breakdown of the two parts:

True but not right: This can refer to something that is factually accurate or aligns with a certain understanding, but whose expression or application might not be beneficial or appropriate in a given situation. For instance:

One might be aware of a personal truth about someone that is not kind or constructive to reveal, according to a Reddit discussion.

Telling a harsh truth that causes unnecessary suffering, says Quora.

Right but not true: This refers to actions or statements that are compassionate and beneficial in a particular context, even if they deviate from strict literal truth.

An example of this is the Buddhist concept of skillful means (upaya), which emphasizes adapting teachings and practices to the unique needs of individuals, according to Bright Way Zen. The goal is to guide others towards liberation from suffering, even if it requires a temporary or partial truth that helps them progress along their path, according to Tricycle: The Buddhist Review.

A classic parable illustrating this is the father who tricks his children out of a burning house by promising them even better toys outside, notes Bright Way Zen. His actions were not strictly true in terms of his promise, but they were "right" because they saved his children's lives.

Key concepts

Right View (Samma Ditthi): The understanding of things as they truly are, free from delusion or distortion. This is an integral part of the Eightfold Path, says www.buddhist-spirituality.net.

Skillful Means (Upaya): The compassionate and flexible adaptation of teachings and actions to suit the specific needs and circumstances of others, according to Bright Way Zen.

Two Truths Doctrine: This Mahayana concept differentiates between relative (or conventional) truth and ultimate truth. Relative truth pertains to the world of appearances, while ultimate truth goes beyond dualistic concepts and reveals the interconnected nature of existence, says Lion’s Roar.

In essence, the phrase highlights that in Buddhism, wisdom and compassion are inseparable. While understanding the truth of things is crucial, expressing that truth and acting upon it must be tempered with kindness, empathy, and an awareness of its potential impact on others. Sometimes, a direct truth might cause unnecessary suffering, while a slightly less direct approach, guided by compassion, might be more effective in leading someone toward liberation from their own suffering.
