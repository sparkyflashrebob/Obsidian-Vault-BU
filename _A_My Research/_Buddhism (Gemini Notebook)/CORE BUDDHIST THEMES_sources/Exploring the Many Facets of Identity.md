# Exploring the Many Facets of Identity

#### The following information comes from the Notebook of MMTCP Education:

Based on the sources and our conversation history, "identity" is a rich and multifaceted theme explored in various ways, ranging from the fundamental spiritual inquiry into the nature of the self to the complex dynamics of social and cultural identities.

#### Here are the various ways that "identity" is explored:

#### The Fundamental Spiritual Inquiry ("Who Am I?"):

A core question in most spiritual traditions is "Who am I?". This formal practice of self-inquiry is a powerful way to see beyond our stories of self and reveal the mystery of our true nature.

Asking "Who am I?" is presented as a pointer to the true nature, not an intellectual question with a word or thought as an answer. It can lead to a space of alert Presence or pure awareness after the thought. This inquiry helps find the "Being" aspect of being a "human being," which is often forgotten.

Investigating awareness involves looking back at "who's looking" or "what's aware".

One discovers that what they have been looking for all their life is their self, but not the historical self. The question "Who am I?" points to Stillness.

#### The Sense of a Separate Self, Ego, and "Selfing":

Much of our lives are spent in the "trance of a separate self" moving through time. The trance becomes increasingly apparent over time.

Waking up from the story of a separate self is described as a process of enlightenment.

The "ego loves the sense of self". It can manifest in grandiose fantasies or trying to be the "most spiritual" person.

"Selfing" is the term used for the constant self-referencing that occurs in a mentally constructed world where the protagonist is self. It can manifest as insecurity (thinking "I'm nothing") or specialness (thinking "I'm the sage on the stage"). Selfing gets in the way of flow and presence in teaching.

Identifying with thought when it is charged or having valence is a sign of a core belief that makes sense of reality and "who we are".

Mindfulness practice can open up a sense of self to investigate what happens when that self falls away.

Glimpses of freedom awaken intrinsic awareness and release attachment to the belief that organizes the sense of self.

#### Identity Disorientation and Freedom When Beliefs Dissolve:

When a sense of self has been organized around a belief (like "I don't deserve love") and that belief is seen as untrue, one may feel disoriented and confused, not knowing "who I would be" or "who you are anymore".

This disorientation can create space for relaxation, or a sense of groundlessness that is enlivening, fascinating, or frightening.

Asking "Who would I be if I no longer lived with this belief?" holds great spiritual power.

Losing the sense of a separate self allows for spontaneous and creative living without the encumbrance of constricting beliefs or habits, making it possible to realize the "true mystery and wholeness of who you are".

#### Identity and the RAIN Practice:

The RAIN (Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Nurture/Nourish) practice is presented as a way to "undo identity" and stuckness.

Recognizing what is arising (like anxiety) loosens identification; the sense of "who I am" enlarges, becoming more like the sky aware of the cloud rather than being inside it.

Allowing releases the resistance that keeps us hooked to emotions, which is an "incredibly powerful piece of dissolving identity".

Investigating (primarily the felt sense in the body, not cognitive analysis) helps drop the identity with the body, as identity is sustained by thoughts. Questions like "What am I believing?" are part of investigation. Investigating the vulnerability underneath difficult feelings shifts identity from the "scared self" to mindful presence.

Non-identification (the original N in RAIN) is described as the "gift of RAIN," the freedom that comes from the practice, not a doing. Without Nurture, this can feel like a "dry emptiness".

Nurturing completes the process of shifting from the self identified with beliefs and feelings to the "more authentic self". After investigating and nurturing, there's a realization of "who we are" as the awareness that cares.

#### Identity in Relation to Difficult Experiences:

Pain or other difficult experiences can "snag our identity" if we are not mindful. For example, not noticing physical tensing can lead to identifying as a "physically oppressed self," or not noticing mental judging leads to being snagged as "the judge".

Identifying with what is happening is a quick conditioning response when pain arises.

Agreeing to be with challenging experiences ("This too is part of life. You belong.") creates space and presence, dissolving identity hooks.

Working with anger, blame, and resentment often involves being identified with a small part of oneself (victim, aggressor, defender) and seeing only a small part of others. Healing involves widening the lens to experience others from awareness, returning to respect.

#### Social, Cultural, and Group Identities (especially Race):

Identity is explored in the context of race, gender, oppression, and marginalization.

Mindfulness practice expands attention from the personal to the collective, the individual to the community, and the personal into the cultural collective. This involves exploring the interplay between internal cultural conditioning and external interactions.

Racial identity is presented as a key area of exploration. We are all individuals, but also part of racial group identities. Racial group identities are not equal; some are dominant, others subordinated.

Identity is described as constructed, not a "real thing that's out there somewhere". Privilege often involves being taught not to see identity's constructed nature. These constructed identities can lead to needless suffering. Mindfulness is a tool for investigating how race is constructed internally.

There is a distinction between individual identity and group identity. It can be hard to see dynamics of harm and well-being through just an individual lens; a group identity lens reveals patterns.

Affinity groups are a way to cultivate "groupness" and unpack conditioning around race in a safe, intimate space.

The concept of a "racialized self" and the experience that arises in racialized situations is discussed.

Privilege can be enacted on an interpersonal level, such as asserting the "right" to be seen as an individual rather than a member of a racial group, which is not a privilege afforded to non-white people. Shifting to an identity where one is oppressed to avoid the "race-based hot seat" is another example.

Trying on new ways of relating to race feels uncomfortable because it involves standing on unfamiliar ground, not necessarily danger.

Reflection exercises involve considering when one first became aware of race, the relationship between skin color and caste/class, how this has impacted one's life, and whether one identifies with designations like "person of color" or "BIPOC".

Participants are asked to list all the ways they identify (student, musician, gender, etc.) to see the many dimensions of identity.

The intention/impact dynamic is explored, emphasizing understanding impact, as denying impact can deny identity.

In conflict, there can be a habit of blaming the group identity of another. Inquiry involves asking "What is it like being you?" individually or collectively.

The concept of self-identification refers to the voluntary declaration of Indigenous identity.

#### The Teacher's Identity and Role:

The role of a spiritual teacher or meditation teacher is described as a function, not a fixed identity.

Teachers are encouraged to notice "from which seat" they teach, especially regarding racial dynamics.

Developing as a teacher involves relating wisely to "selfing" (both insecurity and specialness).

Taking the risk to lead or share authentically when one's "old identity didn't fit" that role is part of growth.

Setting intention before teaching ("What really matters?") is related to aspiration and identity as a teacher.

Reflecting on how one's learning in DEIA is incorporated into teaching practice relates to one's evolving identity as a teacher.

In summary, identity is explored as a spiritual concept (the sense of a separate self vs. true nature/awareness), a psychological construct (beliefs shaping who we think we are, identification with thoughts and emotions), a social reality (racial and group identities, constructed nature of identity), and in the context of personal growth and the teacher's role (unpacking conditioned identity, stepping into function vs. fixed self). Practices like RAIN and inquiry are key tools for investigating and loosening the grip of limiting or contracted identities.
