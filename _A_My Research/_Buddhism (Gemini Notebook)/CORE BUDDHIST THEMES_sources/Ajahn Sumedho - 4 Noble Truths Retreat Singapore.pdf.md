The sources describe a meditation retreat centered on the contemplation and investigation of the Four Noble Truths, emphasizing mindfulness, awareness, and the realization of ultimate reality (Dhamma) in the present moment. The retreat environment is structured to limit sensory distractions and promote stillness and reflection.

Here is a detailed day-by-day summary of the retreat, focusing on the main themes and key points discussed by Ajahn Sumedho:

The 4 Noble Truths Retreat: Day-by-Day Summary

Day 1: Establishing the Foundation of Refuge and Suffering (Dukkha)

Summary of Day 1: The retreat begins with a welcome and an explanation of the foundational framework: taking the Three Refuges and adhering to the Eight Precepts. The central focus is introduced—the Four Noble Truths—as a means of understanding human experience and realizing the Deathless.

Key Points for Day 1

Details Source Citation

The Three Refuges

Buddha is defined as the refuge in awareness/mindfulness; Dhamma is the refuge in ultimate reality; and Sangha is the refuge in those who practice in the proper way. Dhamma is the true, silent refuge, available all the time.

The Eight Precepts

These precepts impose restraint and form, guiding attendees away from distractions (such as intentionally killing, stealing, sexual activity, false speech/chatting, intoxicants, eating after designated time, adornments, and luxurious sleep). They encourage maintaining noble silence to prevent distraction through speaking.

First Noble Truth (Dukkha)

Suffering (Dukkha) is the most common, ordinary human experience. It is something to be understood, not merely believed in or run away from. Understanding suffering leads to the realization of the Deathless.

Key Points for Day 1

Details Source Citation

Awareness as the Goal

Awareness is identified as the gate to the deathless; realizing one's true nature is ultimately perfect Dhamma rather than personality or emotional habits.

Day 2: The Impermanence of Conditions and the Witnessing Position

Summary of Day 2: The discussion shifts to the core meditation practice, defining it as witnessing reality without judgment. The fundamental insight—the impermanence of all conditions (Anicca)—is highlighted as the key to liberation.

Key Points for Day 2

Details Source Citation

Puto (Witnessing)

The core practice is to be the puto (witness/observer) of

experience, which involves observing without thinking, judging, or criticizing. Buddha is a symbol for this wisdom and knowing.

Nature of Dhamma

Dhamma (ultimate reality) is perfect, unconditioned, and timeless; it is a pointer, not a phenomenon that can be objectified, seen, or thought.

Anicca (Impermanence)

The primary reflection is that all conditions/phenomena are impermanent. Suffering arises when individuals lose perspective and identify with changing conditions, thoughts, or emotions.

Consciousness Consciousness is described as boundless, non-personal, and the very ground of being. It is what we trust, and space and form (like the body) manifest and disappear within it.

Day 3: Investigating the Body and Non-Attachment to Self

Summary of Day 3: The third day, typically marking the end of the initial adjustment period, encourages calming down, patience, and relaxation. The reflection deepens into the first Noble Truth by focusing on the body and the delusion of identity.

Key Points for Day 3

Details Source Citation

Initial Adjustment

The first three days focus on patience and relaxation, allowing participants to become accustomed to the retreat environment and calm their minds.

The "I Am" Position

Attendees are advised to frame experiences of suffering by taking the person out of them (e.g., "There is pain in the body" or "There is anger") rather than interpreting them from the "I am" position ("I am angry"). The ego, or personality view

(sakayadi), is the first fetter to realization.

True Nature vs. Identity

Through insight meditation (Vipassanā), practitioners begin to see that their true nature is free, pure, and perfect conscious awareness, which is the gate to the deathless. Grasping conditioned identities is the cause of suffering.

Day 4: Continued Investigation and Mindfulness

Summary of Day 4: While specific daily talk details are minimal in the sources, this day follows the pattern of continuous, sustained application of the core principles: reflection, non-judging observation of phenomena, and cultivating mindfulness (Sati). The Four Noble Truths remain the "task" of the retreat.

Key Points for Day 4

Details Source Citation

Sustained Investigation

The task remains the sustained investigation of suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path leading to cessation.

Mindful Postures

Practices include focusing on the four postures (sitting, standing, walking, lying down) and the natural function of breathing to establish awareness and connect to the present moment.

Right Effort Right effort is sometimes equated with no effort—the relaxed state of being here and now, trusting awareness

rather than applying tension or trying to get something.

Summary of Day 5: This day contrasts different meditation approaches and clarifies the crucial

purpose of the core teachings. Techniques like samatha (concentration) are acknowledged as

useful for tranquil states, but the ultimate goal is the wisdom realized through investigating Dhamma.

Key Points for Day 5

Details Source Citation

Samatha vs. Vipassanā

Samatha (concentration practice, e.g., using Butto mantra

on the breath) is helpful to stop the wandering mind and achieve temporary tranquility. However, true wisdom

(Vipassanā, insight) arises from reflecting on Dhamma, as

tranquility is a created state.

Directional Sign The Four Noble Truths are a directional sign pointing to ultimate reality, not a dogma or metaphysical teaching. The mistake of organized religion is grasping the sign instead of following the direction it points.

Bliss/Cessation (Nirodha)

True bliss is defined as silent, uncreated awareness. The goal is the realization of the cessation of suffering (Nirodha), which is the end of the born, the created, the formed, and the conditioned.

Investigate for Yourself

The teaching is an encouragement to investigate life and find the truth of the Four Noble Truths for oneself, rather than relying on belief or ceremony.

Day 6: Trusting Awareness and Integrating Practice

Summary of Day 6: As the retreat nears its end, the emphasis shifts to trusting pure awareness and preparing to integrate the practice into daily life. The core instruction "it's like this" is used as a non-critical tool for immediate presence.

Key Points for Day 6

Trusting Awareness

The main emphasis is to trust pure awareness. This awareness is already here and now, timeless, and is the gate to the deathless. It is not something to be developed or acquired.

Impersonal Conditions

All conditions are impermanent and not self (anatta);

Lay Practice Integration

The retreat results in a natural calming due to limited distractions. The Five Precepts are recommended as a very wise standard for restraint, providing a strong moral foundation for mindful daily life outside the retreat setting.

Day 7: Conclusion and Reaffirmation

Summary of Day 7: The final morning of the retreat focuses on the closing ceremony and the transition back to daily life. The essential teaching—trusting in continuous awareness—is reaffirmed as the lasting refuge.

Key Points for Day 7

Details Source Citation

Transition and Ceremony

The retreat ends, and participants transition from the Eight Precepts back to the Five Precepts. The closing ceremonies (including asking for forgiveness) provide a form for marking these events.

Final Instruction

The determination to trust in awareness is reinforced.

Awareness of Emotions

The essential practice involves being aware of emotional states like anger, fear, or jealousy, rather than identifying with them, following them, or resisting them. Awareness is the real refuge.

Ongoing Refuge

The abiding refuge remains in Buddha, Dhamma, and Sangha (awareness, truth, and community of practitioners).

The entirety of the retreat acts as a skillful means to observe phenomena, leading to the

insight (Vipassanā) that enables the "penny to drop"—a direct, intuitive seeing of truth that

transcends intellectual understanding. This consistent application of reflection creates the

conditions for realizing the unconditioned, unborn reality (Nibbana).
