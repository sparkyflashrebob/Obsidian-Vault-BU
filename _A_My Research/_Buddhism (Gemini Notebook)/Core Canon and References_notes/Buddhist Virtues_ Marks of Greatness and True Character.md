# Buddhist Virtues: Marks of Greatness and True Character

The sources provide extensive information on the "Thirty-Two Marks of a Great Man" within the broader context of various qualities and attributes discussed in Buddhist teachings.

### The Thirty-Two Marks of a Great Man

The "Thirty-Two Marks of a Great Man" (P. mahāpurisa) are specific physical signs that indicate an individual destined to become either a universal monarch (wheel-turning king) or a Fully Enlightened Buddha \[1-16\]. In ancient Indian culture, these marks served as indicators of a great person \[1, 2\].

\*\*Description and Significance:\*\*
The texts enumerate these marks in detail, which include:
\*   Feet with level tread and wheels with a thousand spokes on the soles \[17-22\].
\*   Long fingers and toes, soft and tender hands and feet, and net-like hands and feet \[17-22\].
\*   High-raised ankles and legs like an antelope’s \[17-22\].
\*   The ability to touch and rub his knees with either hand while standing without bending \[17-22\].
\*   Male organs enclosed in a sheath \[17-22\].
\*   A bright, golden complexion and delicate, smooth skin that no dust adheres to \[17-22\].
\*   Body-hairs that are separate, one to each pore, and grow upwards, bluish-black, curling in rings to the right \[17-22\].
\*   A divinely straight body with seven convex surfaces, a lion-like front part, and no hollow between the shoulders \[17, 18, 20-23\].
\*   Proportioned like a banyan-tree, with height equal to arm span, and an evenly rounded bust \[17, 18, 20-23\].
\*   A perfect sense of taste and jaws like a lion’s \[17, 18, 20-23\].
\*   Forty even teeth with no spaces, and very bright canine teeth \[17, 18, 20-23\].
\*   A very long tongue and a Brahma-like voice, like the karavīka-bird \[17, 18, 20-23\].
\*   Deep blue eyes with eyelashes like a cow’s \[17, 18, 20-23\].
\*   White, soft hair between the eyebrows and a head shaped like a royal turban \[17, 18, 20-23\].

These marks are not merely arbitrary physical traits; they are considered "virtuous ripenings" and are the result of specific virtuous deeds performed in past lives as a bodhisatta \[1, 6, 24, 25\]. They reveal the person's inner qualities and serve a pedagogical purpose by illustrating the connection between action and karmic results \[26-28\]. While some scholars find certain marks "absurd" or "mythological," the texts emphasize their role in demonstrating ethical qualities \[27, 28\]. The Buddha, as a supreme emanation body, appears with these marks to guide disciples toward higher rebirth and the highest good \[1\]. Notably, sages from other traditions recognized these marks but were unaware of their karmic origins \[24, 29\]. There are also "eighty minor marks" that further describe a Great Man \[26-28\].

The Buddha sometimes performed feats of psychic power to demonstrate these marks, such as revealing his sheathed genitals or extending his tongue to cover his forehead, to dispel doubts of observers like Ambaṭṭha and Sela \[30-36\].

### Broader Context of Qualities and Attributes

The sources discuss various qualities and attributes that are essential in the Buddhist path, applying to Buddhas, Arahants, and "true men" (sappurisa).

\*\*Qualities of the Buddha:\*\*
The Buddha possesses numerous profound qualities beyond the thirty-two physical marks. He is described as an Arahant, fully-enlightened, perfected in knowledge and conduct, a Well-Farer, Knower of the worlds, incomparable Trainer of men, and Teacher of gods and humans \[37-42\]. His teachings are consistently portrayed as "deep, hard to see, hard to realize, tranquil, refined, beyond the scope of conjecture, subtle, to-be-experienced by the wise" \[43-56\].

Specific attributes unique to a Buddha include:
\*   \*\*Ten Tathāgata powers and four kinds of self-confidence:\*\* These powers of knowledge and fearlessness are distinctive of a Buddha, enabling him to guide and instruct beings according to their individual aptitudes and dispositions \[57-59\].
\*   \*\*Eighteen unique qualities:\*\* These encompass unshared behaviors (e.g., no mistaken physical actions due to mindfulness), unshared realizations, unshared awakening activities, and unshared pristine wisdoms \[60-62\]. His knowledge regarding the exhaustion of defilements is completely purified, unstained, and luminous, with all elaborations due to latencies destroyed \[63\].
\*   \*\*Compassion and fearlessness:\*\* He is always upheld by great compassion, is fearless, not lacking courage, and not outshone by any being in the world \[63\].
\*   \*\*Mastery over thought and jhānas:\*\* He can think whatever he wants, gain the four jhānas (meditative absorptions) at will, and has attained mental mastery over thought \[64, 65\].
\*   \*\*Unassailability:\*\* He cannot be overcome by lust, hatred, delusion, or any external or internal foe \[66, 67\].

The Buddha's attitude towards praise and criticism is also notable; he does not respond with false modesty but notes where praise is factual and helpful as a teaching strategy \[68, 69\]. The very recollection of the Buddha's qualities is said to inspire confidence, provide spiritual direction, and overcome despair \[70, 71\].

\*\*Qualities of Arahants:\*\*
Arahants, as perfected disciples, are liberated in mind and embody profound qualities. They venerate the Buddha, recognizing that his attainment of enlightenment assists others in achieving their own liberation \[57\]. Arahants are characterized by their imperturbability in the face of powerful sense objects and possess ten powers of knowledge \[72\].
The texts distinguish between types of Arahants, such as those "liberated-in-both-ways" (capable of immaterial attainments) and those "liberated-by-wisdom" (lacking that specific capacity) \[73, 74\]. Those Arahants who possess all three true knowledges and all six direct knowledges are accorded special veneration \[73, 74\].

\*\*Qualities of a "True Man" (Sappurisa) and "Person of Integrity":\*\*
The teachings classify individuals into categories like "moral" (susīla) and "immoral" (dussīla), "foolish" (bāla) and "wise" (paṇḍita), and "good man" (sappurisa) and "bad man" (asappurisa) \[75\].
\*   \*\*Wise Person:\*\* Demonstrates the ability to answer deep questions effectively, avoids physical and vocal misconduct (especially harsh words), and works for the welfare of oneself and others \[76, 77\].
\*   \*\*Good Person:\*\* Possesses the ability to know and acknowledge gratitude, qualifies as a good friend, associates with the wise, and is willing and skillful in helping the poor and needy \[76\].
\*   \*\*True Man (Sappurisa):\*\* Characterized by faith, shame (hiri), and fear of wrongdoing (ottappa); being learned, energetic, mindful, and wise \[78, 79\]. A true man associates with other recluses and brahmins who share these wholesome qualities \[78, 79\]. Their intentions, counsel, speech, and actions are free from affliction for themselves or others, and they adhere to right view, acknowledging the reality of good and bad actions and different realms of existence \[80-84\]. Unlike a "person of no integrity" (asappurisa), a good person is discreet about others' faults but open about their virtues and their own shortcomings \[85-88\].

\*\*Cultivation and Evaluation of Qualities:\*\*
The Buddhist path emphasizes the cultivation of wholesome qualities and the abandonment of unwholesome ones \[84, 89-91\]. Key practices for this include:
\*   \*\*The Ten Perfections (Pāramī):\*\* Giving, morality, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness, resolve, loving-kindness, and equanimity \[92-94\].
\*   \*\*The Three Higher Trainings (Sikkhā):\*\* Higher morality (adhisīla), higher mind (adhicitta), and higher wisdom (adhipaññā) \[95-99\].
\*   \*\*The Seven Purifications (Visuddhis):\*\* Purity in terms of virtue, mind, view, overcoming perplexity, knowledge and vision of what is and is not the path, knowledge and vision of the way, and knowledge and vision \[99-102\].

The texts stress the importance of "appropriate attention" (yoniso manasikāra) and having "admirable friends" (kalyāṇa-mitta) as the most crucial internal and external factors for attaining the goal of practice \[103, 104\]. It is advised to test any view or belief by its practical results and to check it against the experience of observant and wise individuals \[89, 90, 103\]. When choosing spiritual mentors, one should investigate their internal qualities rather than relying on titles or external appearance \[105-107\]. The sources also warn against "shameless monks" (alajjī) and "immoral monks" (dussīlo), noting that ignorance of the Vinaya (monastic discipline) among lay people can contribute to the decline of moral standards in the Saṅgha \[108-110\]. Critiquing the faults of others, particularly Bodhisattvas, is considered unwholesome and detrimental to one's own spiritual progress; instead, the practice of examining and abandoning one's own mistakes is emphasized \[111\].
