  From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia Various forms of emotional suffering in Stoicism
  **Stoic passions** are various forms of [emotional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional) suffering in [Stoicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism), a school of [Hellenistic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_philosophy).

#### Definition

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=1)]
*The passions* are transliterated *pathê* from Greek.[ 1 ] The Greek word pathos was a wide-ranging term indicating an infliction one suffers.[ 2 ] The Stoics used the word to discuss many common emotions such as anger, fear and excessive joy.[ 3 ] A passion is a disturbing and misleading force in the mind which occurs because of a failure to reason correctly.[ 2 ] For the Stoic [Chrysippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysippus) the passions are evaluative judgements.[ 4 ] A person experiencing such an emotion has incorrectly valued an indifferent thing.[ 5 ] A fault of judgement, some false notion of good or evil, lies at the root of each passion.[ 6 ] Incorrect judgement as to a present good gives rise to delight, while lust is a wrong estimate about the future.[ 6 ] Unreal imaginings of evil cause distress about the present, or fear for the future.[ 6 ]
These states of feeling are disturbances of mental health which upset the natural balance of the soul, and destroy its self-control.[ 6 ] They are harmful because they conflict with right reason.[ 7 ] The ideal Stoic would instead measure things at their real value,[ 6 ] and see that the passions are not natural.[ 8 ] To be free of the passions is to have a happiness which is self-contained.[ 8 ] There would be nothing to fear—for unreason is the only evil; no cause for anger—for others cannot harm you.[ 8 ]

#### Primary passions

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=2)]
The Stoics beginning with [Zeno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeno_of_Citium) arranged the passions under four headings: distress, pleasure, fear and lust.[ 9 ] One report of the Stoic definitions of these passions appears in the treatise *On Passions* by [Pseudo-Andronicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andronicus_of_Rhodes) (trans. Long & Sedley, pg. 411, modified):
**Distress** ( **lupē** ) [Distress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(medicine)) is an [irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrationality) contraction, or a fresh [opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opinion) that something bad is present, at which people think it right to be [depressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_(mood)). **Fear** ( **phobos** ) [Fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear) is an irrational [aversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_aversion), or avoidance of an expected [danger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk). **Lust** ( **epithumia** ) [Lust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lust) is an irrational desire, or pursuit of an expected [good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodness_and_value_theory) but in reality bad. **Delight** ( **hēdonē** ) Delight is an irrational swelling, or a fresh opinion that something good is present, at which people think it right to be [elated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mania).
Two of these passions (distress and delight) refer to emotions currently present, and two of these (fear and lust) refer to emotions directed at the future.[ 9 ] Thus there are just two states directed at the prospect of good and evil, but subdivided as to whether they are present or future:[ 10 ]
**Present** **Future** **Good** Delight Lust **Evil** Distress Fear

#### Subdivisions

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=3)]
Numerous subdivisions of the same class are brought under the head of the separate passions. The definitions are those of the translation of Cicero's *Tusculan Disputations* by J. E. King.

##### Distress

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=4)] Envy [Envy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy) is distress incurred by reason of a [neighbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood)'s prosperity. Rivalry Rivalry is distress, should another be in possession of the object desired and one has to go without it oneself. Jealousy [Jealousy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jealousy) is distress arising from the fact that the thing one has coveted oneself is in the possession of the other man as well as one's own. Compassion [Compassion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compassion) is distress arising from the wretchedness of a neighbor in undeserved suffering. Anxiety [Anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety) is oppressive distress. Mourning [Mourning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mourning) is distress arising from the untimely death of a beloved object. Sadness [Sadness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sadness) is tearful distress. Troubling Troubling is burdensome distress. Grief [Grief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grief) is torturing distress. Lamenting Distress accompanied by wailing. Depression [Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_(mood)) is distress accompanied by brooding. Vexation Vexation is lasting distress. Despondency Despondency is distress without any prospect of amelioration.

##### Fear

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=5)] Sluggishness Sluggishness is fear of ensuing toil. Shame [Shame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shame) is fear of disgrace. Fright [Fright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear) is paralyzing fear which causes paleness, trembling and chattering of teeth. Timidity [Timidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timidity) is fear of approaching [evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evil). Consternation Consternation is fear upsetting the [mental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind) balance. Pusillanimity Pusillanimity is fear following on the heels of fright like an attendant. Bewilderment Bewilderment is fear paralyzing [thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought). Faintheartedness Faintheartedness is lasting fear.

##### Lust

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=6)] Anger [Anger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anger) is lust of punishing the man who is thought to have inflicted an undeserved [injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injury). Rage [Rage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rage_(emotion)) is anger springing up and suddenly showing itself. Hatred [Hatred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hate) is inveterate anger. Enmity Enmity is anger watching as opportunity for [revenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenge). Wrath [Wrath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrath) is anger of greater bitterness conceived in the innermost heart and [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul). Greed [Greed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_deadly_sins#Greed) is insatiable lust. Longing Longing is lust of beholding someone who is not present.

##### Delight

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=7)] Malice [Malice](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/malice) is [pleasure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleasure) derived from a neighbor's evil which brings no advantage to oneself. Rapture [Rapture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapture) is pleasure soothing the soul by charm of the sense of [hearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_(sense)). Ostentation [Ostentation](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ostentation) is pleasure shown in outward demeanor and puffing oneself out extravagantly.

#### Good feelings

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Stoic_passions&action=edit&section=8)]
The [wise person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sage_(philosophy)) ( *sophos* ) is someone who is free from the passions (apatheia). Instead of passion, the sage experiences good-feelings ( *eupatheia* ) which are clear-headed.[ 11 ] These emotional impulses are not excessive, but nor are they diminished emotions.[ 12 ][ 13 ] Instead they are the correct rational emotions.[ 13 ] The Stoics listed the good-feelings under the headings of joy ( *chara* ), wish ( *boulesis* ), and caution ( *eulabeia* ).[ 5 ] Thus if something is present which is a genuine good, then the wise person experiences an uplift in the soul—joy ( *chara* ).[ 14 ] The Stoics also subdivided the good-feelings:[ 15 ]

* **Joy:** Enjoyment
* Cheerfulness
* Good spirits
* **Wish:** Good intent
* Goodwill
* Welcoming
* Cherishing
* Love
* **Caution:** Moral shame
* Reverence
