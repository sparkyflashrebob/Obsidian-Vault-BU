  In the contemporary Mahayana and [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) Buddhist traditions, the three kleshas of ignorance, attachment, and aversion are identified as the root or source of all other kleshas. These are referred to as the three poisons in the Mahayana tradition, or as the three *unwholesome roots* in the Theravada tradition.
  While the [early Buddhist texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Buddhist_texts) of the [Pali Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_Canon) do not specifically enumerate the three root kleshas, the *three poisons* (and the kleshas generally) came to be seen as the very roots of [samsaric existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_(Buddhism)).

  In the [Pali Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_Canon)'s discourses (sutta), *kilesa* is often associated with the various passions that defile bodily and mental states. In the Pali Canon's [Abhidhamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidhamma_Pitaka) and post-canonical [Pali literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_literature), ten defilements are identified, the first three of which – greed, hatred, delusion – are considered to be the "roots" of suffering.

##### Sutta Piṭaka: mental hindrances

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=2)]
In the Pali Canon's [Sutta Piṭaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutta_Pi%E1%B9%ADaka), *kilesa* and its correlate *upakkilesa* [ 1 ] are affective obstacles to the pursuit of direct knowledge (abhijñā) and wisdom (pañña).
For instance, the [Samyutta Nikaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samyutta_Nikaya) includes a collection of ten discourses ([SN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samyutta_Nikaya) 27, Kilesa-sa *ṃ* *yutta* ) that state that any association of "desire-passion" ( *chanda-rāgo* ) with the body or mind[ 2 ] is a "defilement of mind" ( *cittasse'so upakkileso* ):
"Monks, any desire-passion with regard to the eye is a defilement of the mind. Any desire-passion with regard to the ear... the nose... the tongue... the body... the intellect is a defilement of the mind. When, with regard to [these six bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayatana), the defilements of awareness are abandoned, then the mind is inclined to [renunciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nekkhamma). The mind fostered by renunciation feels malleable for the [direct knowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhijna) of those [qualities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhamma) worth realizing."[ 3 ]More broadly, the [five hindrances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_hindrances) – sensual desire ( *kāmacchanda* ), anger ( *byāpāda* ), sloth-torpor ( *thīna-middha* ), restlessness-worry ( *uddhacca-kukkucca* ), and doubt ( *vicikicchā* ) – are frequently associated with *kilesa* in the following (or a similar) manner:
[A]ll those Blessed Ones had first abandoned the five hindrances,
defilements of the mind that weaken wisdom ...[ 4 ]sabbe te bhagavanto pañcanīvara *ṇ* e pahāya
cetaso upakkilese paññāya dubbalīkara *ṇ* e ... .[ 5 ]Additionally, in the [Khuddaka Nikaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khuddaka_Nikaya)'s [Niddesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niddesa), *kilesa* is identified as a component of or synonymous with craving ( *taṇhā* ) and lust ( *rāga* ).[ 6 ]

##### Abhidhamma: ten defilements and unwholesome roots

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=3)]
While the Sutta Pitaka does not offer a list of *kilesa* , the [Abhidhamma Pitaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidhamma_Pitaka)'s [Dhammasangani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhammasangani) (Dhs. 1229 *ff* .) and [Vibhanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibhanga) (Vbh. XII) as well as in the post-canonical [Visuddhimagga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visuddhimagga) (Vsm. XXII 49, 65) enumerate ten defilements ( *dasa kilesa-vatthūni* ) as follows:

1. greed (lobha)
2. hatred (dosa)
3. delusion (moha)
4. conceit (māna)
5. wrong views (micchāditthi)
6. doubt (vicikicchā)
7. torpor (thīna *ṃ* )
8. restlessness (uddhacca *ṃ* )
9. shamelessness (ahirika *ṃ* )
10. recklessness (anottappa *ṃ* )[ 7 ]
    The Vibhanga also includes an eightfold list (a *ṭṭ* *ha kilesa-vatthūni* ) composed of the first eight of the above ten.[ 8 ]
    Throughout Pali literature, the first three *kilesa* in the above tenfold Abhidhamma list ( *lobha dosa moha* ) are known as the "unwholesome roots" ( *akusala-mūla* or the root of [akusala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_views_on_sin)); and, their opposites ( *alobha adosa amoha* ) are the three "wholesome roots" ( *kusala-mūla* or the root of [kusala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merit_(Buddhism)#Pu%C3%B1%C3%B1a,_kusala_and_Nirvana)).[ 9 ] The presence of such a wholesome or unwholesome root during a mental, verbal or bodily action conditions future states of consciousness and associated mental factors (see [Karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma_(Buddhism))).[ 10 ]

The 5th-century CE [commentarial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atthakatha)[Visuddhimagga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atthakatha), in its discussion of "Dependent Origination" (Pali: paticca-samuppada) ([Vsm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visuddhimagga). XVII), presents different expository methods for understanding this teaching's twelve factors (nidana). One method (Vsm. XVII, 298) divides the twelve factors into three "rounds" (va *ṭṭ* *a* ):

* the "round of defilements" (kilesa-va *ṭṭ* *a* )
* the "round of [kamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma_in_Buddhism)" (kamma-va *ṭṭ* *a* )
* the "round of results" (vipāka-va *ṭṭ* *a* ).[ 12 ][ 13 ]
  In this framework (see Figure to the right, starting from the bottom of the Figure), *kilesa* (["ignorance"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avidya_(Buddhism))) conditions *kamma* (["formations"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankhara)) which conditions [results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vipaka) (["consciousness"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinnana) through ["feelings"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedana)) which in turn condition *kilesa* (["craving"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%E1%B9%87h%C4%81) and ["clinging"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upadana)) which condition *kamma* (["becoming"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhava)) and so on.[ 11 ]Buddhaghosa (Vsm. XVII, 298) concludes:
  So this [Wheel of Becoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel_of_Becoming), having a triple round with these three rounds, should be understood to spin, revolving again and again, forever; for the conditions are not cut off as long as the round of defilements is not cut off.[ 12 ]As can be seen, in this framework, the round of defilements consists of:
* ignorance (avijjā)
* craving ( *taṇhā* )
* clinging (upādāna).[ 12 ]
  Elsewhere in the Visuddhimagga (Vsm. XXII, 88), in the context of the four noble persons ( *ariya-puggala* , see [Four stages of enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_stages_of_enlightenment)), the text refers to a precursor to the attainment of [nibbana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nibbana) as being the complete eradication of "the defilements that are the root of the round" (va *ṭṭ* *a-mūla-kilesā* ).[ 14 ]

#### Sanskrit Sravaka and Mahayana literature

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=5)]

##### Three poisons

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=6)] Main article: [Three poisons (Buddhism)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_poisons_(Buddhism))The three kleshas of ignorance, attachment and aversion are referred to as the **three poisons** (Skt. *triviṣa* ) in the Mahayana tradition and as the **three unwholesome roots** (Pāli, *akusala-mūla* ; Skt. *akuśala-mūla* ) in the Theravada tradition. These three poisons (or unwholesome roots) are considered to be the root of all the other kleshas.

##### Six root kleshas of the Abhidharma

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=8)]
The Abhidharma-kośa identifies six root kleshas ( *mūlakleśa* ):

* Attachment ( *raga* )
* Anger (pratigha)
* Ignorance (avidya)
* Pride/Conceit (māna)
* Doubt (vicikitsa)
* Wrong view/False view/Opinionatedness ( *dṛiṣṭi* )[ 17 ]
  In the context of the [Yogācāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogacara) school of Buddhism, Muller (2004: p. 207) states that the Six Klesha arise due to the "...reification of an 'imagined self' (Sanskrit: *satkāya-dṛṣṭi* )".[ 18 ]

##### Mahaparinirvana Sutra

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=9)]
The [Mahayana Mahaparinirvana Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana_Mahaparinirvana_Sutra) lists approximately 50 kleshas, including those of attachment, aversion, stupidity, jealousy, pride, heedlessness, haughtiness, ill-will, quarrelsomeness, wrong livelihood, deceit, consorting with immoral friends, attachment to pleasure, to sleep, to eating, and to yawning; delighting in excessive talking and uttering lies, as well as thoughts of harm.[citation needed]

##### Two obscurations

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=10)]
Mahayana literature often features an enumeration of "two obscurations" (Wylie: *sgrib gnyis* ), the "obscuration of conflicting emotions" (Sanskrit: *kleśa-avaraṇa* , Wylie: *nyon-mongs-pa'i sgrib-ma* ) and the "obscuration concerning the knowable" (Sanskrit: *jñeya-avaraṇa* , Wylie: *shes-bya'i sgrib-ma* ).[ 19 ]

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=11)]

#### Overcoming the kleshas

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kleshas_(Buddhism)&action=edit&section=12)]
All Buddhist schools teach that through Tranquility ([Samatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samatha)) meditation the kilesas are pacified, though not eradicated, and through Insight ([Vipassana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vipassana)) the true nature of the kilesas and the mind itself is understood. When the empty nature of the Self and the Mind is fully understood, there is no longer a root for the disturbing emotions to be attached to, and the disturbing emotions lose their power to distract the mind.[citation needed]
