# **The Cloud of Unknowing and Buddhist Contemplative Traditions: A Comparative Analysis of Apophatic Mysticism and Meditative Praxis**

## **The Apophatic Architecture of The Cloud of Unknowing**

Originating in the late fourteenth century from an anonymous cloistered cleric—widely presumed to have been a Carthusian prior or solitary living in the English Midlands—*The Cloud of Unknowing* represents an exceptional vernacular synthesis of Christian Neoplatonic apophaticism and late medieval faculty psychology1. Drawing from the foundational sixth-century Greek corpus of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, which the author also translated into Middle English under the title *Dionise Hid Divinite*, the text establishes an uncompromising theology of the *via negativa*1. The treatise addresses a young contemplative embarking on the transition from the active to the singular solitary life, proposing that the ultimate ontological reality of God infinitely exceeds the capacities of human conceptualization, sensory imagination, and discursive intellect1.  
The contemplative architecture of the work rests upon an operational polarity: the "cloud of forgetting" and the "cloud of unknowing"1. To prepare the consciousness for the unitive presence of the Divine, the contemplative is directed to cast all created reality beneath a thick, impenetrable "cloud of forgetting"1. This exclusionary field encompasses not only secular preoccupations and memories of worldly affairs, but also the intellect's own pious, discursive meditations1. Traditional theological reflections regarding the attributes of God, imaginative reconstructions of the Passion of Christ, and devotional veneration of angels or saints—practices celebrated as the summit of the active life—are explicitly classified as liabilities within the higher work of contemplation1. The author argues that whatever is held in the discursive intellect occupies the field of consciousness, thereby standing between the soul and God and keeping the practitioner fixated upon a created object beneath the Creator1.  
Above the soul, separating the creature from the divine essence, hangs the "cloud of unknowing"1. This barrier is not a material darkness or an atmospheric phenomenon, but an epistemological darkness arising from the absolute disproportion between finite human knowing and the transcendent infinity of God1. In Chapter 6, the author articulates the central axiom of the treatise: while God remains perpetually incomprehensible to the human intellect (*wit*), the Godhead is entirely accessible to the human capacity for loving desire (*will*)1. Because discursive thinking cannot attain to God, conceptual thought must be surrendered, allowing a "naked intent directed unto God" to pierce the darkness1.  
The primary operative instrument in this contemplative practice is the "sharp dart of longing love"1. Rejecting analytical meditation, the practitioner cultivates an affective, unified impulse of the will1. To stabilize this intention against discursive interruptions, the author instructs the contemplative to condense spiritual intent into a single monosyllabic anchor word, such as "GOD" or "LOVE"1. This word is not to be analyzed, expounded upon, or discursively probed; rather, it is fastened to the heart as an undivided spiritual instrument to suppress incoming conceptual thoughts into the cloud of forgetting1. Contemplation is thus stripped of conceptual scaffolding, demanding an intentional dwelling in what the author terms "nowhere bodily and nought," where ordinary intellectual faculties fail and divine reality is received solely through unmerited grace1.

## **Buddhist Contemplative Paradigms: Zen, Vipassanā, and Samatha**

Buddhist meditation encompasses diverse lineages that share an epistemological commitment to extinguishing mental afflictions, dissolving cognitive delusions, and recognizing things as they actually are (*yathābhūta*)6. In Soto Zen, systematized by Eihei Dōgen in thirteenth-century Japan, the foundational praxis is *shikantaza* ("just sitting")8. In programmatic texts such as *Fukan Zazengi* and *Genjōkōan*, Dōgen rejects instrumentalist paradigms that view sitting as a sequential technique aimed at achieving an extrinsic state of enlightenment10. For Dōgen, practice and realization are non-dual (*shushō-ittō*); sitting upright in awareness constitutes the immediate realization of inherent Buddha-nature9.  
Dōgen characterizes the inner posture of *shikantaza* as "thinking of not-thinking" by passing into "beyond-thinking" (*hishiryō*)11. This state represents an open, non-dual awareness wherein all measuring, judging, and conceptual processing are suspended, allowing the practitioner to "drop off body and mind" (*shinjin datsuraku*)10. Rather than projecting an ego-bound subject onto external phenomena, the practitioner permits the myriad phenomena of reality (*dharmas*) to substantiate the true self9. This radically apophatic stance resonates with what modern Zen traditions, such as that of Seung Sahn, designate as the cultivation of "don't-know mind," a state of immediate, pre-conceptual awareness that precedes cognitive fabrication13.  
In Theravāda and classical Indian Buddhist phenomenology, meditative training is organized around the complementary frameworks of *samatha* (tranquility or mental stabilization) and *vipassanā* (insight into impermanence \[*anicca*\], suffering \[*dukkha*\], and non-self \[*anattā*\])6. A primary psychological objective in this meditative matrix is the pacification and cessation of *papañca*—the proliferation of discursive thoughts, linguistic projections, and ego-referential fabrications that obscure direct sensory perception15. In early discourses such as the *Cūḷa-Suññata-sutta* ("Lesser Discourse on Emptiness"), the Buddha outlines a systematic process of stripping away perceptual complexities17. The meditator progressively empties consciousness of the perception of human settlement, then the perception of forest, followed by the perception of earth, eventually traversing the formless meditative attainments (*arūpa-jhānas*) to reach the unshakeable liberation of the mind17. In this trajectory, emptiness (*suññatā*) functions as an active meditative approach: consciousness systematically attends to what is absent in the present state of awareness while remaining aware of what remains, ultimately eradicating mental taints (*āsavas*) and resting in unconditioned peace6.

## **Methodological and Psychological Convergences**

The operational parallels between *The Cloud of Unknowing* and Buddhist meditative practices illustrate common psychological mechanisms for managing human consciousness6. These intersections become apparent across the treatment of discursive distraction, attentional focusing, and the mitigation of psychosomatic anomalies1.  
The management of discursive proliferation constitutes a point of intersection between both traditions1. In *The Cloud*, discursive thoughts—regardless of their moral or theological quality—are diagnosed as disruptions during contemplative practice1. The author illustrates how an apprentice contemplative who entertains a seemingly holy thought regarding the kindness or Passion of Christ soon finds the discursive apparatus analyzing its details1. This analytical reflection immediately stimulates memory, introducing recollections of personal transgressions, geographical settings, and secular concerns, ultimately scattering the soul across cognitive tangents1. This psychological sequence matches the Theravāda description of *papañca*, where raw sensory input prompts feeling (*vedanā*), which triggers perception (*saññā*), conceptual thought (*vitakka*), and an unchecked cascade of conceptual proliferation that entangles the mind15.  
To overcome this conceptual drift, Chapter 32 of *The Cloud* outlines two specific inner methods1. The first is a technique of deliberate disregard: the contemplative learns to look "over the shoulders" of intrusive thoughts, fixing the gaze of the heart upon the Divine beyond them and acting as though unaware of their arising1. This technique mirrors the bare awareness developed in *vipassanā* and *shikantaza*, where transient mental phenomena are observed without grasping, identification, or aversion, allowing thoughts to self-liberate within the field of awareness1. The second method is one of total surrender: when thoughts prove overwhelming, the practitioner "cowers down" like a defeated captive, acknowledging complete egoic inability to quiet the mind1. This capitulation dismantles the subtle pride and psychic strain that perpetuate cognitive friction, fostering an inner humility that dissolves the ego's attempts to control the contemplative process1.  
The mechanics of concentration in both traditions rely on intentional cognitive reduction1. *The Cloud* prescribes compressing spiritual intention into a monosyllabic word1. The author stresses that this word must remain unanalyzed and unified; should the intellect attempt to explain its attributes, the practitioner must refuse discursive dialogue1. The sacred monosyllable operates not as an object of semantic meditation, but as an attentional anchor that gathers the dispersed faculties of consciousness1. This practice functions identically to the use of a meditation subject (*kammaṭṭhāna*), such as the breath in *ānāpānasati*, seed syllables in Vajrayāna, or the critical phrase (*huatou*) in Zen koan introspection20. The monosyllable collects the mind into concentrated unity (*ekaggatā*), allowing discursive activity to subside so that the practitioner can eventually rest in open, wordless presence1.  
Both traditions also establish clear warnings regarding somatic strain and psycho-sensory deviations1. The author of *The Cloud* criticizes novices who misunderstand spiritual metaphors in a literal, physical fashion1. Those who interpret instructions to "lift up the heart" or "draw inward" as physical actions strain their chests, roll their eyes toward the ceiling, or forcefully turn their sensory organs inward against nature1. The treatise explains that such strain causes nervous exhaustion, neurological instability, emotional volatility, and a range of involuntary mannerisms—such as physical twitching, compulsive fidgeting, unnatural vocal piping, and frantic physical gestures1.

The treatise also cautions against psycho-sensory experiences during contemplation, including spontaneous visual flashes, phantom melodies, sweet olfactory sensations, and physiological heat in the chest or viscera1. While contemporary medieval culture often revered such manifestations as marks of divine favor, the author categorizes them as hallucinations or demonic deceptions rooted in physical overheating and spiritual vanity, instructing practitioners to dismiss them1.  
This corresponds to the Zen concept of *makyo*—perceptual distortions, waking dreams, and physiological sensations that arise during deep zazen as subconscious impressions release into conscious awareness20. Zen masters consistently warn that *makyo* must not be mistaken for enlightenment (*satori*), directing students to remain detached from all such phenomena20. In a similar vein, classical Theravāda *abhidhamma* literature classifies visions of light and auras (*obhāsa*) as corruptions of insight (*vipassanupakkilesa*), which tempt the meditator into claiming spiritual attainment6. Both traditions maintain that authentic interior transformation unfolds in a bare, non-sensory ground of awareness, stripped of psychic phenomena1.

## **Epistemological and Metaphysical Convergences: Unknowing and Śūnyatā**

Beyond procedural mechanics, comparative scholars of mysticism—such as William Johnston, Rupert Gethin, and Ninian Smart—have examined the epistemological continuities between the Dionysian "divine darkness" of *The Cloud* and the Buddhist doctrine of emptiness (*śūnyatā*)6.  
In *The Cloud of Unknowing*, the experiential approach to God is designated as "nowhere bodily" and "nought" to the discursive intellect1. When the practitioner ceases projecting spatio-temporal categories and created concepts onto reality, ordinary sensory consciousness perceives an absolute void1. In Chapter 68, the author explains that the "outer man" evaluates this silent interior state as "nought," whereas the transformed "inner man" recognizes it as "All"1. This darkness is not an ontological void or an absence of being; rather, it is an epistemological blindness caused by an excess of divine light that overwhelms the finite faculties of human reason1.  
This paradoxical apophasis shares common ground with the treatment of *śūnyatā* across Madhyamaka philosophy and Zen discourse15. Emptiness in Mahāyāna Buddhism is not an ontological void, but the radical absence of intrinsic, independent existence (*svabhāva*) in all conditioned and unconditioned phenomena10. Everything exists purely in an open web of interdependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*)10.  
Resting in emptiness requires relinquishing intellectual grasping, conceptual categories, and linguistic dualisms3. The author of *The Cloud* insists that God cannot be comprehended through dimensional, spatial, or qualitative descriptions, just as Buddhist philosophy employs the fourfold negation (*catuṣkoṭi*) to demonstrate that ultimate reality cannot be captured by assertions of existence, non-existence, both, or neither1. In both frameworks, liberation from conceptual delusion requires an epistemological collapse of discursive frameworks, opening into an immediate apprehension of reality beyond concepts1.  
This epistemological emptying is linked to the deconstruction of the reified ego10. In Chapters 43 and 44 of *The Cloud*, the author identifies the deepest barrier to unitive contemplation: the "naked witting and feeling of thine own being"1. Even when the external world and previous spiritual experiences are submerged beneath the cloud of forgetting, the persistent, self-referential awareness of one's own ego remains1. The author describes this reified self-awareness as a heavy, corrupting burden—a "foul stinking lump" congealed with one's being—that prevents the soul from resting purely in the Divine1. The contemplative must endure a profound interior sorrow until this existential clinging to autonomous selfhood is overcome by grace1. The author clarifies that the practitioner must not desire ontological non-existence, which would be an offense against the goodness of creation, but rather must be liberated from the self-reflexive consciousness of the ego1.  
This interior de-centering parallels the Buddhist realization of *anattā* (non-self)7. In Buddhist analysis, suffering and delusion arise from grasping the impermanent aggregates (*skandhas*) as a permanent, substantial self10. The experiential breakthrough in Zen is summarized in Dōgen’s *Genjōkōan*: "To study the Buddha Way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self. To forget the self is to be verified by all things. To be verified by all things is to let the body and mind of the self and the body and mind of others drop off"9. Both *The Cloud* and the Zen tradition demonstrate that ultimate spiritual realization requires an unraveling of egoic reference, shifting the practitioner from an insulated, grasping subject to an unconditioned field of presence1.

## **Irreducible Divergences: Relational Theism versus Non-Theistic Interdependence**

Despite notable structural and phenomenological alignments, significant divergences emerge when examining the ontological frameworks, the role of affective desire, and the theological foundations governing *The Cloud of Unknowing* and Buddhist meditation7. Reducing these two traditions to an identical perennial essence obscures the foundational distinctions that animate their practices6.  
The metaphysics of *The Cloud of Unknowing* is rooted in classical Christian theism and creation theology1. Although the author uses bold apophatic language, stating that the soul is "made a God in grace" and unified with the Divine as "one spirit," he maintains an orthodox ontological boundary: the creature remains distinct from the Creator in nature1. God is the uncreated, transcendent source of existence, while the human soul is a created, contingent being1. Mystical union (*unio mystica*) is an encounter between two distinct realities united in love1. The goal is an affective harmony of wills, wherein the human will surrenders fully to the divine will1.  
Buddhist traditions, by contrast, are fundamentally non-theistic27. There is no personal Creator, no transcendent ontological Ground of Being, and no divine Other to whom the will surrenders7. The principle of dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*) explicitly precludes a primary, uncaused cause10. Meditative awakening is not a union between two realities, nor the reception of supernatural life into a created soul; it is *bodhi*—the direct realization of the nature of existence, the exhaustion of karmic conditioning, and liberation from the cycle of birth and death (*saṃsāra*)8.

| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Metaphysical Reality** | Transcendent, Triune, personal God; Absolute Creator of all existence1. | Non-theistic suchness (*tathatā*); Emptiness (*śūnyatā*); Interdependent Origination8. |
| **Spiritual Goal** | Relational unitive contemplation (*unio mystica*); union in love and will1. | Awakening (*bodhi*); cessation of defilements (*nibbāna*); release from suffering7. |

| **Soteriological Agency** | Absolute primacy of unmerited divine grace; God initiates and completes the work1. | Self-reliance (*jiriki*); mastery of mental conditioning; uncovering inherent Buddha-nature11. |
| **Function of Desire** | Cultivation of spiritual yearning ("blind stirring of love", "sharp dart of longing love")1. | Cessation of craving (*taṇhā*) and clinging (*upādāna*); resting in equanimity (*upekkhā*)22. |
| **Anthropology of Self** | Real created soul; burdened by original sin; transformed by grace, eternally distinct in essence1. | Absence of permanent soul (*anattā*); transient aggregate of five skandhas empty of self-nature10. |

The psychological function of desire marks a foundational divergence between the two paths1. The contemplative methodology of *The Cloud* relies upon cultivating deep spiritual longing1. The author repeatedly urges the apprentice to stir up a "sharp dart of longing love," to "languish for love," and to press into the cloud of unknowing1. The intellect is silenced not to extinguish intentionality, but to free the will so it may reach toward the personal Divine1.  
In contrast, Buddhist psychology identifies craving (*taṇhā*) and clinging (*upādāna*) as the root causes of suffering (*dukkha*)22. While early Buddhist pedagogy utilizes wholesome desire (*chanda*) as a provisional tool along the path, advanced practices such as Soto Zen’s *shikantaza* demand the cessation of all seeking or directional yearning8. Dōgen cautions that harboring aspirations to "become a Buddha" creates dualistic tension, insisting that genuine sitting requires resting without conceptual aims11. This distinction reflects the taxonomy described by theologian Aloysius Pieris: the Christian path operates primarily through the dynamic of *Agape* (transformative self-giving love), whereas the Buddhist path operates through *Prajñā* (liberating, non-attached wisdom)22.  
The question of soteriological agency further illustrates this contrast1. In *The Cloud*, the contemplative act cannot be achieved through human skill, psychological technique, or natural effort1. It remains "the work of only God, specially wrought in what soul that Him liketh, without any desert of the same soul"1. The human role is limited to disposing the soul through humility and non-resistance1. In Buddhist thought, liberation does not depend on external divine election or the infusion of supernatural grace24. Even traditions that emphasize "other-power" (*tariki*), such as Pure Land Buddhism, operate within the causal frameworks of karma and compassionate vow-power, while meditative traditions like Theravāda and Zen emphasize personal effort and the realization of inherent Buddha-nature11. As Dōgen writes in the *Fukan Zazengi*, the reality of awakening is already present; one need only abandon conceptual fabrication to realize it11.  
These structural differences provide the foundation for Steven T. Katz’s constructivist critique of comparative mysticism6. Katz challenges perennialist models that posit an identical, unmediated mystical state that is merely interpreted through varying religious vocabularies post-factum7. Katz contends that there are no unmediated mystical experiences: the religious doctrines, theological expectations, linguistic habits, and cultural training that a practitioner brings to practice actively structure the experience itself7.  
Applied to *The Cloud of Unknowing* and Buddhist meditation, Katz's contextualist critique demonstrates that the Christian contemplative does not encounter an abstract void and label it "God"; rather, the experience is shaped from the outset by Christian Trinitarian concepts, penitential humility, and the desire for union with Christ1. The silence of *The Cloud* remains a relational communion with the hidden Creator1.  
Conversely, the Buddhist practitioner practicing *vipassanā* or *zazen* is shaped by the paradigms of *anattā*, dependent co-arising, and the cessation of cyclic rebirth10. The experience of emptiness is an experiential deconstruction of substantialist delusions rather than an encounter with a transcendent Person7. While functional, somatic, and cognitive parallels exist across meditative states, the meaning and intentional content of those states are bound to their respective theological and philosophical frameworks24.

## **The Modern Synthesis: Intermonastic Dialogue and Centering Prayer**

The historical intersection between *The Cloud of Unknowing* and Buddhist meditation evolved during the latter half of the twentieth century, moving from textual comparison to experiential monastic dialogue and liturgical adaptation8.  
Thomas Merton was instrumental in initiating this dialogue28. In his late writings, including *Mystics and Zen Masters* (1967) and *Zen and the Birds of Appetite* (1968), as well as his exchanges with D.T. Suzuki, Merton argued that Zen’s direct deconstruction of the ego and conceptual dualisms provided a valuable perspective for Western theology26. Merton identified significant parallels between the apophatic void of the Dionysian tradition, John of the Cross, and *The Cloud of Unknowing*, and the non-dual awareness cultivated in Zen26. For Merton, engaging with Zen offered Western contemplatives a way to recover their own apophatic traditions, stripping away institutional rationalism to rediscover contemplative prayer of the heart26.  
William Johnston, an Irish Jesuit scholar at Sophia University in Tokyo, examined these connections in *The Mysticism of the Cloud of Unknowing* (1967), *The Still Point* (1970), and *Christian Zen* (1971)23. Johnston documented phenomenological resonances between the sudden realization of Zen *satori* and the "beam of ghostly light" piercing the cloud described in fourteenth-century English mysticism4. Johnston suggested that Zen somatic practices—including physical alignment, breath regulation, and methodologies for settling mental fluctuations—could serve as a useful support for Christian apophatic prayer, helping practitioners sustain loving intent without falling into physical strain23.  
The practical integration of these insights culminated in the mid-1970s at St. Joseph’s Abbey in Spencer, Massachusetts8. Fr. William Meninger encountered *The Cloud of Unknowing* and, collaborating with Abbot Thomas Keating and Fr. Basil Pennington, sought to make contemplative prayer accessible to contemporary people8. Initially termed "The Prayer of the Cloud," the method was later designated Centering Prayer, borrowing a phrase from Merton's descriptions of prayer centered on God8.  
The developers of Centering Prayer synthesized the medieval instructions of *The Cloud* with the structured sitting formats and silent discipline characteristic of Zen sesshins8. The practice formalizes the teachings of *The Cloud* into a daily discipline: practitioners sit quietly with closed eyes, introduce a sacred word to signify their intention to consent to God's presence, and gently return to that word whenever thoughts, memories, or sensations arise8.  
Centering Prayer emphasizes intention over attention: the focus is not on sustained concentration upon an object, but on maintaining an interior posture of consent to divine action within8. This modern development demonstrates how medieval Christian apophaticism and East Asian meditative structures have informed one another, contributing to a contemporary recovery of silent, non-discursive contemplative practice3.

## **Conclusions**

*The Cloud of Unknowing* and Buddhist meditative traditions share a common psychological insight: when the human mind is caught in discursive proliferation (*papañca*) and ego-referential conceptuality, it remains cut off from direct apprehension of reality1. Both systems create rigorous apophatic methods designed to quiet the discursive intellect, reduce the grasping ego, and move beyond conceptual speculation1. In their practical methods for managing wandering thoughts, their use of simple attentional anchors, and their shared wariness regarding somatic strain and sensory illusions (*makyo*), both traditions demonstrate comparable approaches to the mechanics of human attention1.  
These phenomenological parallels, however, do not diminish their distinct metaphysical and theological horizons6. *The Cloud of Unknowing* remains an apophatic expression of Christian theism1. Its engine is the loving will (*agape*), its foundation is the reality of a transcendent Creator, and its culmination is an unmerited union (*unio mystica*) granted by divine grace1.  
Buddhist meditation, conversely, operates within a non-theistic framework of dependent origination and non-self (*anattā*)7. Its engine is liberating wisdom (*prajñā*), its ground is the dynamic emptiness (*śūnyatā*) of all things, and its culmination is awakening (*bodhi*) from delusion8.  
A comparative study of *The Cloud of Unknowing* and Buddhist meditation reveals two distinct ontological journeys that employ remarkably similar psychological mechanisms6. While their theological frameworks remain distinct, their shared commitment to silent contemplation demonstrates that human consciousness reaches its deepest levels of stillness when discursive intellectual activity falls away1.
