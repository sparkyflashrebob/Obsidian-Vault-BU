  The **three poisons** (Sanskrit: *triviṣa* ; Tibetan: *dug gsum* ) in the [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana) tradition or the **three unwholesome roots** (Sanskrit: *akuśala-mūla* ; Pāli: *akusala-mūla* ) in the [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) tradition are a [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) term that refers to the three root [kleshas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleshas_(Buddhism)) that lead to all negative states. These three states are [delusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha_(Buddhism)), also known as [ignorance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avidy%C4%81_(Buddhism)); [greed or sensual attachment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raga_(Buddhism)); and [hatred or aversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dvesha_(Buddhism)).[ 1 ][ 2 ] These three poisons are considered to be three afflictions or character flaws that are innate in beings and the root of craving, and so causing [suffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dukkha) and [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebirth_(Buddhism)).[ 1 ][ 3 ]
  The three poisons are symbolically shown at the center of the Buddhist Bhavachakra artwork, with the rooster, snake, and pig, representing greed, ill-will and delusion respectively.[ 4 ][ 5 ]

#### Brief description

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Three_poisons&action=edit&section=1)]
In the [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) teachings, the three poisons (of ignorance, attachment, and aversion) are the primary causes that keep sentient beings trapped in [samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_(Buddhism)). These three poisons are said to be the root of all of the other [kleshas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleshas_(Buddhism)).[ 6 ][ 7 ] The three poisons are represented in the hub of the [wheel of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavacakra) as a pig, a bird, and a snake (representing ignorance, attachment, and aversion, respectively). As shown in the [wheel of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhavacakra) (Sanskrit: *bhavacakra* ), the three poisons lead to the creation of [karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma), which leads to rebirth in the six realms of samsara.[ 1 ][ 8 ][ 9 ]

#### Opposite wholesome qualities

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Three_poisons&action=edit&section=2)]
The three wholesome [mental factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_factors_(Buddhism)) that are identified as the opposites of the three poisons are:[ 10 ][ 11 ]

* amoha (non-delusion) or [paññā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_in_Buddhism) (wisdom)
* [alobha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_in_Buddhism) (non-attachment) or [dāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C4%81na) (generosity)
* [adveṣa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C4%81na) (non-hatred) or [mettā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mett%C4%81) (loving-kindness)
  The [Buddhist path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_Paths_to_liberation) considers these essential for [liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana_(Buddhism)).[ 10 ]

#### Sanskrit/Pali/Tibetan terms and translations

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Three_poisons&action=edit&section=3)]
The three kleshas of ignorance, attachment and aversion are referred to as the *three poisons* (Skt. *triviṣa* ; Tibetan: *dug gsum* ) in the [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana) tradition and as the *three unwholesome roots* (Pāli, *akusala-mūla* ; Skt. *akuśala-mūla* ) in the [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) tradition.
The Sanskrit, Pali, and Tibetan terms for each of the three poisons are as follows:
Poison Sanskrit[ 12 ][ 13 ]Pali Tibetan[ 12 ][ 14 ]Alternate English translations[ 12 ]Skt./Pali/Tib. Synonym[ 15 ]Delusion [moha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha_(Buddhism))moha gti mug confusion, bewilderment, ignorance [avidyā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avidy%C4%81_(Buddhism)) (Skt.); avijjā (Pāli); ma rigpa (Tib.) Attachment [rāga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raga_(Buddhism))lobha 'dod chags desire, sensuality, greed n/a Aversion [dveṣa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dvesha_(Buddhism))dosa zhe sdang anger, hatred, hostility n/a
In the Mahayana tradition moha is identified as a subcategory of avidya. Whereas *avidya* is defined as a fundamental ignorance, *moha* is defined as delusion, confusion and incorrect beliefs. In the Theravada tradition, *moha* and *avidya* are equivalent terms, but they are used in different contexts; *moha* is used when referring to mental factors, and *avidya* is used when referring to the [twelve links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Nidanas).[ 1 ]

#### Meditation and the three poisons

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Three_poisons&action=edit&section=4)]
Meditation is regarded in Buddhism as a primary method for developing a calm and concentrated mind to overcome the three poisons. Through meditative introspection, an individual develops self-awareness and insight into their own mind, which helps reduce internal obstacles. However, effective meditation often requires support such as "moral aptitude" and "good friends" (or teachers).[ 16 ]
