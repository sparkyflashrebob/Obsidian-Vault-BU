# Creative Tactics in your Group (or, getting beyond marches or rallies)

*By Daniel Hunter, Global Trainings Manager of 350 [July 2018]*
A colleague recently asked:
*I am working with some folks around an upcoming event organizing. They have questions about how to move beyond marches and rallies for events. They keep doing those tactics over and over again. Any suggestions on how to help organizers think creatively about their events?*
*It’s a good question: Why don’t groups come up with creative actions all the time? Why do groups get stuck doing the same tactics over and over again?* To be clear: if what they’re doing works every time, keep doing it. But for most groups creativity is important. For one, doing the same tactic usually appeals to only the same people. So if we want to grow our group, we have to do new things. And our targets usually learn to adapt to our actions. They’ll learn how to respond, so repeating action has less effectiveness. If our targets are learning and adapting, shouldn’t we? As children, we are abundantly creative. Watch any 5-year-old play. They have an endless range of abundance, mimicking and adaptation. We lose a lot of that as we get older; but we still have that potential. There’s a couple of things I find that help us get more creative in group meetings: creating playful *encounters, encouraging idea creation outside of formal meeting spaces, reducing judgement in our* groups (at least during some sessions), increasing courage, and (ironically) creating a lot of structure. I’ll say more about each below.
*● Creating playful encounters : Most meetings are boring. As a result, boring things* emerge. The groups that I’ve seen that are the most creative with tactics add some sense of play into their meetings, have raucous debates punctuated with bawdy jokes, or use physical games or activities. Why do these matter? Because creativity is a kind of act of authenticity, where people reveal something unusual or off-beat (“hey, what if we surrounded their house before they left for the meeting?“). Play encourages free expression.
TOOLS: Games , buddies, mock debates, people-sized strategy game , “strategy game” , Macro-strategy Tug of War
*● Encouraging idea creation outside of formal meeting spaces : The best action* designers create actions inspired by historical or previous campaigns. Or they get inspired reading a booklet on different tactics. Or they found it in a dream. Or while walking down the grocery store. Or talking with their mom. (All true stories.) These are all outside of our
meeting spaces. It’s a poor design if our meetings are the only way for generate good ideas. We
shouldn’t do a brainstorm session and then assume we flow right into making a decision. The lesson is separating: spark some ideas in a meeting, get the juices really flowing,
*then cut it off and wait for creativity until the next meeting. Then seek a decision, including* gathering ideas outside the formal space.
*TOOLS: Movement history books (a few personal favorites: Parting the Waters , This is an Uprising , or Strategy & Soul ) or campaign stories (like from the Global Nonviolent Database ) or resources of tactics (like Beautiful Trouble or Beautiful Rising , or the open-access Wiki* Actipedia ) or more tactics in the Online Skill-ups on How Social Movements Win or Art.350.org
*● Reducing judgement in our groups : Lately, every time I brainstorm I notice I start* with a preamble, “I’m gonna come up with some really stupid ideas first…” I’m avoiding *people shutting down an idea too early. Throwing out ideas too quickly is a bad habit many* of our meetings make. Instead, push it, prod it, entertain it. Even entertaining stupid ideas *can reveal other ideas or considerations. And that doesn’t mean getting bogged down in all* ideas — it just means following a creative flow.
TOOLS: Thoughtful facilitation, like how to help a group make decisions or games like Yes, let’s
*● Staying in the large group : Let’s face it, people think differently. Some people* generate hundreds of actions. Others think carefully through each one. While others rush to implement. And so on.
That diversity is good — and some people’s flows invariably gets in the way of other people’s flows – like the logistical implementers immediately critiquing the wild brainstormers.
One of the quickest ways to shut-down a good creative flow is to stay in the large group the whole time. The lesson: use a lot of small groups.
TOOLS: Small group tasks! And Team Types (North, East, South, West) to give language for different methods of creativity.
*● Increasing courage: Another reason we don’t do something new: because it’s safer to* do the old thing. We lack courage to experiment.
In a group I often make this very explicit and talk about “comfort zones.” What we know and our comfortable with is our comfort zones. But to learn something new or for escalated actions, we usually have to act outside of our comfort zone — in our discomfort zone.
Explaining this concept helps some groups take the leap into new actions.
Other groups draw courage from stories. Sometimes it’s inspiring stories of people taking successful risks. Other times, groups may respond to stories of failure by groups who *didn’t adapt or change. We move either because of the carrot (success!) or the stick* (failure!).
And with other groups they may feel paralyzed about taking a risk because their *only yardstick is immediate success. Give groups other ways of measuring moving forward:* getting new media, reaching out to a new group of people, building up our internal skills and learning, and so on.
TOOLS: Expanding Comfort Zones , Story-telling, Theory of Action Logic , How to create News-worthy actions , and Maximise/Minimise Learning
*● Creating a lot of structure: Okay, so this can seem counterintuitive. Some people* think that creativity is stimulated by open-endedness. But, actually, creativity needs boundaries and structure!
Far from stopping creativity, limitations in creativity help focus the mind. In a group meeting, those limitations may look like: “come up with 3 tactics in the next 10 minutes that we haven’t done…” or “come up with an action that involves our target but at a location we have never done before.”
To inspire us, my group created a rule: “no marches, and no rallies.” After we got good at that, we later escalated it to “no action that we’ve ever seen/heard anyone else do before.” How about that for inspiring creativity?
TOOLS: action accordion , Moving your Allies , Finding Tactics that Matter — perhaps my favorite right now is a new tool colleagues Katey and Andrew created called Action
MadLibs [1]
By now you may be thinking: geez, my group can’t do all these tools! And that’s right — or at *least not all at once. And there’s good news! You don’t have to do them all. The role of a good facilitator is to read the group. Determine which issues are most in their way. Lack of courage? Not enough structure? Staying in the large group too much? Pick a tool from that section. If you think you have more than one issue (like most groups),* pick one to work on for your next meeting and get your group in better shape. The process of moving a group forward is iterative: so find a tool, experiment, and try it out! Good luck!
[1] In case you’re wondering: Why aren’t more of these tools on the 350 Trainings Website? Many of them are very Western. i.e.
MadLibs and Action Accordion are both rooted in Western games and we’ve never tested them extensively to see if they work *outside North America/Western Europe. If you do, let us know the results!*
