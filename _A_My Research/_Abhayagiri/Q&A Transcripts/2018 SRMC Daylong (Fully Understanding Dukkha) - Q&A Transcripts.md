## Detailed Transcripts: Morning Q&A Session

### Question 1 [01:31:41]: Strategies for Detachment & The Dalai Lama's "Lion's Roar"
* **Questioner:** Female Meditator.
* **Question:** *"Can you speak to different strategies for learning detachment?"*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  *"The Noble Eightfold Path! Human beings naturally want a quick fix. When I was an anagarika driving Ajahn Amaro and Ajahn Pasanno to UCLA to attend a teaching by His Holiness the Dalai Lama, an attendee asked if there was a path that is a bit easier or faster to end dukkha. The auditorium went dead silent. The Dalai Lama paused for two minutes, tears rolling down his cheeks, and then delivered a lion's roar: 'There is no fast! There is no easy! If there were, don't you think I would be teaching you that?!' This is a path of long-term commitment. Detachment begins with khanti (patience)—the willingness to coexist with the unpleasant without immediately reacting."*

### Question 6 [01:58:28]: Caregiver Burnout & Holding Space Without Fixing
* **Questioner:** Female Meditator (Caregiver).
* **Question:** *"For those of us who are caregivers dealing with suffering in others: what are ways to practice with ourselves while offering compassionate action to someone who cannot lift themselves out of delusion?"*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  *"Caregivers often carry an unconscious demand that their care must cure the other person. When the person doesn't improve, the caregiver experiences vicarious guilt and burnout. The most powerful gift a caregiver can offer is a non-judgmental presence—a safe zone. When we meet someone with deep listening and equanimity, we act as a clear mirror. And caregivers must know their limits. Stepping back to rest, meditate, and replenish is wisdom, not selfishness."*

### Question 7 [02:04:54]: Loving-Kindness & The Salt Crystal (*AN 3.99*)
* **Questioner:** Male Meditator.
* **Question:** *"Where do loving-kindness and compassion fit into the framework of understanding dukkha?"*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  *"Without self-compassion, contemplating dukkha breeds depression and aversion. In the Lonaphala Sutta (AN 3.99), the Buddha says that a lump of salt dropped into a small cup makes the water undrinkable. But that exact same lump of salt dropped into the Ganges River is diluted by the vast volume of water. When awareness is vast, patient, and full of mettā, painful conditions do not overwhelm the heart."*

## Detailed Transcripts: Afternoon Q&A Highlighted Dialogue

### Question 17 [07:21:16]: Global Suffering, News & Burnout (Woman 1)
* **Questioner:** Woman 1.
* **Question:**
  > *"So, thank you so much. My question is regarding to the suffering that is in the world that we see every day, that human flow, wars, families separated from parents that is happening every day. Every time you listen to the news—that I like to do it, I like to know what's going on. But also it's a fact because it's suffering of other people, or you go to San Francisco and you see people on the street, living on the street.  
  > So my question is: still knowing what's going on in the world, how can we be in a balance? Because I don't want to be numb about what's going on; I don't want to look the other way, otherwise I'm not going to be human anymore. How can we deal with everyday suffering in the world?"*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  > *"That's a big one. And you have to find your own balance with that. If it's driving you into a despair that causes confusion and unremitting angst, then you're picking it up and holding on to it in a way that you don't need to.  
  > You can still be present for the suffering of other people as long as you can feel comfortable with the pain that it causes inside... Part of developing real compassion (*karuṇā*) is developing equanimity (*upekkhā*). Understanding that the potential for human suffering in the world is vast. It's painful to see, and we can't close our eyes to it.  
  > And we choose to act in ways that we have the capacity for, but you need to have the wisdom to know what your capacity is. If you keep stretching yourself beyond your learned capacity, you're going to burn out and you won't be any good to anybody. When it's time, step back, take a break, refresh, take refuge in the skills of release, let that refresh your mind before you jump back in. You don't have to feel guilty about having limits."*

### Question 18 [07:28:18]: Embodied Suffering, Chronic Pain & Trauma (Woman 2)
* **Questioner:** Woman 2.
* **Question:**
  > *"You've talked a couple times about returning to the body to escape from suffering in the mind. I'm wondering if you can talk about embodied suffering, like responses to trauma and chronic pain embedding in the body. I live in chronic pain and there are physical thought forms that crop up. My knee hurt last week, now it hurts again. I think my knee is holding onto the memory of being in pain, but there's no mental thought process... It's like my knee is remembering in my knee."*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  > *"The mind and body are closely connected, but to me, trying to figure out if the knee is remembering has a flavor of proliferation (*papañca*)—trying to make conceptual sense out of an experience.  
  > And my tendency would be to say, well, **just experience it**. Don't try and figure it out on a conceptual level. Just: if there's a pain in the knee, there's a pain in the knee. If there's a thought, there's a thought. If there's a thought of the pain, there's a thought of the pain.  
  > It's not to try and figure out what is related to what, but just to know it as an experience and watch it. See if it causes dukkha. Don't hold onto it. See what happens when you let it go. Trying to figure it out is just adding another layer of proliferation that you don't need to do."*

### Question 19 [07:33:13]: Sense of Self, Narrative Therapy & Not-Self as Deconstruction (Andy)
* **Questioner:** Andy (in the back).
* **Question:**
  > *"I'm hoping you'll enjoy going back to your last teaching today about sense of self. Really interested to hear you talk more. So there's actually like narrative therapy, where you're using story to help people make meaning of themselves in their life. There's also personal branding with career development.  
  > And yet you had this tantalizing comment, which I agree with: how much your sense of self is a source of suffering.  
  > So I was wondering: **what is the alternative to these narratives that are trying to build up a sense of self? What is your story for no self?**"*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  > *"Oh boy. **It's a story of not making stories.**"*
* **Andy:**
  > *"Can you talk about maybe the benefits that people experience before and after having no self?"*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  > *"No self—not-self (*anattā*)—isn't something you have or you don't have. And it's not a statement of metaphysics. It's not a philosophy or something we have to achieve or attain.  
  > **It's a contemplative strategy.**  
  > In whatever we're looking at in terms of our experience of the five khandhas, we apply a contemplative strategy of looking clearly at this body and asking ourselves:  
  > * Does it have the qualities of what I would consider to be self?  
  > * If it were self, would it cause me affliction and pain?  
  > * Wouldn't I be able to say, 'let it not be like this,' or 'make it like this'?  
  > That would be the quality of self or control that I would say should be present if this body were myself."*
* **Andy:**
  > *"You're talking about basically disidentifying from the perceived source of our suffering, whether that's outside of us or just the fact that we have an identity."*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  > *"**Correct.** We examine whatever it is that we think we might be, or that we hold on to as a self or a conception of self.  
  > We look at our body; we examine it thoroughly. We ask: *'Can I regard this as myself? Can I regard something that's always changing, really not under my control, unpredictable—can I regard that as self?'* And if I'm looking clearly, the answer is no.  
  > I take that same contemplation to feelings, perceptions, thinking, and consciousness. Form is not self, feeling is not self, perceptions are not self, mental formations are not self, consciousness is not self.  
  > And then I leave it there. I don't create any more narrative. I don't create a story. I just experience what that is like."*
* **Andy:**
  > *"**So it's like a process of subtraction?**"*
* **Ajahn Karunadhammo:**
  > *"**Deconstruction.**  
  > So on that note, let's deconstruct this day. We've come together, we've constructed a nice environment for seven or eight hours... And for just one or two minutes, let's just sit down quietly, close our eyes, deconstruct the day."*
