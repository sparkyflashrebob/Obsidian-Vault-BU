##### Native Children: Kinship with Nature

Native American traditions emphasize a deep and inherent connection between children and the natural world, fostering a holistic understanding of their place within creation.
Here's how children relate to nature, animals, trees, and the sky:

* **Connection to Nature as a Foundation for Learning and Being**
  + From the earliest moments of life, Native American children are raised with the belief that they are an integral part of nature, not separate from it. This spiritual influence is considered supremely important for the unborn and young child, instilling a love for the Great Mystery and a sense of connectedness with all creation.
  + Children are taught that true politeness is demonstrated through actions, not just words, and are guided to interact with their environment and elders respectfully. They learn to sit or lie on the ground, becoming conscious of the multitude of life forms around them, recognizing that all things possess personality.
  + The world is seen as a **"library"** where stones, leaves, grass, brooks, birds, and animals are "books" that teach lessons about life and beauty. Children learn to appreciate natural phenomena, adjusting to storms, winds, and frosts without complaint, understanding that these are expressions of the Great Mystery. This continuous observation of nature helps children develop interest, wonder, and admiration, enriching their existence and making life vivid and pulsing.
  + The Lakota people, for example, literally loved the soil, sitting or reclining on the ground with a feeling of being close to a **"mothering power."** Old people liked to walk barefoot on the **"sacred earth,"** and their tipis and altars were made of earth, reflecting the Earth as the final abiding place for all living and growing things. This connection to the earth helps one think more deeply, feel more keenly, and see clearly into life's mysteries, fostering kinship with other lives.
* **Relating to Animals**
  + Children are raised to understand that all creatures are kin. In traditional belief systems, the Bear is seen as a grandfather, Rattlesnake an aunt, Beaver a cousin, Eagle an uncle, Deer a sister, and Buffalo a brother. These animals are not only relatives but also teachers, protectors, guardians, supernatural aids, and sources of power and knowledge.
  + Children study the habits of animals as closely as others study books. They are taught to communicate with animals through praying, talking, singing, dancing, meditating, touching, smelling, and offering gifts like tobacco or food.
  + Stories are integral to this learning. For instance, the "Creation of the Animal People" myth explains that early humans were sometimes half-animal, bird, or fish, possessing special powers and communicating telepathically. Animals, birds, reptiles, and fish shared their knowledge and power with humans, and these creatures are to be respected and cared for, lest their spirits turn against people if abused.
  + Specific animal encounters are seen as messages. For example, the presence of a Lizard in the house near a baby is a sign of protection, warning adults if the baby is sick or in danger, and traditionally, boys are taught never to kill a Lizard. Hummingbirds zooming into a classroom or a King Snake crawling into a lunch bucket, as experienced by the author, are examples of these early encounters that develop a child's awareness of nature's communication.
* **Trees and Plants**
  + Trees are considered living, communicating entities. The Great Spirit's life force flows through plants and trees, considering them kindred.
  + Children learn from the stories of trees and their significance, such as George Copway's statement that "Nature's wide domain" with "giant arms of the forest trees" is a greater birthplace than palaces. The act of cutting grass or digging for stones is seen as harming "Mother's hair" or "bones," reflecting a deep spiritual reverence for the land. Tatanga Mani (Walking Buffalo) also states that trees talk to each other and to humans if one listens.
* **Mountains and the Sky**
  + Natural phenomena like mountains and celestial events are considered sacred and imbued with spiritual significance. The Great Spirit is seen as overseeing all things.
  + The blue heavens are seen as covering children, and Chief Seattle's saying "All things are connected. Whatever befalls the earth befalls the children of the earth" underscores the interconnectedness with the land.
  + Children are taught to "listen to the silver voice of the aspen, or the clashing cymbals of the birch; and at night she points to the heavenly blazed trail through nature’s galaxy of splendor to nature’s God. Silence, love, reverence—this is the trinity of first lessons".
  + Mountains and other landforms are finished beauty for the Lakota, filling the world with knowledge. Bear Butte, for instance, is described as a sacred mountain used for vision quests and council meetings, a liminal space between realms where the divine can be perceived. Children may learn about these sacred places and their significance through stories and teachings.
    This deep engagement with nature from childhood ensures that Native American people often maintain a strong connection to their environment, seeing it not merely as a resource, but as a living, sacred relative.
