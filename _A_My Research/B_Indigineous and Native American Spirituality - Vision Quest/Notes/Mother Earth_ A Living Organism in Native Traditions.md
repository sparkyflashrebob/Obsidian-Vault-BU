In Native American traditions, the Earth is profoundly understood and revered as a **living, breathing organism**, often personified as **Mother Earth** or **Grandmother Earth** [1-8]. This perspective is central to indigenous spirituality, which views humans not as separate from nature, but as an integral part of it [9, 10].

Here's how this concept is elaborated in the sources:

* **Earth as a Feminine, Nurturing Being:**

  + The Earth is literally perceived as a **woman**, a **mothering power** that nurtures all life [1, 4, 8]. The Lakota people, for instance, felt a deep connection to the soil, sitting or reclining on the ground as if close to a "mothering power" [1, 11]. Walking barefoot on the "sacred earth" was seen as good for the skin, and their tipis and altars were made of earth, reflecting it as the "final abiding place of all things that lived and grew" [1, 2].
  + Chief Luther Standing Bear emphasizes that this connection helps one "think more deeply and to feel more keenly; he can see more clearly into the mysteries of life and come closer in kinship to other lives about him" [2, 11, 12].
  + Wovoka, a Paiute leader, expressed this deep reverence by metaphorically questioning the desecration of the land: "Shall I take a knife and tear my mother’s bosom?" when asked to plow, or "how dare I cut my mother’s hair?" when asked to cut grass [13].
* **Earth's Physiology and Sentience:**

  + The "Creation of the Animal People" myth explicitly states that the Earth was once a human being, a woman, made by the Great Creator [4]. In this myth, **the soil is her flesh, the rocks are her bones, the wind is her breath, and the trees, grass, rivers, and streams are her veins** [4, 7].
  + Doug Good Feather elaborates on this, describing the **jungles and forests as the lungs** of Grandmother Earth, the **wind as her breath**, and the **creeks, rivers, and oceans as her vascular system**, with the **water being her blood** [7]. He directly states that "the earth has its own physiology" [7].
  + The Earth's sentience is also highlighted, as she will let humans know if they "should do her any wrong" through phenomena like earthquakes, floods, fire, and heat from volcanoes, indicating she can get "mad or sick" [4]. Water itself is also believed to express sentient qualities, responding to positive and negative energy [14].
* **Interconnectedness and Reciprocal Relationship:**

  + Native American spirituality emphasizes that "humans are seen as an integral part of nature, not separate from it" [9]. This belief fosters a "direct kinship with all of Creation" [15]. The Lakota phrase "Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ" translates to "we are all related" and "all is connected," encompassing not just humans but "the two-leggeds, the four-leggeds, the swimmers, the crawlers, the wingeds, the tree people and all those who grow roots, the stone and mineral people, the elementals... everything" [16-18].
  + This deep connection implies a **reciprocal relationship** [15, 19, 20]. The sources state that "we cannot survive or live without our 'relations.' We also realize that they cannot live without us" [15]. This means humans have a responsibility to "replenish or giving something back in return" to avoid breaking the "sacred circle" [19].
  + "Power centers" on Earth are compared to "psychic centers" or "endocrine glands" in the human body, serving as "chakras" or "vital organs" [21, 22]. Interfering with these centers causes a "serious imbalance" and "negative and detrimental reaction," as the Earth, like a living organism, needs recharging [20, 23, 24].
* **Consequences of Disrespect and the Need for Healing:**

  + Treating the Earth as a mere resource rather than a living being leads to sickness and suffering [6, 24, 25]. "When we get out of balance with Nature and the Earth we become sick," and if the Mother Earth "dies, we all die" [24, 25].
  + The belief that "man’s heart, away from nature, becomes hard" and "lack of respect for growing, living things soon led to lack of respect for humans, too" highlights the moral implications of this worldview [26].
  + The degradation of the environment, through pollution, exploitation, and disrespect, is seen as directly linked to a loss of spiritual knowledge and balance [24, 25, 27].
  + Healing the Earth requires healing ourselves and transforming our relationship with it, as "all change begins with the self" [28-30]. This is emphasized through practices like the **Earth-Healing Ceremony**, which aims to bond with the Earth and make restitution for harm [31-33].

In essence, thinking of the Earth as a living and breathing organism instills a profound sense of kinship, responsibility, and reverence in Native American cultures, shaping their entire worldview and guiding their interactions with the natural world [9, 10, 34, 35].
