Native American spirituality is incredibly diverse, encompassing a vast array of beliefs and practices that vary significantly among the hundreds of distinct tribes and nations across North America [1]. There isn't one single "Native American religion," but rather a spectrum of traditions deeply rooted in the history, land, and culture of each community [1]. However, several common themes and characteristics often appear across these traditions [1].

**Core Principles and Themes of Native American Wisdom and Spirituality:**

* **Connection to Nature and Animism**

  + A **profound reverence for the natural world is central**, viewing humans as an integral part of nature, not separate from it [1, 2]. Natural phenomena like mountains, rivers, animals, and celestial events are often considered sacred and imbued with spiritual significance [1]. Environmental stewardship is seen as a spiritual responsibility to maintain balance and harmony [1].
  + Many traditions hold **animistic beliefs**, meaning that spirits or life forces reside in all things – animals, plants, rocks, bodies of water, and even inanimate objects [3-5]. This also extends to the belief that the Earth itself is a living and breathing organism [6, 7].
  + The Great Creator placed all things on Earth, making everything a source of "power" to be revered [4]. Native American people believe every living thing in nature has its own spirit and is connected to the Great Spirit [4].
* **Belief in a Great Spirit/Creator**

  + Many tribes believe in an all-powerful, all-knowing Creator or "Great Spirit" who oversees all things [8]. This Creator has many names, including God, Jehovah, Allah, Yahweh, the Great Mystery, or the Great Invisible One [9-11].
  + The Great Spirit's work is seen in almost everything: Sun, Moon, Stars, Trees, Wind, and Mountains [12]. The Creator also provided humanity with the elemental gifts of fire, water, air, and earth to balance their egos [13].
  + The Great Mystery is a neutral force that places no judgment or punishment, but because humans fear the unknown, they label experiences as "good" or "bad" [14].
* **Holistic View of Health and Well-being**

  + Health is understood as a **harmony of body, mind, and spirit**, as well as harmony with others and with one's environment [3]. Illness can be seen as an imbalance in these relationships [3].
  + The Lakota recognized a **great unifying life force from Wakan Tanka** (the Great Spirit) that flowed through all things, making all things kindred and brought together by the same Great Mystery [15, 16]. This kinship with all creatures of the earth, sky, and water was a real and active principle [15].
* **Generosity and Reciprocity**

  + **Giving thanks and giving back are central** to many traditions, reflecting the generosity of the Creator and the natural world [17].
  + There is a **law of reciprocity** in Nature, meaning humans cannot keep taking without replenishing or giving something back in return, otherwise the sacred circle is broken [18].
  + The indigenous principle of **"Chop three, take two"** at Standing Rock demonstrated that there was always enough when generosity and reciprocity were practiced [19].
* **The Sacred Hoop of Life and Interconnectedness (Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ)**

  + The Sacred Hoop of Life is a spiritual way of life where all life is understood as a **continuous journey through doorways in a sacred spiraling movement** [20].
  + This concept highlights that **all beings and all things, both seen and unseen, are interconnected and intrinsically related** (Mitákuye Oyás’iŋ – "we are all related" and "all is connected") [21]. This includes "the two-leggeds, the four-leggeds, the swimmers, the crawlers, the wingeds, the tree people and all those who grow roots, the stone and mineral people, the elementals...everyone" [22].
  + Every action creates a cascading series of reactions that go far beyond human perception [23, 24]. Kindness is the spiritual default of those who walk this path [22].
* **Everyday is Sacred**

  + For Native Americans, **all days are God's days**, not just one day in seven [25]. Spiritual wisdom is part of the fabric of daily life and experience [26].
  + The drum represents the whole world and connects one with the sacred, linking to the tones of rocks, water, air, wind, and rain [27]. Drumming connects individuals to their bones, heartbeat, and the Earth Mother, helping to cleanse negative spiritual energy, focus meditation, dissolve negative emotions, and call upon spirits [28]. The drum is the heart of human intelligence and the heart's connection to the Creator [29, 30].
  + "Every day is sacred, not just one day of the week" [27].

**Transmission of Wisdom:**

* **Oral Tradition:** Spiritual beliefs and practices are primarily passed down through **oral traditions, including myths, stories, songs, and allegories**, rather than through written holy books [3, 26, 31, 32]. The "Grandfather stories" serve a multifaceted purpose, often tribal and geographically specific [33].
* **Role of Elders:** Elders are highly respected as **storehouses of rich knowledge, understanding, and traditional wisdom** [34, 35]. They teach by telling stories, relating parables, and sharing ancient myths [36]. Native youth are taught to respect adults and the aged, avoiding interruption and direct address [37].
* **Learning from Nature (The World as a Library):**

  + **Knowledge was inherent in all things; the world was a library, and its books were the stones, leaves, grass, brooks, and the birds and animals** [32, 38].
  + Native people were **close students of nature**, studying animal habits [39]. Observation leads to appreciation that life is more than human manifestation, enriching existence [40].
  + Nature communicates through **omens, signs, and mythic-religious symbols**, forming a definite system of knowledge [41-43]. This language can be learned by paying attention to the behavior of creatures and natural phenomena [44-46].
  + Animals and birds are considered "people," teachers, protectors, guardians, supernatural aids, and sources of power and knowledge [47].
  + Developing symbolic thinking involves studying myths, observing nature, and connecting with the subconscious [48-50].
* **Importance of Community and Ceremony**

  + Many spiritual practices are communal, involving elaborate ceremonies, dances, feasts, and rituals for various occasions [8, 51].
  + **Sweat lodges** are used for physical, emotional, and spiritual cleansing and purification [51-53]. Entering the sweat lodge on hands and knees symbolizes humility and rebirth [54].
  + **Vision quests** are a significant personal practice involving solitude, fasting, and prayer to seek spiritual guidance or connection with the spirit world [8, 55, 56]. These quests are a ceremony to celebrate a life transition, deepen understanding, connect with the spirit, and find one's individual path [57].
  + **Pipe ceremonies** involve smoking sacred tobacco and other herbs to create a pathway to the spirit world [51].
  + **Smudging**, the burning of sacred herbs like sage, cedar, or sweetgrass, is used for purification and cleansing [51, 52]. It wards off bad spirits and negative energies, while sweetgrass attracts good forces [58].
  + **Medicine wheels** are sacred symbols representing the interconnectedness of all things, often used for healing and teaching [17, 59]. They symbolize unity, strength, protection, and infinity [60].
  + **Drums** are used in ceremonies to connect with the sacred, focus meditation, dissolve negative emotions, and draw spiritual qualities into life [28].
  + **Spirit Food (waéglepi)** is an ancient way of showing reverence to ancestors by offering a small portion of food and drink, keeping a channel of communication open with them [61, 62].
  + **Medicine Walks** are indigenous practices for spiritual communion with Mother Earth, seeking signs, omens, and signals from the Creator through nature [63]. This practice enhances intuition, psychic senses, lucid dreaming, and deflects negativity [64].

**Key Virtues and Values:**

* **Silence:** Profoundly believed in as a sign of perfect equilibrium and a cornerstone of character [65, 66]. It is a mark of respect and allows for deeper thought [67, 68]. Expectant mothers practice silence and isolation to instill love of the Great Mystery in the unborn child [69].
* **Honesty and Integrity:** Honesty is an internal moral value, while integrity represents actions [70]. Consistently acting with integrity builds admiration and trust [71].
* **Humility and Humbleness:** Humility is thinking of oneself less, while humbleness involves outward practice [72]. Entering the sweat lodge on hands and knees is an intentional act of humbling oneself before Mother Earth [54].
* **Respect and Honor:** Respect is an internal feeling earned through admiration, while honor is an external action showing that respect and love [73, 74]. Reverence is showing the spirit world acknowledgement and comes from all virtues [75].
* **Compassion and Empathy:** Compassion is feeling another's feelings, and empathy is taking action to change their experience [76]. "Your liberation is bound up with mine" [77]. Through compassion, a psychic pairing with other beings is created [78].
* **Joy, Love, Harmony, and Wisdom:** These are the four primal expressions of spirit, central to discovering oneself and engaging life [79]. Harmony is a state of awareness and balance, not perfection [80]. Wisdom is experience in action, often cultivated through listening to nature and elders [81, 82].

**Distinction from Western Views and Impact of Colonization:**

* **Land Ownership:** Native Americans share a belief that **the earth is a spiritual presence that must be honored, not mastered** [83]. They did not believe in selling land, viewing it as belonging to the Great Spirit for all his children [84-87]. This contrasted with Western Europeans who saw the land as savage and to be tamed and used as they saw fit [83].
* **Education:** Native peoples had different conceptions of education, prioritizing practical skills for living in the woods over sciences taught in colleges [88, 89].
* **Critique of "Civilization":** Western civilization is seen as having gotten "out of balance with our earth" [90]. It has caused destruction of forests and animal life, leading to a hard heart and lack of respect for living things and humans [85, 91]. Cities are painful to the red man's eyes, lacking quiet places and clean air, and the clatter insults the ears [92]. "Civilization" has been thrust upon Native peoples, but it has not added to their sense of justice, reverence for life, love for truth, honesty, generosity, or faith in the Great Mystery [93, 94].
* **Misunderstanding of Spirituality:** Europeans called Native Americans "pagans, heathens, and savages" for praying to elements of nature, not understanding their worship of the Great Spirit through Creation [9, 12]. Native people do not want churches because they teach quarreling about God [95].
* **Cultural Appropriation vs. Appreciation:** The sources emphasize the importance of appreciating Native American culture and universal teachings without appropriating them [96-98]. Stealing elements of culture is insulting and dishonoring [96].
* **Sacred Sites vs. Economic Value:** There is a conflict over the exploitation of sacred power centers for economic gain (minerals, timber, gas, water) versus their spiritual and aesthetic value [99-101]. Violation of power centers causes serious imbalance, leading to negative outcomes like weather patterns, volcanic eruptions, and diseases [102, 103].

**The "Think Indigenous" Concept:**

"Think Indigenous" is a philosophy of traditional indigenous life that aims to **raise the vibration of love on Mother Earth** [104]. It is a nature-based way of living in alignment with Mother Earth and the spiritual laws of the universe [105]. This philosophy helps individuals reconnect with their ancestral spiritual knowledge, find balance, live in congruence with the environment, and enhance intuition and psychic abilities [106]. It is **not a religion** and can be practiced alongside any other religious belief [105].

The "Think Indigenous" approach includes a **Threefold Path** [107]:

1. **The Way of the Seven Generations (Conscious Living):** Making decisions with deep consideration for the ancestral past and the future lives of unborn relatives seven generations from today [107-109]. It emphasizes learning from past mistakes and successes [109].
2. **The Way of the Buffalo (Mindful Consumption):** Practicing respect and honor for all beings on Mother Earth, taking only what is needed, and using everything taken [108, 110, 111]. This involves intentional and deliberate stewardship and moving from a linear "produce-profit" mindset to a circular mindset that considers the entire life cycle of goods and the impact on the Earth [112-114].
3. **The Way of the Village (Collective Impact):** Finding and being part of a like-minded community, elevating planetary consciousness by working, playing, and praying together [110, 115]. It emphasizes self-reliance within the community and supporting one another [116].

Overall, Native American wisdom and spirituality, as presented in these sources, offer a profound and holistic worldview deeply intertwined with the natural world, emphasizing interconnectedness, reciprocity, respect, and a continuous learning process from both elders and nature.
