[ LAND PERMISSION PRAYER ]
"Great Mystery, Creator, and Grandfather/Grandmother Earth,
We gather at the foot of Granite Mountain in the Mono Basin.
We humbly acknowledge that we stand upon the ancestral, unceded lands of the
Kootzaduka’a (Mono Lake Northern Paiute), Nüümü, and Newe peoples,
who have loved, prayed upon, and stewarded these granite ridges, alkali waters,
and pinyon groves since time immemorial.

We honor the language of this land and the Kootzaduka’a people, whose very name
carries their sacred relationship to kootzabe—the alkali fly pupae of Mono Lake.
We commit to pronouncing their name with care and reverence, setting aside
English defaults and recognizing that this land spoke Kootzaduka’a thousands
of years before English ever touched this basin.

To the spirits of Granite Mountain, the ancient pinyons, the sagebrush,
and the waters of Mono Lake—we ask for your permission to walk gently.
We ask that you hold these seekers in their solitude and fasting.
We promise to leave no trace, honor your balance, and carry the medicine
of our renewal back to a world in need.
Aho / Amen / Mitákuye Oyás'iŋ."

[ SPOKEN OPENING SCRIPT FOR COUNCIL 1 ]
"Welcome back, relatives, to Granite Mountain.
For over twenty-five years, our evolving tribe has returned to these pinyon groves and granite ridges, bringing our quiet presence, our prayers, and our lives back to this soil.
Before we pass the talking piece, we begin by formally acknowledging the ancestral stewards of this watershed—the Kootzaduka’a (Mono Lake Northern Paiute), Nüümü, and Newe peoples, who have loved and prayed on this land since time immemorial. We speak the name Kootzaduka’a with intentional care today, honoring their language and their deep identity with kootzabe—the alkali fly pupae of Mono Lake. We recognize that their language echoed off these canyon walls thousands of years before English ever touched this basin, and we ask permission of this mountain, these ancient rocks, and the spirits of this place to walk gently as guests.

Today, we enter the stage of Severance—the sacred process of stepping away from daily routines, modern speed, and the busy roles we carry back in the valley.
Our council today rests on four universal intentions: to speak from the heart, to listen from the heart without judgment, to be lean in our expression, and to allow our words to arise spontaneously without rehearsing.

This circle is an absolute sanctuary.
For each of us walking a path of recovery, well-being, or deep inner cultivation, know that this mountain is a space of pure rest and dignity. You do not need to fix, fight, or analyze anything here. Allow yourself to drop gently into your body—feeling the solid, unshakeable granite beneath you, the warmth of the high-desert sun on your skin, and the wind moving through the pines.

For those of us sitting with deep grief—holding the memory and love of a partner or dear one who has passed—the mountain is vast enough to hold the full depth of your love and sorrow without judgment or haste. On this land, you can create a quiet space or Death Lodge to speak the words of completion: 'I forgive you,' 'Please forgive me,' 'Thank you,' 'I love you,' and 'Goodbye'.

And as we sit here in circle, we hold court for those we know and love who are experiencing aging, frailty, or ill-health. We hold their well-being, comfort, and peace at the center of our thoughts as we do our work on the land. As we sit in silence, we dedicate our practice and send unconditional loving-kindness and compassion to those in need.

Whether you pray to God, honor Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka—the Great Spirit—or rest in the quiet, unconditioned awareness of mindfulness and Theravada practice, know that we are all drinking from the exact same waterhole. The land does not demand a single doctrine; it only asks for our presence.

Let us take one more collective breath.
As we sit here in circle, we might consider what brings us to this mountain today, and what intent we wish to hold in our hearts as we step toward the threshold."

[ ORAL TEACHING: THE SACRED HOOP OF THE FOUR DIRECTIONS ]
(from Doug Good Feather, Think Indigenous)
- East (Wiyóhpiyata) — Fire & New Beginnings: Morning light, awakening vision, gratitude, generosity.
- South (Itókagata) — Air & The Breath of Life: Summer vitality, innocence, mental clarity, honest integrity.
- West (Wiyóȟpeyata) — Water & Healing Powers: Sunset, emotional depths, humility, surrender, releasing old grief.
- North (Wazíyata) — Earth Medicine & Fortitude: Winter stillness, deep roots of ancestors, compassion, endurance, physical grounding.
- Above (Waŋkátakab) & Below (Khúta): Great Mystery above, Mother Earth below, uniting within the Center of Life (Čhaŋté / Heart).

[ STORY TO READ: THE MUDDY ROAD — LEAVING THE BURDEN BEHIND ]
Two monks, Tanzan and Ekido, were walking along a rain-drenched country road. The mud was deep and thick. At an impassable crossroads stood a young woman in an exquisite silk kimono, unable to cross without ruining her clothes.

Without a word or hesitation, Tanzan scooped the woman up into his arms, carried her across the deep mud, set her down gently on dry ground, and walked on.

Ekido said nothing, but inwardly he seethed. Hours passed in stony silence. Night fell, and when they finally arrived at the temple lodging, Ekido could bear it no longer:
"We monks are forbidden to touch women, let alone pick them up and hold them! Why did you do that? It is against our sacred vows!"

Tanzan looked at his companion with calm compassion and smiled:
"I left the girl on the other side of the road hours ago. Why are you still carrying her?"

Quest Medicine: On the mountain, we discover how much baggage—resentments, identities, rules, grievances—we have carried up the hill. The wilderness invites us to set down what ended long ago.

[ REFLECTION TO READ: EACH LIFE, ITS OWN MOUNTAIN AND RIVER ]
Every life is its own vast mountain and sea. We are born into different fires, raised under different skies. The storms we’ve witnessed vary, and the warmth and cold we carry in our hearts are never the same.

Some are surrounded by flowers from birth — gentle and open, never knowing the bite of frost. Others grow up dancing with the wind and rain — learning to hold their own umbrella, quietly hiding their scars.

Between travelers lie gaps of circumstance, trial, and perspective. Blooming has its brilliance; wilderness has its own quiet peace. Passion is one way to live — stillness is another worthy path.

You have your flower-strewn path. I have my thorny trail.
We need not look up to each other, nor look down.
We simply walk our own journeys and allow the other to do the same.

With kindness in our words, clarity in our sight, acceptance of differences, and compassion for every circumstance — this is the finest practice for the rest of our lives.

[ PARABLE TO READ: THE TIGERS AND THE WILD STRAWBERRY ]
A seeker was walking alone through the wild forest when he heard the low, earth-shaking growl of a tiger behind him. He ran harder than he had ever run in his life until the ground abruptly ended at the lip of a precipitous cliff.

With no choice, he caught hold of a wild vine swinging over the abyss and lowered himself over the precipice. Dangling in midair, he looked down. Waiting at the bottom of the canyon rocks was a second hungry tiger, gazing upward in patient certainty. Above him, the first tiger paced the cliff edge.

Suspended between two deaths, the man looked closely at the vine that held him. From a cleft in the rock emerged two mice—one white, one black. Little by little, in steady alternation—day and night—they began to gnaw at the fibrous stem. He heard the fibers snapping one by one.

In that moment of absolute stillness, where there was nowhere left to run, no future to plan, and no past to regret, he noticed something growing from a crevice within arm's reach: a single wild strawberry, crimson, glistening in the sunlight, completely alive.

Reaching out with one free hand, he plucked the berry and placed it on his tongue.

It was the sweetest thing he had ever tasted in his entire life.

Not because the berry possessed magical power, but because for the first time in his life, he was unreservedly, completely present. The tigers of mortality are always there—yesterday, today, and tomorrow. The only question the quest asks is: Are you present enough to taste the strawberry?

[ POEM TO READ: OUR DEEPEST LIGHT ]
(by Marianne Williamson)
"Our deepest fear is not that we are inadequate.
Our deepest fear is that we are powerful beyond measure.
It is our light, not our darkness, that most frightens us.

We ask ourselves, who am I to be brilliant, gorgeous, talented, fabulous?
Actually, who are you not to be?
You are a child of God. Your playing small does not serve the world.
There is nothing enlightened about shrinking so that other people won't feel insecure around you.

We were born to make manifest the glory of God that is within us.
It is not just in some of us; it is in everyone.
And as we let our own light shine,
we unconsciously give other people permission to do the same.
As we are liberated from our own fear,
our presence automatically liberates others."

[ POEM TO READ: WHEN DEATH COMES ]
(by Mary Oliver)
When death comes
like the hungry bear in autumn;
when death comes and takes all the bright coins from his purse
to buy me, and snaps the purse shut;
when death comes
like the measle-pox;
when death comes
like an iceberg between the shoulder blades,

I want to step through the door full of curiosity, wondering:
what is it going to be like, that cottage of darkness?

And therefore I look upon everything
as a brotherhood and a sisterhood,
and I look upon time as no more than an idea,
and I consider eternity as another possibility,

and I think of each life as a flower, as common
as a field daisy, and as singular,

and each name a comfortable music in the mouth,
tending, as all music does, toward silence,

and each body a lion of courage, and something
precious to the earth.

When it's over, I want to say all my life
I was a bride married to amazement.
I was the bridegroom, taking the world into my arms.

When it's over, I don't want to wonder
if I have made of my life something particular, and real.

I don't want to find myself sighing and frightened,
or full of argument.

I don't want to end up simply having visited this world.
