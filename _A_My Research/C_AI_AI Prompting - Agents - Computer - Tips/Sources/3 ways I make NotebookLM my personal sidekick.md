  NotebookLM is marketed as a [research and notes companion](https://www.xda-developers.com/turned-notebooklm-into-research-lab/), but honestly, I don’t quite use it the way Google had intended. The “official” pitch is that you upload documents, and it helps you pull insights and keep track of sources. This is undoubtedly useful for things like academic work, but it can also get boring if that’s all you use it for.
  After [pairing NotebookLM with my top design tool](https://www.xda-developers.com/paired-notebooklm-with-free-design-tool/) and also [using it to level up my writing skills](https://www.xda-developers.com/use-notebooklm-to-be-better-writer/), I realized that it’s far more than just a research and learning tool. Where it actually shines for me is in its off-label uses. I’ve been treating it more like an AI sidekick than anything else – the kind that pokes at my ideas, tracks my progress, or even plays devil’s advocate sometimes. It truly can [become a second brain](https://www.xda-developers.com/using-notebooklm-as-second-brain/), and I’ve come up with a bunch of ways to bend it to my workflows across various areas of life.

#### Remixing my own ideas

##### Turning scraps into something new

Like probably most creatives, I have countless half-baked ideas, and most of them end up getting lost in a notes app. But after [pairing NotebookLM with Google Keep](https://www.xda-developers.com/notebooklm-plus-google-keep-productivity-powerhouse/), I saw how powerful it can be at contextualizing my notes. It's like an AI collaborator that never gets tired of hearing me repeat myself, however incoherently. And it’s not like asking a chatbot for ideas, but more like taking what I’ve already written and pushing it into fresh shapes I never would have thought of.
The best use case of this for me is my novel. I upload my random notes to a new notebook every month, and many of them revolve around ideas for the story, world, and characters. Asking NotebookLM to give me some fresh perspective on what I’ve already written has been a game-changer. It quite literally remixes your own ideas. Here are some of the prompts that have helped me do this:
Read the notes for chapter 3 and summarize it from the viewpoint of the antagonist.

Reimagine the notes from this chapter in a comedic tone without changing the plot.

You can adjust these prompts to whatever subject you’re working with. I find it particularly helpful for design concepts, too.

#### A “what if” simulator

##### And it’s interactive

One of the ways I’ve been playing with NotebookLM that kind of blows my mind is turning it into a “what if” simulator. The concept of “what if” is vast, but basically, it’s a tool that can analyze and play out different outcomes of particular scenarios. This can include games, story choices, or even everyday decisions.
I’ll throw in a scenario as a prompt, like a plot twist in my novel or a hypothetical UX design problem, and ask NotebookLM to explore the possible outcomes. The key, here, is to ensure you have the right sources uploaded to your notebook; the AI can’t pull much relevant context out of barebones sources. There are so many ways you can play around with this, as long as your prompt frames it as a scenario with clear options or variables:
In this mobile app interface design, what would happen if I skipped the second step? Predict user reactions and potential pitfalls.

To get the most out of your custom “what if” simulator, make use of the follow-up prompt recommendations that NotebookLM gives you.

#### Pattern spotter and skills tracker

##### Noticing the small stuff I tend to miss

NotebookLM can be like a second brain for noticing patterns I’d never catch on my own. I feed it my journal entries, work logs, even [my entire Obsidian vault](https://www.xda-developers.com/fed-my-entire-obsidian-vault-into-notebooklm/), which gives it more than enough to work with. From there, I can prompt it to surface patterns related to specific topics. This is a rather silly one, but it actually helped point out my most-noted and least-noted grocery list items, which was quite insightful.
This also doubles as a skills tracker. As long as I keep feeding it new sources, it can compare recent notes against older ones. This can help me notice how advanced I’m getting with a design tool, for example. The key, here, is to include a date inside the source itself, because NotebookLM doesn’t log when exactly each source was added. A prompt along these lines usually gets me what I’m looking for:
Compare my notes on using Penpot from March 2025 against August 2025 (dates are included in the sources). How has my skill level changed, and what new techniques have I started using?

I reckoned the Mind Map feature would be useful here since it always provides a concise and digestible overview, but it turns out it’s not that great at surfacing specific patterns. A detailed prompt will get you much further with pattern spotting and skills tracking, and I also recommend using the follow-up prompts.

##### NotebookLM is more versatile than we give it credit for

The more I push NotebookLM into the off-label territory, the more it keeps surprising me with new ways it can fit into my life. It’s become a quiet sidekick that sharpens how I think, create, and approach my workflows. As long as I keep feeding it information and ask the right questions, it won’t leave my corner any time soon.
