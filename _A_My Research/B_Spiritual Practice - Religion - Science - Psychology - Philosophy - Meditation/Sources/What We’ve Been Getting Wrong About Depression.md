Work is needed beyond the scope of therapy to create a more supportive and safer environment for us all. Bianca van Dijk / Pixabay Source: Bianca van Dijk / Pixabay What do we know about depression? We can list its symptoms, such as low mood, low energy, slowing of thought and movement, loss of concentration, and loss of pleasure and interest in activities. There is also a disruption of our patterns of appetite and sleep, and negative, hopeless, and, at worst, suicidal thoughts. We lose the ability and desire to socialize with others.
But what exactly is depression? Based on 30 years of clinical work, I am convinced that depression is a normal and understandable biological response to threat which has become “stuck.”
Our human nervous system responds to danger and life-threat not just with fight, flight, or freeze responses (fear-arousal), but also with fear-collapse—a sudden shutdown of energy production throughout the body. These two aspects of our fear system can be best understood by looking at the context in which they evolved. When a predator attacks its prey, the prey goes into fear-arousal—running or fighting to help it escape. However, if this response fails, an older threat response shuts down the body and the prey collapses, as we can see when a cat catches a mouse.
This fear-collapse—the same biological mechanism as a faint[1], but without full loss of consciousness—stops the predator’s attack. This can lead to a window of opportunity for escape, triggering a sudden switch back into fight or flight (you may have seen a mouse escaping after a cat has got bored with its inactivity).
We share this fear response with mice and the rest of the animal world[2], but it evolved in response to relatively brief attacks and chases[3], and where life-threatening danger is continuous, and no place of safety can be reached, our fear system cannot deactivate or reset. This can leave us habitually responding with fear-arousal or fear-collapse to any level of threat—real or imagined.
Humans are intensely social animals, for whom expulsion from the group was a life-threatening danger, so our fear-system is also wired-up to react to social threats (e.g., rejection, abandonment, bullying, loss of status) as if these were physical attacks, potentially triggering fear-collapse. Observing my own fear responses has led me to reflect that we experience this drop in energy not only as a total physical collapse, but also (for less than life-threatening dangers) as a partial shutdown, just as we can experience a full faint, and also various levels of “feeling faint.”
Threat-induced shutdown (fear-collapse) would appear to be the biological mechanism underlying depression. This theory adequately explains our experience of depression. The shutdown is brought about by a drop in heart rate and blood pressure, which starves our cells of glucose and oxygen. This disables the cell respiration process whereby mitochondria convert glucose and oxygen into the energy that powers each cell.
This means that muscle cells stop working; we are physically debilitated. Brain cells controlling social engagement and thinking are also disabled—hence the loss of the desire and ability to relate to others and the loss of concentration and slowing of thought that is seen in depression. The body’s lack of energy ensures that whatever thinking capacity remains is negative and hopeless. The shutdown also affects the cells of the digestive tract, severely affecting appetite, and all of these impacts lead inevitably to the loss of our sense of pleasure in life.
Lack of understanding can stigmatize depression sufferers. In the past, this very real energy loss was often treated as imaginary (“it’s all in your head”). More recently, the commonly accepted explanation that it is caused by a serotonin imbalance in the brain has been found to be unsubstantiated, though antidepressants can provide temporary relief, which can open up the possibility of working with a therapist for those with more severe depression. The fear-collapse theory points to an alternative biological basis for the symptoms of depression which aligns with lived experience, social and environmental causation, and trauma-informed clinical practice.
In the contest between prey and predator, the way out of fear-collapse is back through fear-arousal. If the predator’s attention is distracted, creating a window of opportunity, there can be a sudden metabolic shift from collapse into hyper-arousal, which can provide the energy for an escape to a place of safety where the fear system can reset.
This shift out of fear-collapse can be activated when therapists help their clients get in touch with anger that has previously been inaccessible or help them reconnect with sensations in their limbs linked to past reactions to danger. These approaches reactivate fear-arousal responses, which can reverse the depressive paralysis of chronic fear-collapse and restore normal vitality.
However, the key factor in switching off our fear responses is safety. The fear system can only deactivate if we can find safe places in which our body can relax. In a world of social disconnection, job insecurity, over-monitored employees, over-tested schoolchildren, collapsing care services, social media lynch-mobs, fear-driven politics and government-created “hostile environments,” safe places where we can truly relax are disappearing.
Depression is not some sort of defect in the individual sufferer—a sense of safety depends on the environments in which we live and work. Along with anxiety, depression arises from normal biological responses to threat, which become stuck and cannot deactivate because the context of our lives is sensed to be insufficiently safe. By giving full attention to a client’s external circumstances, as well as their hard-wired reactions to threat, therapists play an important role in the alleviation of depression, but ultimately, the solution to the scourge of depression which affects so many of us lies in the responsibility we hold individually and collectively to create a society in which every person can feel safe and secure.
