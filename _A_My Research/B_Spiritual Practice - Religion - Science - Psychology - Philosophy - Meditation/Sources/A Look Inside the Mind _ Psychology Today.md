A Look Inside the Mind

What makes the mind different from the brain and why it matters.

The mind is a process, not a place.

Thoughts follow habits, not logic.

Change starts with curiosity.

We talk about “changing minds” or “losing our mind” as if the mind is separate from the brain. But is it? Where does the mind come from, and how does it actually work? When you try to describe the mind, things get slippery fast. You can point to the brain on a scan. You can cut it, scan it, stimulate it. But the mind? You can’t see it. You can’t hold it. And yet you know it’s there. It’s the voice in your head, the emotions that well up before words, the gut sense that something’s off. It’s the inner experience of being you.

The brain is a physical organ. It has mass, shape, blood flow, and neurons. The mind, in contrast, feels more like a process, an invisible stream of thoughts, memories, sensations, beliefs, and desires. It’s not something you have as much as something you are. The mind is what the brain does when it’s awake, alive, and processing the world.

So, where did the idea of the “mind” even originate? Early humans didn’t have MRI machines or

neuroscience

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/neuroscience

textbooks. But they knew they had thoughts and feelings that muscles or bones couldn’t explain. Across cultures, people sensed that something was steering their decisions from the inside. Some called it the soul. Others called it spirit, heart, will, or consciousness. These early ideas were trying to describe the mind in the only language they had.

Today, we see the mind as an emergent property of the brain. It arises not from any single neuron, but from billions firing together. Just like a traffic jam isn’t any one car, the mind isn’t any one cell. It’s the pattern of activity that gives rise to awareness,

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/identity

, and thought. Neuroscience now frames mental activity as the coordinated dynamics of brain networks, rather than isolating one “seat of the mind” (Bertolero & Bassett, 2019). Emergent

materialism

https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/consumer-behavior

similarly holds that the mind arises from brain function, even if it cannot always be reduced to individual neural constituents (Feinberg & Mallett, 2020).

Rules, Loops, and Filters

So if the mind is the output of the brain, does it follow any rules? Surprisingly, yes. Not rules like math, but patterns. The mind likes stories. It seeks patterns. It fills in gaps. It compares everything to what it already knows. It looks for meaning even where there might be none. These rules help us survive. They make us fast, intuitive, and emotionally driven. But they also make us biased, stubborn, and easily misled.

Much of what we call “thinking” isn’t thinking at all. It’s remembering. Repeating. Reacting. The mind relies on loops, including habits, routines, and automatic responses, which are shaped by past experiences. These loops are powerful because they save energy. But they can also trap us, keeping us locked in outdated beliefs, old fears, or bad habits.

The mind also filters reality. It can’t take in everything, so it focuses on what it believes matters most. This is why two people can witness the same event and come away with totally different impressions. Your mind isn’t a camera. It’s an editor. It cuts, colors, and crops the raw footage of life before it ever reaches your conscious awareness.

Emotion gives meaning to experience. Meaning is what drives the mind. Neuroscientific evidence supports that emotional processes often precede, and shape, reasoning and

How to Change a Mind

If the mind runs on loops and filters and feelings, how do you ever change it? Not just yours, but someone else’s? How do people shift perspectives or break free from deeply held beliefs?

The first step is recognizing that the mind resists change. Not because it’s lazy, but because change is risky. If your current view of the world is keeping you safe and functional, your brain has no incentive to upgrade it. To change a mind, you have to create safety first: emotional, social, and intellectual safety.

You also need friction. Minds don’t shift from facts alone. They shift when there’s a contradiction that can’t be ignored. When lived experience clashes with a belief. This dissonance creates an opening. But what fills that opening has to come from within. You can’t force someone to change their mind. You can only help them question it, explore it, and find something better to replace it with.

Curiosity helps. So does humility. The most flexible minds aren’t the ones with all the answers. They’re the ones that keep asking better questions. When you get curious about your own thinking—why you believe what you believe, where those beliefs came from, and whether they still serve you— you start to take back control.

Because ultimately, the mind is malleable. It learns. It adapts. It can grow stronger or get stuck. It can be trained like a muscle or neglected like an attic. It can evolve, if you let it. We don’t just have minds. We

minds. Understanding how they work and shape our world, guiding our actions, and resisting or embracing change might be the most human thing we can do.
