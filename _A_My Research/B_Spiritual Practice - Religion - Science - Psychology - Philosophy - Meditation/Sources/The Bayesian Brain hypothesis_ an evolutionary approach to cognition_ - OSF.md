The Bayesian Brain: an evolutionary approach to cognition

Introduction

"Nothing in biology makes sense, except in the light of evolution."

T. Dobzansky (1973).

We are largely unaware of the mechanisms that determine our thoughts, emotions and behaviour.

Are we integrating memory and novel information in a consistent manner? Are our decisions

balancing preferences and constraints? Is there any fundamental principle that governs the

emergence and the multiple interactions of our sensations, beliefs, emotions and intentions? These

questions have fascinated observers of human behaviour for centuries, and underpin most of the

human sciences. Experimental psychology is nothing but a modern instantiation of this scientific

and philosophical tradition, the concern of which is to disclose the processes underlying cognition

and behaviour using experimental methods. The experimental emphasis is crucial here, as it has

lifted the ambitions of psychologists up to the standards of quantitative science. Now, one simple

though deep insight neuroscience has brought to psychology is the following: most hypotheses

regarding cognitive mechanisms can only be discriminated on the basis of their biological

underpinning. In other words, if we aim at understanding why we do what we do, think and feel, we

should probably bother looking in the brain.

Having said this, the neuroscientific approach to cognition goes beyond the need for additional

experimental data: it comes with natural laws that embodied cognition has to satisfy. From an

evolutionary perspective for example, one could say that the brain is a machine, the primary

function of which is to produce the most adaptive behavioural response. This means that the

cerebral mechanisms that subtend behaviour might have been optimized through natural selection.

This is important, because we can start to try to understand the brain from optimality principles that

are grounded in the necessity for the brain to adapt to its environment.

The Bayesian Brain: an evolutionary approach to cognition

Two distinct mathematical theories allow us to introduce optimality principles in a formal and

consistent framework. Information theory is a probabilistic account of how information is optimally

transmitted and processed. It relies on probability calculus, and, more precisely, on Bayes' rule. It

was originally developed to solve problems of numerical signal processing (e.g. data compressing

and storage). It quickly extended beyond academic barriers, finding applications in statistics,

physics, artificial intelligence, telecommunications, etc... More recently, it was successfully applied

to model the way the brain perceives, understands and/or learns about its environment. However,

despite the breadth of mental phenomena that it seems capable of explaining, information theory

omits an essential element, a notion without which we would be incapable of acting. The missing

link between what we know and what we do is the notion of intention. For achieving one’s goal, not

all knowledge is useful. More precisely, knowledge is useful insofar as it helps predicting the

possible outcomes of candidate actions. What is needed here is a way to define the relevance

and/or the importance of any information, irrespective of the way it has been obtained. This is the

realm of decision theory, which effectively starts where information theory ends. In brief, decision

theory identifies the most instrumental course of action, given potential constraints and

assumptions about action outcomes. Its first achievements are in the domain of statistics, where it

was used to design hypothesis tests. Economists then thought it could help them dealing with a

pressing issue: rationality1. In this context, it gave rise to game theory, which describes the

behaviour of agents engaged in (cooperative or competitive) social interactions. Physics and

robotics then applied it to systems, whose actions influence their environment. This difficult

problem required the development of a large body of theoretical work now known under the name

of control theory. Nowadays, some psychologists use it to analyze motivation, because it provides

an objective definition to notions such as incentive, reward or preferences.

The Bayesian Brain: an evolutionary approach to cognition

Taken together, information theory and decision theory are called by the name of Bayesian

decision theory or BDT. BDT helps us identifying what an agent should do to best achieve her

goals, given her available information. In other terms, the behavioural response of a Bayesian

agent to an environmental stimulus is, by construction, optimal. First of all, BDT provides a

normative reference point that can be compared to experimentally observed (overt) behaviour. This

is important, because one can ask whether peoples' behaviour systematically deviates from

optimality. As we will see below, such biases have already been experimentally demonstrated. In

the perceptual domain, they appear as impressive and robust (e.g., visual) illusions. More

generally, these biases express themselves as distortions of the objective information regarding

the relevant variables of a decision problem. They quickly were interpreted in terms of markers of

suboptimal cognitive processes, which called for principled explanations. On the one hand, one

may argue that the brain's blunders are the outcome of information processing mechanisms that

are imperfect (e.g. noisy) and/or constrained by the system's limited resources (cf. memory,

attention, reasoning, etc...). On the other hand, one has to realize that someone’s error may, under

a more subjective standpoint, be viewed as the best course of action, under his/her idiosyncratic

perspective on things. In other words, subjective information processing will exhibit properties that

may appear objectively suboptimal. Of course, these explanations are not mutually exclusive.

However, they rely on different standpoints regarding the optimality of information processing,

which is the premise of the bayesian brain hypothesis:

 cerebral information processing has been optimized by natural selection;

 cerebral information processing is thus optimal on average, but can appear suboptimal in

some circumstances.

Here the optimality of cerebral information processing cannot be dissociated from the

(evolutionary) function of cognitive processes. This is because evolutionary pressure does not act

blindly on all aspects of cerebral processing: it shapes the brains' skills that are required for solving

problems that are important for the species' survival. Recall that cerebral information processing

The action-perception loop

In this section, we will expose the Bayesian interpretation of perception and motor control. Taken

together, these cognitive functions form the action-perception loop, i.e. the way in which we

interact with our environment. We will see how to frame the Bayesian brain hypothesis, in order to

predict the fundamental properties of this interaction. But let us first recall the basic elements of

BDT that will be useful in the remainder of this paper.

BDT for dummies

"If people do not believe that mathematics is simple, it is only because they do not realize how

attention to uncertainty, i.e. the lack of information about variables that are relevant for a given

paracetamol (utile against flu symptoms, useless against cancer) and lung surgery (utile against

cancer, deleterious for flu). What would you do?

One of the major limitations of motor control is due to some form of "residual motor uncertainty":

the execution of an intentional movement is never perfectly reliable, i.e. it is noisy. In other terms,

the motor system cannot precisely anticipate the consequence of a (central) motor command

(Faisal et al., 2008). In such circumstances, the optimal architecture of a system whose aim is to

control motor actions needs to correct the motor command on the basis of online sensory

information. This raises the question of how sensorimotor integration is achieved: how does the

brain use sensory feedback signals to reduce movement uncertainty? The BDT model of

sensorimotor integration suggests that the sensory consequences of movements need to be

predicted from the motor command, and compared to actual sensory feedback signals. Utile

information on the movement is then derived from the deviation of sensory sensory signals to their

prediction, which is called sensory prediction error. This has many implications on the expected

properties of sensorimotor integration. First, when no sensory feedback signal is available,

proprioceptive perception or proprioception26 is biased towards the prediction derived from the

motor command. Second, the impact of the sensory feedback signal on proprioception is greater at

the end of the movement, because the uncertainty of the system's prediction grows with time. By

manipulating the magnitude of movements' external perturbations (noise) in the absence of visual

feedback (movements are performed in the dark), D. Wolpert and collaborators were able to show

that the proprioceptive perception of one's hand position verifies these properties (Körding and

Wolpert, 2004; Wolpert et al., 1995).

The free energy principle

Cognitive neuroscience has produced a broad set of empirical findings that reveal the functional

segregation of the brain's cognitive systems. This reinforced a modular view on brain organization,

which essentially neglects functional interdependencies between brain systems. Over the past two

decades however, the field has been looking for ways of accomplishing a novel paradigm shift.

Beyond localizing the brain regions that contribute to a given cognitive function, researchers now

attempt to address the question of how information is transmitted within brain systems (Sporns,

2013). This derives from the principle of functional integration, which essentially suggests that the

function of a given brain component (brain region, neural population, neuron, etc…) may be best

understood in terms of the nature of information exchanges it has with other brain components

(Tononi et al., 1994).

There are very few theoretical frameworks that enable understanding (i) why and how brain activity

self-organizes according to functional segregation and integration principles, and (ii) how these

properties blend and shape neural information processing (Zeki and Shipp, 1988). In this section,

we will summarize the Free Energy Principle or FEP (Friston, 2010) : a global theory of brain

function based on a Bayesian interpretation of the neural code, i.e. the link between Marr’s

algorithmic and implementational levels. We will see how the FEP provides a theoretical

justification for the Bayesian brain hypothesis, from the homeostatic nature of adaptive biological

systems, and how it ties distinct flavours of BDT-optimality together. But to fully grasp the

conceptual novelty of the FEP, one first needs to consider a criticism that is commonly raised

against the neural implications of the Bayesian brain hypothesis. In brief: why should the neural

code be optimal (Colombo and Seriès, 2012)? This question, in fact, is twofold. First, it is unclear

whether the neurophysiological mechanisms that are capable of implementing bayesian

information processing are available to the brain. Second, when exerted at a neurobiological level,

selective pressure may act against the costly BDT-optimality of brain information processing. Let

description can be operated without loss of information on the visual scene. This is the essential

information sources that might consume it" (Simon, 1969). In brief, attentional control can be

resources of attentional processing are limited (Marois and Ivanoff, 2005). Under this view,

cognitive miser (Stanovich, 2009). Second, mental effort regulation may have been shaped by

Social cognition is the set of cognitive processes that are specifically engaged when people

interact with each other. In particular, social cognition is what enables us to understand and bond

ability to recognize and comprehend others' mental states such as beliefs, desires, emotions, etc.

Second, we will consider the conditions that foster the emergence of (certain forms of) collective

intelligence.

explanation for the emergence of complexity. This applies, in particular, to the functional

organization of the brain. Thus, evolutionary pressure shaped the mechanisms that enable the

brain to adapt near-optimally to the socio-environmental features that are relevant for survival.

Let us dismiss the "panglossian" critique straightaway59: the bayesian brain hypothesis does not

state that every brain phenotype is a necessity, every behaviour is adaptive, and/or every mental

process is optimal. The reason is manifold. First, selective pressure is, in fine, an obligation of

ends, not an obligation of means. Although all members of a given species face the same

computational problems, there is no reason to think that the solutions to these problems are

unique, and/or independent of socio-environmental idiosyncrasies (including, e.g., age or gender).

Second, as we have seen, a feature that is optimal on average can induce systematic biases in

specific situations (cf. visual illusions). Third, the system's properties that have been shaped by

evolutionary pressure in our evolutionary past may be unadapted in our current environment60.

Fourth, some of these properties may result from a compromise between different computational

problems, which may induce conflicting evolutionary forces61.

Having said this, the bayesian brain hypothesis may be criticized in more subtle ways. In particular:

under an evolutionary perspective on human behaviour, would it not be sufficient to identify its

biological determinants? In other terms, is it really necessary to care about cognitive mechanisms

to disclose the natural laws that shape our behaviour? This matter is much broader than the scope

of the current book chapter. This is why I will reduce it to two specific issues. I refer readers

interested in a deeper discussion of this topic to Symons (1992).

To conclude, the bayesian brain hypothesis takes a formal perspective on how selective pressure

computational problems that the brain faces promotes distinct properties (precision, efficiency,

flexibility, sophistication, etc...), all of which seem to gracefully derive from the same BDT optimality

principles. One may thus think that the bayesian brain hypothesis is close to bringing a complete

and quantitative theory of human cognition and behaviour. This is not the case however, for many

reasons. First, the bayesian interpretation of cognition is not an established approach in the field66.

Second, there are divergent opinions about how bayesian brain models should be used and where

their theoretical and/or empirical value lies. Third, most bayesian models that can be found in the

existing literature focus on restricted domains of cognition. Fourth, the clinical utility (in psychiatry

and/or neurology) of these models is yet to be demonstrated67. All these reasons are not unrelated;

the bayesian perspective on cognition is not, so to say, organized in an optimal manner. At this
