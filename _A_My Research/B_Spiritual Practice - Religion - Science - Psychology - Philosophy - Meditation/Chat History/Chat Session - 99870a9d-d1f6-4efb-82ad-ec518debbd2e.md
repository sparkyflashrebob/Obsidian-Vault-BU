In the *Madhyamaka* (Middle Way) tradition, philosopher Nagarjuna uses the **four-fold logic** (*catuṣkoṭi* or *tetralemma*) as a radical deconstructive tool designed specifically to break the conceptual habits of ordinary rational thinking [1-3].

Here is how this logic systematically dismantles standard cognitive patterns:

### 1. Transcending Western Binary Logic

Ordinary human thinking and Western rationalism (such as Aristotelian logic) rely on a binary, two-fold framework: something either **is** (\(A\)) or **is not** (\(\text{not } A\)) [2]. Nagarjuna expands this into four exhaustive logical possibilities regarding any proposition [1, 3]:

1. **Affirmation:** You are this (\(A\))
2. **Negation:** You are not this (\(\text{not } A\))
3. **Conjunction:** You are both this and that (\(A \text{ and } \text{not } A\))
4. **Disjunction/Neither:** You are neither this nor that (\(\text{neither } A \text{ nor } \text{not } A\))

By systematically presenting and then dismantling all four possible logical positions, the *catuṣkoṭi* leaves the rational mind with nowhere to land or anchor [3].

### 2. Radical Deconstruction and "Ontological Annihilation"

Nagarjuna’s method (specifically in the *Prasaṅgika* approach) is not meant to build a new philosophical theory, but to achieve **complete ontological annihilation** of all metaphysical positions [3]. Ordinary thinking constantly seeks a fixed conceptual "ground" or stance to hold onto [4, 5]. The four-fold logic smashes every attempt to form a rigid metaphysical position, exposing that any conceptual stance is ultimately unsustainable [3, 4].

### 3. Exposing the Limits of Language and Cognition

Ordinary thinking assumes that human language and conceptual categories accurately reflect ultimate reality. Nagarjuna’s logic reveals that ultimate reality is **beyond cognition and the dualistic, reductive nature of language** [4]. Because language naturally divides reality into subject/object and binary opposites, any attempt to define ultimate reality through words or conceptual formulas fails [4].

### 4. Steering Between Eternalism and Nihilism

Ordinary thought tends to swing between two extreme traps [6]:

* **Eternalism:** Believing that objects, the self, or God have a permanent, fixed essence (*svabhāva*) [6, 7].
* **Nihilism:** Believing that because things lack inherent essence, nothing exists and there is no meaning at all [6, 8].

The four-fold logic dismantles both extremes, placing the practitioner in the **Middle Way** between "nothing and everything" [6].

### 5. Deconstructing Emptiness Itself (*Śūnyatā*)

The ultimate move in Nagarjuna’s philosophy—what sets it apart from other non-dual systems—is applying the four-fold logic to **emptiness itself** [1, 7, 9]. Nagarjuna insists that even the concept of emptiness or non-duality is empty of inherent existence (*swabhava*) [7]. This prevents the rational mind from turning "emptiness" or "non-duality" into a new conceptual dogma or ideological anchor [5, 7].

### 6. Liberation Through Groundlessness and Paradox

Ultimately, the purpose of dismantling rational thinking is **soteriological (emancipatory)** rather than merely theoretical [5, 10]. When the linear, rational mind breaks down against contradiction and paradox, the practitioner is forced to relinquish all mental grasping [5, 11]. Below all intellectual constructs lies complete "groundlessness"—a state of open, unconstructed non-dual awareness [5, 12].

  1. You are this;
  2. You are not this;
  3. You are both this and that;
  4. You are neither this nor that [1, 6].
     Unlike standard Western Aristotelian binary logic (\(A\) or \(\text{not } A\)), this *Prasaṅgika* dialectic smashes all conceptual positions, demonstrating that ultimate reality cannot be captured by language or rational cognition [5-7]. The goal is to break the rational mind and dissolve conceptual grasping [1, 8, 9].
* **The Two Truths and Emptiness (*Śūnyatā*):**
  Gupta emphasizes that Buddhist emptiness is not nihilism [3, 10]. Reality operates on two levels [7, 11]:

  + **Ultimate Truth (*Paramārtha-satya*):** Everything is empty of inherent, fixed essence (*svabhāva*) [7, 13]. Ultimate reality is absolute potentiality (*Tathāgatagarbha*—the womb of potential) [13, 14].

  Gupta distinguishes Eastern non-duality (*Advaita* / *Anatta*) from Western philosophical idealism [15, 16]. Western idealism often reduces reality to psychological "mental states" [16]. In contrast, classical non-duality is **apophatic** (*neti neti*—"not this, not that") [15, 17]. It negates dualistic boundaries rather than attempting to fit awareness into a rigid metaphysical box [15, 18].
* **Ontological Porosity and Epistemic Humility:**
  Rather than clinging to a single metaphysical model (whether mechanistic materialism, idealist monism, or New Age claims of "I am God"), Gupta advocates for **ontological porosity**—holding metaphysical frameworks lightly, exercising epistemic humility ("I don't know shit"), and accommodating a plurality of worldviews pragmatically [3, 11, 19-21].

### 2. The Nature and Practice of Meditation: Familiarization, Presence, and the Subtle Body

* **Meditation as *Gom* (Familiarization):**
  In Tibetan Buddhism, the word translated as meditation is *Gom* (*sgom*), which literally means **familiarization**—specifically, becoming familiar with the recognition of non-dual awareness [22, 23]. It is not a technique to suppress thoughts or accumulate achievements, but a continuous recognition of who you already are beneath the ego [22, 24].
* **"Anytime Anywhere" Practice and Collapsing Subject/Object:**
  Drawing from lineage masters like Yongey Mingyur Rinpoche, Gupta explains that non-dual meditation can be practiced at any moment [22, 25, 26]. By noticing the watching observer and the object being observed, one realizes that the object exists entirely within sensory perception; there is nothing "outside" the senses [22-24]. Collapsing this subject-object duality transforms abstract awareness into **direct, embodied presence** [22, 23].
* **Nature of Mind Traditions (*Dzogchen* & *Mahamudra*):**
  Beyond preliminary focused-attention practices (*shamatha*), nature-of-mind practices directly point out the luminous, unconstructed nature of awareness [17, 27, 28]. Non-dual awareness is not a transient peak experience or "high" to chase, but an underlying modality of being [29, 30].
* **Tantra and the Subtle Body:**
  In Vajrayana (Buddhist Tantra), meditation is intensely embodied and works directly with the **subtle body**—the felt, internal resonance of the phenomenal world within physical sensation [17, 31]. Advanced tantric disciplines involve radical dissolution experiences where boundaries of physical form, identity, and conceptual reference completely dissolve [17, 30, 32].

* **Soteriological Liberation vs. Psychological Healing:**
  Gupta highlights a key divergence between modern Western psychedelic therapy and traditional Eastern contemplative paths [1, 33]. While Western therapy uses psychedelics primarily to repair or heal a broken self-narrative, ancient traditions (such as *Soma* or Vajrayana) used altered states for **soteriological liberation**—losing the self entirely to escape cyclic suffering (*samsara*) [33-36].
* **The Hazard of Chasing Experiences (*State vs. Trait*):**
  A major danger of psychedelics is becoming attached to the temporary "peak experience," leading to spiritual bypassing, dissociation, and hedonistic high-chasing [27, 37, 38]. Because all states are impermanent, the true contemplative task is **stabilizing temporary states into enduring daily traits** [20, 29, 39].

  Humor is highlighted as a vital integration tool [40, 41]. Because paradox breaks the rational mind, being able to laugh at oneself and hold reality lightly prevents fanatical attachment or existential despair [1, 9, 40]. Spiritual practice should be "serious fun"—deeply disciplined yet playful (*Līlā*) [42-44].
* **Grounding, Ethics, and the Bodhisattva Vow:**
  Deconstructing the self without proper grounding can be dangerous, particularly for individuals without a healthy, stable initial self-concept [38, 45, 46]. To safely navigate total dissolution, traditional practice relies on:

  + **Ethical Restraint and Mindfulness:** Containing the mind so it does not become wildly ungrounded [47].
  + **The Bodhisattva Motivation:** Dedicating every practice to the liberation of all living beings, which shatters self-obsessed narratives [48, 49].
  + **Guru and Lineage:** Surrendering to a qualified spiritual teacher who holds an authentic lineage and provides an unshakeable ground of devotion and safety [8, 25, 44, 50, 51].

### The Essential Skill: Voluntarily Returning Attention

According to philosopher and psychologist William James, the single most important skill a human can develop is **"the faculty of voluntarily bringing back a wandering attention over and over again"** [1, 2]. James called this ability the very foundation of **judgment, character, and will**, asserting that possessing it makes one *compos sui* (in charge of oneself) [1].

Crucially, James took it for granted that the human mind naturally drifts [1, 2]. True focus is not an unwavering, static state, but rather the repeated, active effort of noticing when the mind has wandered and gently bringing it back to the chosen object [2, 3].

---

### Personal Crisis and the Discovery of Free Will

This insight emerged from James's own life struggles [4, 5]. In 1870, at age 28, James finished his medical degree but sank into a severe depression accompanied by intense existential fear [6, 7]. His breakthrough occurred after reading the French philosopher Charles Renuvier, who defined **free will** as "the sustaining of a thought because I choose to when I might have other thoughts" [7].

Realizing he could not logically prove free will, James declared in his notebook: *"My first act of free will shall be to believe in free will"* [8]. He decided to act as if he were free, shifting his focus away from endless speculation toward deliberate action and the training of his attention [8].

---

### How Attention Shapes Human Experience

James argued that out of the millions of sensory inputs reaching us at any moment, **"my experience is what I agree to attend to"** [2, 9]. Two people can undergo the exact same external circumstances, but their experience will differ entirely based on where they direct their focus [2]. Because sustained voluntary attention naturally lasts only a few seconds at a time, staying focused is actually a continuous chain of successive choices to return to the task at hand [2, 3].

---

### The 4 Practical Rules for Training Attention and Habits

To free up mental energy for higher creative and intellectual work, James advised handing daily routines over to the "effortless custody of automatism" through habit [3]. Drawing from psychologist Alexander Bain and his own life, James laid out **four rules for building habits and strengthening willpower** [8, 10, 11]:

1. **Launch with strong initiative:** Start with a clear, firm commitment and structure your environment so the old habit is harder to maintain [10].
2. **Allow no exceptions:** Avoid lapses while a new habit is taking root, comparing every slip to dropping a carefully wound ball of string that takes dozens of turns to rewind [10].
3. **Act on resolutions immediately:** Seize the very first opportunity to execute a good intention while the impulse is warm [11].
4. **Perform daily "gratuitous exercise":** Practice doing a small task every day or two simply because you would rather not do it, keeping the "faculty of effort" conditioned for when major challenges arise [11].

---

### Modern Scientific Validation

James warned that every choice leaves a physical trace on the nervous system, referencing the fictional Rip Van Winkle who excused every drink with "I won't count this time"—whereas the body registers every single instance [12]. However, this cumulative effect also works in reverse: just as bad habits compound, every small act of refocusing builds mastery over time [13].

A 2010 Harvard study by Killingsworth and Gilbert validated James's thesis [14]. Sampling thousands of adults via an app, they discovered that minds wander **46.9% of the time** and that a wandering mind directly predicts unhappiness, supporting James's belief that training the mind to be present is fundamental to human well-being [13, 14].

**The 37 Practices of Bodhisattvas**, composed by the Tibetan master Gyalsey Thokmay Sangpo, is a seminal Lojong (mind training) text that offers a practical, step-by-step handbook on how to live the Bodhisattva path [1, 2]. Grounded in the Mahayana tradition, it details the daily disciplines required of a "being of enlightened mind" who vows to achieve full awakening to liberate all sentient beings from the ocean of cyclic existence [3, 4].

When viewed in the larger context of Buddhist spiritual discipline, these 37 practices can be mapped directly onto the classical **Three Trainings (Morality, Concentration, and Wisdom)** [5, 6], as well as the historical evolution of the Buddhist **Path to Liberation** [7].

### 1. Foundational Morality and Renunciation (*Sīla*)

Spiritual discipline in the Buddhist path begins with **Morality (*Sīla*)**, which provides the vital foundation required for establishing mental collectedness and wisdom [6, 8, 9]. In *The 37 Practices*, this is expressed through explicit steps of renunciation and ethical restraint:

* **Renouncing Worldly Attachments:** The text begins by urging the practitioner to leave behind homeland, loved ones, and bad objects to pacify the three poisons of attachment, anger, and confusion [3].
* **Embracing Impermanence:** The Bodhisattva must let go of attachment to this life, reflecting that companions, wealth, and even the "guest-house" of the physical body are transient [1].
* **Taking Refuge and Relying on Teachers:** True protection is sought by taking refuge in the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha) [10], and by cherishing a spiritual teacher whose presence causes faults to end and wholesome qualities to grow [1].
* **Safeguarding Ethics:** Sangpo warns that "without ethics you can't accomplish your own well-being, so wanting to accomplish others' is laughable" [11]. This matches the early Buddhist view that a solid foundation of moral discipline is the irreplaceable support for concentration and deeper insight [9, 12].

### 2. Mental Collectedness and Concentration (*Samādhi*)

The second stage of training, **Concentration (*Samādhi*)**, focuses on calming, unifying, and purifying the mind of obstacles [5, 13, 14]. In *The 37 Practices*, concentration is not merely an isolated retreat into refined states, but an active, vigilant tool used in the midst of daily experience:

* **Conquering the Hindrances:** The text advocates cultivating concentration and seclusion to pacify disturbing emotions [3, 15]. In early Buddhism, this aligns with the conquest of the five hindrances (sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, and doubt) [16, 17]. Sangpo addresses these with specific antidotes: subduing the "enemy of your own anger" with the militia of love and compassion [18], and abandoning sensual pleasures which, like saltwater, only increase one's thirst [18].
* **Vigilant Mindfulness:** A Bodhisattva must remain "armed with the antidotes, the guards of mindfulness and mental alertness," to destroy disturbing emotions like attachment the moment they arise [19].
* **Introspective Awareness:** The text advises constant self-examination [20]. Whatever one is doing, the ultimate discipline is to ask, "What's the state of my mind?" [19] to consistently accomplish the good of others [19].

### 3. The Realization of Wisdom (*Paññā*) and Emptiness

The ultimate goal of Buddhist discipline is **Wisdom (*Paññā*)**, which uncovers the true nature of phenomena and permanently eradicates the latent ignorance underlying suffering [6, 21, 22].

* **Non-Dual Perception:** Sangpo asserts that "Whatever appears is your own mind" [23]. He instructs the practitioner to understand that the mind is inherently free from fabricated extremes, avoiding the duality of "subject and object" [23], and to cultivate the wisdom "which does not conceive the three spheres [as real]" [15].
* **Viewing Form as Illusory:** Agreeable situations (likened to a beautiful rainbow) [23] and disagreeable, painful circumstances (likened to a "child's death in a dream") [23] are both to be contemplated as illusory [23].
* **The Second Turning of the Wheel:** This realization directly reflects the **Second Turning of the Wheel of Dharma** (the Madhyamika school founded by Nagarjuna), which established the concept of *shunyata* (Emptiness) [7, 24]. In this view, samsara and nirvana are "not-two" [4]. By recognizing that all Form is empty, the Bodhisattva learns to find liberating Emptiness directly in the world of Form, rather than seeking a temporary escape in formless states [4, 25, 26].

### 4. The Shift from the Arhat to the Bodhisattva Ideal

Within the larger context of the path to liberation, *The 37 Practices* represents a massive shift from the **Arhat ideal** of early Buddhism to the **Bodhisattva ideal** of the Mahayana [4]:

* **Eradicating Self-Clinging:** Early teachings focus on personal renunciation and the systematic eradication of individual fetters (personality-view, doubt, greed, and aversion) to achieve the Arhat's personal liberation [4, 27, 28].
* **The Bodhisattva Vow:** Conversely, the Bodhisattva fully embraces samsara [4]. Because "all suffering comes from the wish for your own happiness" and "perfect Buddhas are born from the thought to help others," the Bodhisattva’s discipline is to "exchange your own happiness for the suffering of others" [29].
* **Radical Altruism and Lojong (Mind Training):** This results in extreme practices of the *Paramitas* (Perfections) [11, 15]. Under Sangpo's instructions:
  + If someone steals your wealth, you dedicate your body, possessions, and virtue to them [29].
  + If someone tries to cut off your head, you take their misdeeds upon yourself out of compassion [30].
  + If someone defames or derides you publicly, you bow to them as a spiritual teacher [30] and speak of their good qualities [30].
  + If someone you cared for like your own child treats you as an enemy, you cherish them like a mother does a sick child [31].
  + Those who harm you are to be treated like "precious treasures," because they provide the perfect condition to cultivate patience without hostility [11].

### Conclusion: The Unified Path to Liberation

Ultimately, *The 37 Practices* illustrates that the path to liberation is a complete integration of **wisdom** and **skillful means** [15]. True spiritual discipline does not stop at personal calm or the temporary suppression of defilements [32, 33]. By establishing a solid foundation of ethics [11], maintaining unshakeable mindfulness [19], and contemplating the empty, illusory nature of all phenomena [23], the practitioner dismantles the deep-seated illusion of a separate self [34]. In the end, the Bodhisattva dedicates the virtue of all such efforts to perfect enlightenment [2], dissolving the boundary between self and other to bring about the ultimate liberation of all living beings [3, 29].
