MODEL:

On a personal level, karma works as a cycle of **intentional action** and its resulting **subjective experience**, where mindfulness and meditation serve as the primary tools to interrupt and transform this process [1-3]. In the sources, karma is defined fundamentally as **volition** (*cetana*), or the process of intentionally shaping states of body and mind [3, 4].

### How Karma Works in Meeting Experience

When you "meet" an experience, you are encountering what the sources call **karmic results** (*vipaka*) [5, 6]. On a personal level, this manifests in several ways:

* **Resultant Feelings:** Your immediate sense impressions and the accompanying "feeling-tone" (*vedana*)—whether pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral—are often the results of past conditions rather than something you are actively "doing" in the moment [5, 7].
* **The Role of the Subconscious:** In the Yogacara tradition, these results are driven by **karmic seeds** (*bijas*) stored in the "storehouse consciousness" (*alaya-vijnana*) [8, 9]. These seeds act as ingrained dispositions that **predispose how you perceive and respond** to the world, creating a "perfuming" effect that colors your daily reality [9-11].
* **The Feedback Loop:** If you react to these results with judgment or emotional attachment, you create a "ripple effect" of energy that returns to the universe as new karma, essentially **solidifying a loop** of repeated experiences and reactions [2, 12].

### The Role of Mindfulness

Mindfulness (*sati*) acts as a "gatekeeper" at a specific point in the causal chain [13, 14]. Its function is to provide **bare, non-judging awareness** of the present moment, allowing you to see the "gap" between a sensation and your reaction to it [14-16].

* **Purifying Perception:** Mindfulness helps "cleanse" perception by removing the **personal additions**—such as memories, cravings, and aversions—that we typically project onto our experiences [17, 18].
* **Interrupting Proliferation:** By being mindful of the "feeling-tone," you can prevent the mind from moving into **mental proliferation** (*papanca*) or "obsessions," which are the primary drivers of craving and new karma [19-21].
* **Developing Detachment:** As you observe feelings appropriately, you begin to see them as "winds in the sky" that are impermanent and not "yours," which allows for a **healthy degree of detachment** from the karmic cycle [22, 23].

### The Role of Meditation

Meditation, specifically **Vipassana (insight meditation)**, is the systematic practice used to experience these truths directly [24].

* **Direct Observation:** During meditation, you learn to observe sensations arising and passing away without reacting to them [25, 26]. This state of **"pure observation"** or "witness consciousness" prevents the creation of new intentions, allowing the old karmic energy to eventually dissolve [2, 12].
* **Anchoring the Mind:** Practices like mindfulness of breathing provide a **neutral anchor** that calms the body and mind, making it easier to notice and abandon the "hindrances" (like restlessness or aversion) that typically trigger karmic reactions [27-29].
* **Transforming the "Storehouse":** By choosing "wholesome" objects of focus—such as the three marks of existence (impermanence, suffering, and non-self)—you consciously **reprogram your mental habits**, replacing ignorance with wisdom [30-32].

The sources provide a powerful analogy for this process: **the cycle of karma is like feeding.** Just as a person who stops eating doesn't die instantly but survives by digesting the old fat and muscle stored in the body, a meditator who maintains equanimity stops "feeding" the mind new reactions [33]. Over time, the **old "stock" of karma**—past habits and accumulated dispositions—comes to the surface, is processed, and eventually "grows cold" or dissolves, leading to liberation [33, 34].
