The definition of consciousness sometimes includes self-awareness, but this relationship is highly debated and often depends on the specific definition of consciousness being used, particularly concerning the distinction between basic awareness and higher-level human consciousness.

Here is what the sources say regarding why "self-awareness" is frequently involved in the definition of "consciousness," and why this inclusion is controversial:

### 1. The Definitional Challenge (Why Self-Awareness is Used)

The concept of consciousness is notoriously difficult to define without resorting to related terms [1]. When attempting to define consciousness, people often encounter the problem of **circular definition**, making it hard to describe the concept without using words that are essentially synonyms or closely related ideas [1, 2].

The term **self-awareness** is specifically mentioned as one of the concepts frequently used in attempts to describe consciousness [1]. When defining consciousness, one may ask: "how do you describe consciousness without using terms that are kind of related to consciousness, like **self-awareness**?" [1].

The implication is that, for many people, especially those interested in the "human level of consciousness," self-awareness is an inherent characteristic or a highly relevant component of what they mean by consciousness [3].

### 2. Arguments Against Including Self-Awareness in the Definition

Other perspectives argue strongly that consciousness should not necessarily include self-awareness:

* **Minimal Definition of Consciousness:** One minimal definition offered in the sources is that **consciousness is awareness and perception of internal and external stimuli**, which **does not necessarily mean self-awareness** [4]. From this viewpoint, consciousness is simply "one step above a plant, which can only react to internal and external stimuli, without actually being aware of them" [4].
* **The Flatworm Debate:** When applying the minimal definition (consciousness = awareness/perception of stimuli), one commentator suggests you might assign consciousness to a flatworm [3]. However, another argues that a flatworm, even with about 300 neurons, may not be considered "aware" in a meaningful way by most neuroscientists because its response to stimuli is likely very **hardwired**, making it no more conscious than a simple mechanical system [5].
* **Separation of Terms:** Some commentators explicitly differentiate the terms, stating: "**You are confusing self-awareness and awareness. They have nothing to do with each other**" [6]. They suggest that while awareness is a binary (you are either aware or not), self-awareness is a gradation, with all living beings with a brain having it to some extent [6].
* **Focus on Human Consciousness:** Those who insist on including self-awareness are often interested primarily in the **human level of consciousness** [3]. If a definition is overly simplistic, such as "it reacts to the environment," it doesn't fit the conversation most people are interested in having about consciousness [7].

### 3. Self-Awareness in Modern Psychological and Buddhist Contexts

The study of the self and self-awareness confirms its deep link to human cognition, even if it is not universally required for consciousness:

* **Neural Correlates of Self-Concept:** Neuroscience research often compares representations of the self and others, suggesting that the **neural patterns associated with one’s own self-concept are more distinct or discriminable** than those associated with knowledge about others [8]. This distinction occurs in specific brain regions, reinforcing the centrality of the self-concept in complex cognition [8].
* **Metacognitive Awareness and Self-Regulation:** In modern psychological approaches, such as those related to mindfulness, the ability to take a **perspective on one’s inner experience** is referred to as metacognitive awareness, decentering, or defusion [9]. This ability to objectify one's internal states is critical for emotional regulation and involves a **transcendent sense of self or "self as context"** [9, 10].
* **The Illusion of Self:** In Buddhist philosophy, the notion of a permanent, unchanging self (*anattā*) is denied, and the conventional "self" is considered an illusory, imputed construct [11-14]. However, this illusion is created by the constant activities of the **Five Aggregates**, including consciousness (*viññāṇa*) and perception (*saññā*) [15-17]. Yogācāra Buddhism specifically identifies the **Manas-vijnana (seventh consciousness)** as the faculty that continuously operates and mistakenly perceives the *Alaya-vijnana* (storehouse consciousness) as a constant, eternal self, leading to self-delusion and self-centeredness [18, 19]. The denial of this ultimate self is key to liberation [11, 13, 20].

In summary, self-awareness is often included in the definition of consciousness because it is difficult to describe human-level consciousness without it [1], and because human consciousness frequently entails complex reflection on one's own existence [3]. However, a simpler definition of consciousness as basic awareness or sensation explicitly excludes the need for self-awareness [4, 6].
