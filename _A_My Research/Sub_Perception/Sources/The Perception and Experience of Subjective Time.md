Subjective time is basically the way time feels to us, as opposed to the way a clock measures it. It’s the personal, lived experience of time passing — sometimes dragging, sometimes flying — shaped by attention, emotion, memory, and context.

---

Step-by-step breakdown

1. Objective vs subjective:

Objective time: minutes, hours, days — measurable, the same for everyone.

Subjective time: perception of how long something lasts — highly variable.

2. Why it varies:

Attention: When you’re absorbed in an activity (a “flow” state), hours can feel like minutes. Boredom or pain does the opposite.

Emotion: Fear or novelty can stretch time (“the seconds felt like forever”). Joy or routine often compresses it.

Memory: Looking back, weeks packed with new experiences feel longer than weeks of repetition.

Age: A year feels longer to a child than an adult — partly because of proportionality (1 year is a big chunk of a child’s life) and partly because adults encounter less novelty.

3. Philosophical and scientific views:

Psychology: “Time perception” is studied like a sense, influenced by dopamine and neural rhythms.

Philosophy: Thinkers from Augustine to Bergson framed subjective time as central to human existence — not just a distortion, but our true experience of time.

Neuroscience: Brain regions like the basal ganglia and prefrontal cortex help construct our inner timeline.

---

Alternative perspectives

Buddhist angle: Time is a mental construct, arising with perception and dissolving when the mind rests.

Stoic angle: Only the present moment is truly ours — subjective time reveals how easily we let it slip away.

Practical lens: Manipulating subjective time can be useful — structuring days with novelty, breaks, or mindfulness stretches perceived time and reduces the sense of life rushing by.

---

Practical summary

Notice: Pay attention to when time drags vs flies — that’s data on where your energy and attention go.

Stretch it: Seek novelty, mindfulness, and presence to make life feel fuller.

Compress when needed: Routines and focus help long or dull tasks pass faster.

Reflect: Memory reframes time; journaling or marking milestones can expand the way you remember it.
