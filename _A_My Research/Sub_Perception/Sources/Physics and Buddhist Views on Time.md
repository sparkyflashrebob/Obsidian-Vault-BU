1. Time as relational

In relativity, time isn’t an independent river—it stretches and contracts depending on what it’s measured against.

In Buddhism, moments exist only in dependence on causes, conditions, and awareness. No “time” apart from relations. Both point away from any absolute clock.

2. The present as construction

Neuroscience and quantum physics both show how perception lags behind events; our “now” is stitched together from bits already past.

Buddhist thought says the same: what we call “the present” is an aggregate—mind labeling continuity over flickering changes.

3. The arrow of time

Physics traces it to entropy: systems naturally move from order to disorder.

Buddhism traces it to clinging: we feel time’s weight because we resist change. Entropy and attachment are both ways of saying things disperse unless held—though the Buddhist answer isn’t to freeze it, but to let it dissolve freely.

4. Timelessness as insight

In deep meditation, the mind can drop the perception of sequence; experience becomes still but awake. That isn’t cosmic magic—it’s what happens when the usual markers of memory and anticipation go quiet.

Physics has no mystical interest here, but in timeless equations like those of quantum gravity, time also vanishes from the math. Both hint that sequence might be secondary, not fundamental.

5. Freedom from time

For physics, that’s just description—laws that don’t need a ticking parameter.

For Buddhism, it’s liberation: when you stop grasping at moments, suffering loses its foothold.

The scientist studies the map; the meditator explores the terrain.

Different instruments, same mystery.
