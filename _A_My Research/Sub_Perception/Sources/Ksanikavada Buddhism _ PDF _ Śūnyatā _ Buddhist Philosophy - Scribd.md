### Kṣaṇikavāda (Theory of Momentariness)

Kṣaṇikavāda, or the Theory of Momentariness, is one of the most profound and radical doctrines in early
Buddhist philosophy. It is an ontological, epistemological, and soteriological doctrine that not only critiques
the metaphysical realism of eternal substance but also serves as a tool for the liberation of sentient beings.
Rooted in the Buddha’s existential concern with duḥkha (suffering) and his rejection of metaphysical
speculation, Kṣaṇikavāda finds its basis in the cardinal doctrine of Pratītyasamutpāda (Dependent
Origination) and unfolds into a highly systematic account of impermanence (anityatā).

#### Ontological Core: Process vs Substance

Kṣaṇikavāda posits a process metaphysics. All saṃskṛta dharmas (conditioned entities) arise due to
causes and perish in the next moment. They exist only for a partless moment (kṣaṇa). Unlike substance
ontologies (e.g., Vedāntic ātman or Nyāya's dravya), which posit enduring, self-subsistent entities with fixed
svabhāva (essence), Buddhism denies any such inherent nature. Reality, for Buddhism, is not composed of
“things” but of events, which lack ontological permanence.
This view aligns with ontological nominalism and event ontology in modern metaphysics, where the
world is conceived as a stream of causally-connected events rather than substances.

#### Psychological Basis: Vijñāna-Santāna (Stream of Consciousness)

#### Initially, momentariness applied primarily to consciousness (vijñāna). According to early Abhidhamma

thinkers, the so-called continuity of experience is actually a causally connected sequence of discrete
mental states. Each thought, emotion, or perception arises due to specific conditions and disappears
instantly. The so-called “self” is a convenient prajñapti (designation) for this momentary succession.
Later schools, especially Sarvāstivāda and Sautrāntika, applied Kṣaṇikavāda to material phenomena
(rūpa) as well, declaring that all material entities are also fluxional. Even atoms (paramāṇu) are
momentary, and all phenomena are dharmas that arise and perish in a blink.

#### Epistemological Grounding: Artha-Kriyā-Kāritva (Causal Efficacy)

A major argument for momentariness is artha-kriyā-kāritva—the idea that the mark of reality (sattva) is
the ability to perform a function. If something exists, it must have causal efficacy. But efficacy implies
change—i.e., the capacity to affect or be affected. That which never changes (e.g., a changeless soul or
eternal God) can do nothing and is therefore not real.
Thus, permanence is not a strength but a metaphysical liability. This insight shifts Buddhist thought toward
a pragmatist realism: something is real not because it endures, but because it does work in the world.
1
Ethical-Soteriological Utility: Anti-Attachment Praxis
The teaching of Kṣaṇikavāda is not merely speculative—it is normative and pragmatic. Buddha’s intent
was to dismantle attachment (upādāna) and liberate the mind from clinging to illusions of
permanence. If everything is momentary, there is no stable “self” to suffer or rejoice, no possession to be
owned, no pain that is eternal.
This becomes a method of vipassanā (insight meditation): observe phenomena arising and passing away
to internalize their impermanence and cultivate non-attachment (vairāgya). Kṣaṇikavāda thus becomes
an epistemic practice and ethical injunction.

#### Illustrative Similes: Flux and Illusion of Continuity

1. Flame of the Lamp – The lamp appears steady, but it is a series of flickers.
2. River of Heraclitus – You never step into the same river twice; the waters (and you) have changed.
   These are classic metaphors for phenomenal continuity without ontological persistence. This view
   destabilizes the notion of substance and supports a phenomenological ontology.

#### Twelve Nidānas and Temporal Interdependence

#### In the structure of dvādaśāṅga-pratītyasamutpāda (Twelve Links of Dependent Origination), each link

arises momentarily and causally: - Past causes: Avidyā (ignorance), Saṃskāra (dispositions) - Present
processes: Vijñāna, Nāma-rūpa, Vedanā, Tṛṣṇā - Future effects: Jāti (birth), Jarā-maraṇa (death)
These links connect the three lifetimes (past, present, future), demonstrating how karmic continuity is
maintained without a transmigrating soul. It’s a radical middle path between annihilationism and
eternalism.

#### Nairātmyavāda: Denial of Permanent Soul

Kṣaṇikavāda logically entails nairātmya (no-self doctrine). The supposed “self” is merely a conventionally
constructed identity over five impermanent skandhas: - Rūpa – Matter - Vedanā – Sensation - Saṃjñā –
Perception - Saṃskāra – Disposition - Vijñāna – Consciousness
This critique rejects ātman-vāda on several grounds: 1. Immutables can’t act: A changeless self cannot
bind or liberate itself. 2. No rebirth: Eternal self cannot reincarnate. 3. No moral agency: Ethical action
implies change, intention, and transformation.
Instead, the bundle theory of self emerges: the person is a temporary aggregate, not a metaphysical
substratum.
2
Philosophical Debates and Responses
• Vedāntins accuse Buddhists of nihilism (śūnya-vāda). They argue that knowledge, memory, and
ethics require an enduring self.
• Jainas reject Kṣaṇikavāda in favor of anekāntavāda—a more moderate pluralism that accepts both
permanence (guna) and change (paryāya).
• Buddhists defend their position via the notion of dharma-momentariness and yogic pratyakṣa
(direct meditative knowledge). They claim that only through deep meditative insight can one
directly perceive the rise-and-fall of all dharmas.

#### Evolution in Mahāyāna: From Momentariness to Emptiness

#### • Mādhyamika (Nāgārjuna) radicalizes Kṣaṇikavāda with śūnyatā (emptiness)—even momentary

entities lack intrinsic nature (niḥsvabhāvatā).
• Yogācāra preserves momentariness but reframes reality as vijñāna-mātra (consciousness-only).
External objects are illusory, but streams of momentary consciousness continue.

#### Conclusion: From Metaphysics to Liberation

Kṣaṇikavāda is not merely a metaphysical hypothesis but a transformative insight. It shifts the seeker’s
focus from clinging to reality to seeing-through its impermanence. In doing so, it becomes an epistemic
discipline, a moral vision, and a liberatory praxis.
It breaks the illusion of permanence and prepares the mind for prajñā (wisdom) and nirvāṇa (liberation)
—the ultimate goals of the Buddhist path.
