The 5 Basic Assumptions

- Concentration: The ability to focus your attention on what you decide is relevant in the present moment.
- Sensory Clarity: The ability to clearly and precisely observe the components of your sensory experience without getting lost in them.
- Equanimity: The ability to allow your experience to be as it is without fighting, resisting, or clinging to it.
- Recycle the Reaction: If practicing the first three skills brings up something difficult or challenging, you use that very reaction as your new focus of meditation.
- Contact a Competent Guide: If you become overwhelmed or forget how to apply the first four axioms, you reach out to an experienced teacher or guide for help. These assumptions come from [Shinzen Young’s](https://www.google.com/search?q=shinzen+young%E2%80%99s&kgmid=/m/0bp_xs) lifelong synthesis of classical Buddhist meditation traditions and his background in mathematics and science. He formulated them to serve as a foundational, algorithmic framework for his [Unified Mindfulness system](https://unifiedmindfulness.com/a-qa-with-shinzen-young/). [1, 2]

## The Core Origins

- Classical Buddhist Roots: The first three assumptions (Concentration, Sensory Clarity, and Equanimity) represent his modern distillation of traditional Buddhist practices. Concentration comes from _Samadhi_, Clarity from _Vipassana_ (insight), and Equanimity from _Upekkha_. [1]
- The "Triple Skill-Set": Shinzen defines mindfulness not as a vague state of mind, but mathematically as the mathematical product of these three specific attention skills working together. [3]
- Algorithmic Architecture: Shinzen’s background in math led him to structure meditation like a computer script or a clinical algorithm. Assumptions 4 and 5 function as "if/then" troubleshooting logic to ensure the practitioner never gets stuck during a meditation session:
    
    - _If_ the first three skills trigger a difficult emotional reaction, _then_ you recycle the reaction into your focus.
    - _If_ you get too overwhelmed to do that, _then_ you contact a guide. [1, 4]

In Shinzen Young's Unified Mindfulness system, Recycling the Reaction transforms physical pain by ==treating the mental and emotional resistance to the pain as the primary object of meditation==. By applying concentration, clarity, and equanimity to internal dialogue, mental images, and bodily tension, the practice dissolves the psychological backlash that multiplies suffering.

Sensory Clarity breaks down overwhelming emotional states by ==separating them into three distinct sensory channels: visual mental imagery (See In), internal self-talk (Hear In), and physical body sensations (Feel In)==. This divide-and-conquer strategy reduces psychological load, breaks escalating mental loops, and reveals the impermanent nature of emotions. Here is a 15-minute daily practice script based on Shinzen Young’s 5 Basic Assumptions and his See, Hear, Feel framework.

## Preparation (1 Minute)

- Posture: Sit comfortably upright. Keep your spine straight but relaxed.
- Intent: Your goal is not to stop your thoughts or pain, but to track them with Concentration, Sensory Clarity, and Equanimity.

---

## Step 1: Scan the Three Channels (3 Minutes)

Close your eyes and systematically check your internal sensory landscape to build Sensory Clarity.

- See In (Visual): Look at the back of your eyelids. Notice any mental images, shapes, or memories floating by. If it is blank, notice the restful blank space.
- Hear In (Auditory): Listen to your inner world. Notice your internal self-talk, a replayed song, or the quiet gaps between thoughts.
- Feel In (Somatic): Scan your body. Notice physical emotional signatures—like tightness in the chest, a flutter in the stomach, or warmth in the hands.

---

## Step 2: Choose a Focus & Note (6 Minutes)

Pick one channel that feels the most neutral or interesting right now. Rest your attention there to build Concentration. Apply Equanimity by letting the sensations do whatever they want.

- Every 3 to 5 seconds, labels what you experience out loud or silently in your mind:
    
    - If tracking mental images, label it: "See In."
    - If tracking inner talk, label it: "Hear In."
    - If tracking a body sensation, label it: "Feel In."
    
- After each label, savor or soak into that sensation for a few seconds before labeling again.

---

## Step 3: Recycle the Reaction if Pain/Resistance Arises (4 Minutes)

If a difficult physical sensation (pain) or an emotional reaction (frustration, anxiety) arises, immediately trigger Assumption #4: Recycle the Reaction.

- Divide the suffering: Do not look at the pain as a solid block. Use Sensory Clarity to break it apart.
- Isolate the Body: Feel the raw physical sensation of the pain. Label it "Feel In." Soften around it. Let it pulse, burn, or ache without fighting it.
- Isolate the Mind: Look for your mind's _reaction_ to the pain.
    
    - Are there catastrophic thoughts? Label them "Hear In."
    - Are there mental images of worst-case scenarios? Label them "See In."
    
- The Script: _"I am experiencing physical discomfort. I am separating the physical sensation from my mental worry. I am focusing entirely on the raw physical pulse right now... Feel In... Feel In."_

---

## Step 4: Grounding & Post-Meditation (1 Minute)

- Open your eyes slowly.
- Gently look at your surroundings (See Out), listen to ambient room sounds (Hear Out), and feel your feet on the floor (Feel Out).
- Assumption #5 Reminder: If you found this session completely overwhelming, or if you got stuck in a loop of high anxiety or severe pain, make a note to contact a competent guide or teacher to help adjust your technique.
