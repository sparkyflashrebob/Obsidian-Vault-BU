Buddhist Logic
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi

_**Catuṣkoṭi**_ ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"); [Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari "Devanagari"): चतुष्कोटि, [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script "Tibetan script"): མུ་བཞི, [Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wylie_transliteration "Wylie transliteration"): _mu bzhi_, [Sinhalese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinhala_language "Sinhala language"):චතුස්කෝටිකය) refers to logical argument(s) of a 'suite of four discrete functions' or 'an indivisible quaternity' that has multiple applications and has been important in the [Indian logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_logic "Indian logic") and the [Buddhist logico-epistemological traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_logic "Buddhist logic"), particularly those of the [Madhyamaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhyamaka "Madhyamaka") school.

In particular, the catuṣkoṭi is a "four-cornered" system of argumentation that involves the systematic examination of each of the 4 possibilities of a proposition, _P_:

1. _P_; that is being.
2. not _P_; that is not being.
3. _P_ and not _P_; that is being and that is not being.
4. not (_P_ or not _P_); that is neither not being nor is that being.

These four statements hold the following properties: (1) each alternative is mutually exclusive (that is, one of, but no more than one of, the four statements is true) and (2) that all the alternatives are together exhaustive (that is, at least one of them must necessarily be true).[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJayatilleke1967-1) This system of logic not only provides a novel method of classifying propositions into logical alternatives, but also because it does so in such a manner that the alternatives are not dependent on the number of truth-values assumed in the system of logic.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJayatilleke1967-1)

An example of a _Catuṣkoṭi_ using the arbitrary proposition, "Animals understand love" as _P_ would be:

1. Animals understand love
2. Animals do not understand love
3. Animals both do and do not understand love
4. Animals neither do nor do not understand love

### Early Buddhism[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Early Buddhism")]

[Śākyamuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C4%81kyamuni "Śākyamuni"), as remembered by [Ānanda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananda "Ananda") and codified in the _[Brahmajala Sutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmajala_Sutta_(Theravada) "Brahmajala Sutta (Theravada)")_ 2.27, when expounding the sixteenth wrong view, or the fourth wrong view of the ['Eel-Wrigglers' (Pali: amarā-vikheppikā)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjaya_Belatthaputta "Sanjaya Belatthaputta"), the non-committal equivocators who adhered to [Ajñana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aj%C3%B1ana "Ajñana"), the sceptical philosophy, though the grammatical structure is identical to the Catuṣkoṭi (and there are numerous other analogues of this fourfold grammatical structure within this Sutta), the intentionality of the architecture employed by Nagarjuna is not evident, as rendered into English by [Walshe](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Maurice_Walshe&action=edit&redlink=1 "Maurice Walshe (page does not exist)") (1987, 1995: p. 81):

> 'What is the fourth way? Here, an ascetic or Brahmin is dull and stupid. Because of his dullness and stupidity, when he is questioned he resorts to evasive statements and wriggles like an eel: "If you ask me whether there is another world. But I don't say so. And I don't say otherwise. And I don't say it is not, and I don't not say it is not." "Is there no other world?..." "Is there both another world and no other world?..."Is there neither another world nor no other world?..." "Are there spontaneously-born beings?..." "Are there not...?" "Both...? "Neither...?" "Does the Tathagata exist after death? Does he not exist after death? Does he both exist and not exist after death? Does he neither exist nor not exist after death?..." "If I thought so, I would say so...I don't say so...I don't say it is not." This is the fourth case.'[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-3)


### Nagarjuna[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Nagarjuna")]

The Catuṣkoṭi was employed particularly by [Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna "Nagarjuna") who developed it and engaged it as a 'learning, investigative, meditative'[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-5) portal to realize the 'openness' (Sanskrit: [Śūnyatā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C5%ABnyat%C4%81 "Śūnyatā")), of Shakyamuni's [Second Turning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Turning "Second Turning") of the [Dharmacakra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmacakra "Dharmacakra"), as categorized by the _[Sandhinirmocana Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandhinirmocana_Sutra "Sandhinirmocana Sutra")_.

Robinson (1957: p. 294), building on the foundations of [Liebenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Liebenthal "Walter Liebenthal") (1948)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-6) to whom he gives credit, states:

> What Nagarjuna wishes to prove is the irrationality of Existence, or the falsehood of reasoning which is built upon the logical principle that A equals A.... Because two answers, assertion and denial, are always possible to a given question, his arguments contain two refutations, one denying the presence, one the absence of the probandum. **This double refutation is called the Middle Path.** [_emphasis evident in Robinson_][[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-Robinson,_Richard_H._1957_p.294-7)

[Śūnyatā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C5%ABnyat%C4%81 "Śūnyatā") is the ninth '[view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_(Buddhism) "View (Buddhism)")' (Sanskrit: dṛṣṭi), the viewless view, a [superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superposition_principle "Superposition principle") of the eight possible arrays of proposition P [and its 'inseparable contradistinction' (Sanskrit: [apoha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoha "Apoha"))].

|   |   |   |   |   |
|---|---|---|---|---|
||_**Positive configuration**_<br><br>1. **P**<br>2. **Not-P**<br>3. **Both P and Not-P**<br>4. **Neither P nor Not-P**|||_**Negative configuration**_<br><br>1. **Not (P)**<br>2. **Not (Not-P)**<br>3. **Not (Both P and Not-P)**<br>4. **Not (Neither P nor Not-P)**|

The eight arrays or octaves of the iconographic [Dharmacakra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmacakra "Dharmacakra") represent [drishti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_(Buddhism) "View (Buddhism)") or traditional views that [Shakyamuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakyamuni "Shakyamuni") countered. These eight arrays may be [plotted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plot_(graphics) "Plot (graphics)") as [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates "Coordinates") on a multidimensional field which may be rendered as a [sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere "Sphere"), a [mandala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala "Mandala"), a multidimensional shunya or zero where shunyata denotes zero-ness. The eight arrays are in a [concordant](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/concordance "wikt:concordance") relationship where they each constitute a [chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_(geometry) "Chord (geometry)") to the sphere. The coordinates are [equidistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidistant "Equidistant") from the [epicentre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicentre "Epicentre") of shunya where the array of the positive configuration (or hemisphere) and the array of the negative configuration (or hemisphere) constitute two polar [radii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radii "Radii") or diametrical complements, a [diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diameter "Diameter") in sum. These are the 'eight limits' (Wylie: mtha' brgyad; Sanskrit: aṣṭānta) of 'openness' (Sanskrit: śūnyatā),[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-8) where śūnyatā is amplified by 'freedom from constructs' or 'simplicity' (Wylie: spros bral; Sanskrit: [aprapañca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_Proliferation#Aprapa%C3%B1ca "Conceptual Proliferation")).[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-10) [Karmay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samten_Gyaltsen_Karmay "Samten Gyaltsen Karmay") (1988: p. 118) conveys that 'spros bral' is a homologue of 'thig le' (Sanskrit: [bindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bindu_(symbol) "Bindu (symbol)")), where 'spros bral' is literally "without amplification", understood as "that which cannot be displayed".[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-11)

1. P is true ``1 P is not true or Not P is true
2. Not P is true ``2. Not (Not P) is true i.e. P is true
3. Both P and Not P are true i.e. the universal set `` 3 Neither P nor not P are true i.e. it is a null set
4. Neither P nor not P are true it is a null set `` 4. Not (neither p nor not P are true ) = both P and not P are true which is the universal set.

Thus, a four-fold configuration resurfaces, with positive and negative configurations being mere rewritten alternatives (ie. it makes no difference whether one works with the positive or negative catuskoti). For example, if one replaces P with Not P, then the positive configuration set for Not P will be functionally equivalent to the negative configuration of P.

[Sanjaya Belatthiputta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanjaya_Belatthiputta "Sanjaya Belatthiputta"), a 6th century BCE Indian ascetic whose teachings are similar to that of Nagarjuna are compared to that of a "theory of eel-wrigglers" in the famous [Samannaphala Sutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samannaphala_Sutta "Samannaphala Sutta") (DN 2).[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-12)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-13) Sanjaya is recorded as saying:

'If you ask me if there exists another world [after death], if I thought that there exists another world, would I declare that to you? I don't think so. I don't think in that way. I don't think otherwise. I don't think not. I don't think not not. If you asked me if there isn't another world... both is and isn't... neither is nor isn't... if there are beings who transmigrate... if there aren't... both are and aren't... neither are nor aren't... if the [Tathagata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tathagata "Tathagata") exists after death... doesn't... both... neither exists nor exists after death, would I declare that to you? I don't think so. I don't think in that way. I don't think otherwise. I don't think not. I don't think not not.'[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-14)
