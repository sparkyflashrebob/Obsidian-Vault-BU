## Highlights
- symbolic and a propositional function ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13h836h78vyvhwscy9hgsnn))
- Symbolically, they represent the awakening and liberation of the Buddha, and of the potential for his followers to reach the same liberation and freedom as him ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13h8bnhbykyk82eg0nx4gwb))
- As propositions, the Four Truths are a conceptual framework that appear in the [Pali canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali_canon) and early Hybrid [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) Buddhist scriptures,[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson199955,_Quote:_"The_four_noble_truths_are_an_important_part_of_the_Buddha's_biography_that_is_recorded_partially_in_the_Pali_Tipitaka_as_well_as_in_the_Tripitaka_recorded_in_Buddhist_Hybrid_Sanskrit._[..._They]_were_at_the_center_of_a_specific_set_of_teaching_about_the_Buddha,_his_teachings,_and_the_path."-25) as a part of the broader "network of teachings"[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson200185-26) (the "*[dhamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma#Buddhism)* matrix"),[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson200186-27) which have to be taken together.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson200185-26) They provide a conceptual framework for introducing and explaining Buddhist thought, which has to be personally understood or "experienced ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13h8n0f6b4se59j327tvvb9))
- As a proposition, the four truths defy an exact definition, but refer to and express the basic orientation of [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13h903hb4rhgeee23atwk49))
- unguarded sensory contact [gives rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prat%C4%ABtyasamutp%C4%81da) to [craving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%E1%B9%87h%C4%81) and [clinging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up%C4%81d%C4%81na) to [impermanent states and things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%85kh%C4%81ra#Conditioned_things),[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTENyanatiloka198065-31) which are *dukkha*,[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKhantipalo200341-32) "unsatisfactory,"[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnalayo2013b-33) "incapable of satisfying"[[web 4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-Sumedho-first-34) and painful. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13h9f435y5jgvrj06bwr27p))
- This craving keeps us caught in *[saṃsāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra_(Buddhism))*,[[note 4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-Samudaya-37) "wandering", usually interpreted as the endless cycle of repeated [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebirth_(Buddhism)),[[note 5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-"ego-rebirth-40) and the continued *dukkha* that comes with it,[[note 6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-Samsara-41) but also referring to the endless cycle of attraction and rejection that perpetuates the ego-mind ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13h9tfdbr2ycz7ah1ds5y2m))
- here is a way to [end this cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha),[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWarder199945–46-42)[[note 7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-Pleasure-43) namely by attaining *[nirvana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana_(Buddhism))*, cessation of craving, whereafter rebirth and the accompanying *dukkha* will no longer arise again ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13ha4d814pc80rttptebhtf))
- This can be accomplished by following the [eightfold path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path),[[note 2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-Moksha-21) confining our automatic responses to sensory contact by restraining oneself, cultivating discipline and wholesome states, and practicing [mindfulness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindfulness) and *[dhyana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhyana_in_Buddhism)* (meditation).[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTERaju1985147–151-46)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEliot201439–41-47) ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13hadj2hv4jj89v3rwr1mxb))
- *[prajna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_in_Buddhism)*, or "liberating insight", came to be regarded as liberating in itself ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13hc3g1fmc0kpa0pkwkv2wg))
- This "liberating insight" gained a prominent place in the sutras, and the four truths came to represent this liberating insight, ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13hcgfrgm6t52tsrcj3q0xe))
- The four truths grew to be of central importance in the [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) tradition of Buddhism by about the 5th-century CE,[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson199955–56-53)[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson1999230–231-54) which holds that the insight into the four truths is liberating in itself. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13hdanqxd9pk8tcwa97yzeb))
- They are less prominent in the [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana) tradition, which sees the higher aims of insight into *[sunyata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunyata)*, emptiness, and following the [Bodhisattva path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva_path) as central elements in their teachings and practice ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01h13hd4mpdd632ksshszvgtdm))
