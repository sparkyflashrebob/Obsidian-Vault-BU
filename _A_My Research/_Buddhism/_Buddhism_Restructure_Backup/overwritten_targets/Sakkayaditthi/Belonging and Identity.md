Whether I feel tired or invigorated, or even what I would think as “just normal” in terms of the body. Where my body feels okay.  Nothing is standing out.

As far as my mind is concerned that is connected to how my body feels to a certain extent. If the body feels normal then that is good. A mood is an emotion - which is categorized in the 4th Foundation of Mindfulness, which is experienced in the body, that settles in the mind. It becomes our state of mind.

FROM TARA BRACH ON USING RAIN

Now, another question that I was really glad you asked, and I probably would have-- because it came up, so many different people in different ways-- I'll read one of them: "The meaning of N seems to have evolved through the years and meaning 'Not identified' and 'Nurture.' Can you explain the evolution and why it has changed?"
So this is a little bit of history. I first learned about RAIN about-- it was I think 20 years ago. And the way it was presented was "Recognize, Allow, Investigate, and Non-Identification." And actually, I taught and practiced for a number of years. And what I did was when I got to the investigation, I slipped in "Investigate with kindness," because I wanted to bring in the wing of the heart, because it wasn't there in the initial acronym.
But in my own experience, when I was really emotionally stuck, I needed to really go for full on self-compassion. And I got the same feedback from others that using the acronym as it was taught didn't cut through the deep self-aversion or deep feelings of fear and anger. People couldn't investigate, and then go right into feeling not identified. It just didn't-- it didn't work that way.
So what became clear to me is that most big emotional tangles need a dedicated step of self-compassion. We need the two wings in RAIN-- to Recognize is the beginning of the wing of mindfulness. Allow is the beginning of the wing of the heart, making space. And then, they spiral, and then Investigate deepens mindfulness. And we need Nurture, a full blown quality of heart for most big tangles.
So for that reason, I shifted from non-identification to nurture. But the other feedback I got was people would always ask me, "What is-- how do you not identify?" And what I had to explain was that while each of the steps-- like Recognize takes some intentionality; and Allow, we allow, we make room for; and we can Investigate and ask questions; Non-identification isn't a doing. It's actually the gift of RAIN. It's the freedom that comes.

And so what people were describing when they were doing RAIN was that they would-- and what they found was they would do it in the old way without Nurture, and because they were kind of, in some way, bypassing compassion and vulnerability, when they got-- Non-identification was kind of what I call a dry emptiness. There was a sense of, "Well, I am not my hatred, or I'm not my anger." But it wasn't embodied.
And I'll give you an example of what the difference between the old and the revised. With one couple that were both meditating, I met with them. And he traveled a lot, and he was very immersed in his career. And when he was doing a lot of traveling, his wife had an affair. And so they were talking to me, and the challenge was that he claimed he forgave her, and he wanted to move forward.
But she kept saying, "He's still pushing me away and blaming me and mistrustful. And I want to get help, but he's unwilling." And he would say, "No, I've been doing RAIN. And I can recognize and allow what's going on, and I can investigate it. And then I was not-- then I'm not identified. Then I can tell that I am not my hurt, and I am not my anger, and I'm ready to move on.
So I had him do some more RAIN, but I had him deepen the investigation until he got in touch with real vulnerability, and then begin to offer kindness to it. And that broke him open, because as he did that, he started really registering the hugeness of his feelings of hurt and grief and was willing to go into couples therapy. And gradually, as he was doing RAIN, he actually could go from a feeling of self-compassion to opening up to her, compassion for her, and then to a true sense of non-identification, which is really realizing, we're not any of those small parts of self, and we're one with everything-- not identified with separate self and one with everything.
He could feel that tenderness and that field of belonging with her. So what I discovered was that if we practice Recognize, Allow, Investigate, and Nurture, the gift of that is non-identification. And I created a kind of pause after RAIN, where I call it "After the RAIN."

belonging is essential to humans. [Psychologists rank our need to belong on par with our need for love.](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/10.5406/amerjpsyc.126.2.0155.pdf?refreqid=excelsior%3Aeaddc723f4ef43a1878cce2f274aff52) The need to belong is universal and fundamental,

much of what human beings do is done in the service of belongingness.

The human need for [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Need_for_power), [intimacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intimacy), approval, [achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Need_for_achievement) and [affiliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Need_for_affiliation), are all driven by the need to belong. Human culture is compelled and conditioned by pressure to belong.
The need to belong and form [attachments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attachment_theory) is universal among humans.
Those who believe that the need to belong is the major psychological drive also believe that humans are naturally driven toward establishing and sustaining relationships and belongingness.
Certain people who are socially deprived can exhibit physical, behavioral, and psychological problems, such as stress or instability.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belongingness#cite_note-:2-1)

When People form close attachments with one another they are hesitant in breaking social bonds.
Even to the extent that they can be hesitant to dissolve even bad relationships that could be potentially destructive. Breaking off an attachment causes pain that is deeply rooted in the need to belong.
One reason for the need to belong is based on the theory of [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_psychology). In the past belonging to a group was essential to survival: people hunted and cooked in [groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_group). Belonging to a group allowed tribe members to share the workload and protect each other. Not only were they trying to ensure their own survival, but all members of their tribe were invested in each other's outcomes because each member played an important role in the group. More recently in Western society, this is not necessarily the case. Most people no longer belong to tribes, but they still protect those in their groups and still have a desire to belong in groups.
To be accepted within a group, some individuals may convey or conceal certain parts of their [personalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality). Certain aspects of one's [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality) may not be seen as desirable or essential to the group, so people try to convey what they interpret as valuable to the group.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belongingness#cite_note-Baumeister-4)

The desire to form and maintain social bonds is among the most powerful human motives. If an individual's sense of social connectedness is threatened, their ability to self-regulate suffers

Group membership can involve [conformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformity). Conformity is the act of changing one's actions, attitudes, and behaviors to match the norms of others which are unsaid rules that are shared by a group. The tendency to conform results from direct and indirect social pressures occurring in whole societies and in small groups.
If someone is in a situation where they do not know the right way to behave, they look at the cues of others to correct their own behavior. We will look at our Peers for guidance

When our belongingness needs are not met, self-regulation is used to fulfill one's need to belong.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belongingness#cite_note-20) This is defined as the process of regulating oneself, or changing one's behavior, to manage short-term desires according to the [self-regulation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-regulation_theory).

The need to belong is among the most fundamental of all personality processes. Given the negative consequences of [social rejection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_rejection), people developed traits that function to encourage acceptance and to prevent rejection.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belongingness#cite_note-:2-1) But if the need to belong evolved to provide people with a means of meeting their basic needs for survival and reproduction based on evolutionary experiences, thwarting the need to belong should affect a variety of outcomes.
