## In Buddhism, how does desire and hate interact?

In Buddhism, desire and hate are considered two sides of the same coin, both arising from a fundamental misunderstanding of reality.

**Desire:**

- **Craving:** It stems from an illusion of inherent satisfaction in external things and experiences. We chase after desires, believing they will bring happiness, but the fulfillment is always temporary, leading to further craving and dissatisfaction.
- **Attachment:** Clinging to desires creates attachment, which breeds fear of loss and suffering when things don't go as planned. This attachment fuels:
    - **Aversion:** When desired things are threatened or unattainable, aversion arises. We dislike, push away, or even hate what stands in the way of our desires.
    - **Delusion:** Clinging to desire distorts our perception, leading us to believe impermanent things are permanent and separate from ourselves, further fueling the cycle of craving and aversion.

**Hate:**

- **Aversion's extreme:** Hate is the intensified form of aversion, characterized by anger, hostility, and ill will towards what we perceive as obstacles to our desires.
- **Distorted view:** Hate stems from a misunderstanding of ourselves and others. We see ourselves as separate and independent entities, leading to a sense of "us" versus "them," fueling conflict and animosity.
- **Source of suffering:** Hate brings immense suffering, not just to the object of hatred but also to ourselves. It consumes us with negativity and prevents genuine happiness and peace.

**Breaking the cycle:**

Buddhism teaches that the path to liberation lies in breaking the cycle of desire and hate. This involves:

- **Recognizing the illusion of self:** Through meditation and mindfulness, we see the impermanent and interconnected nature of reality, dissolving the illusion of a separate self and its desires.
- **Cultivating non-attachment:** We learn to let go of cravings and attachments, appreciating experiences without clinging to them.
- **Developing compassion:** By understanding the interconnectedness of all beings, we cultivate empathy and compassion, replacing hate with understanding and forgiveness.

By understanding the interplay of desire and hate, we can work towards dismantling the roots of suffering and cultivate a life of inner peace and wisdom.

Remember, this is just a brief overview, and the Buddhist teachings on desire and hate are vast and nuanced. If you'd like to learn more, I recommend exploring resources from reputable Buddhist teachers and institutions.

## Are there specific suttas where the buddha discussed this?

Absolutely! The Buddha frequently discussed the interplay of desire and hate in various suttas, offering insightful teachings on their nature and how to overcome them. Here are a few examples:

**Suttas on the Three Poisons:**

- **Rathakarama Sutta (AN 3.90):** This sutta compares desire, hate, and delusion to three robbers lurking in the forest of the mind, waiting to attack and cause suffering.
- **Kalama Sutta (AN 10.1):** The Buddha advises the Kalamas not to get caught up in views about desire and hate, but to investigate and understand their true nature for themselves.
- **Cula-kammavibhanga Sutta (MN 135):** This sutta explains how the ten unwholesome actions are rooted in desire, hate, and delusion, and how practicing their opposites leads to liberation.

**Suttas on Desire and Attachment:**

- **Samyutta Nikaya 12.56:** This sutta describes the "fire of desire" that consumes all it encounters, leading to suffering and bondage.
- **Udana 8.1:** The Buddha compares clinging to desire to a monkey caught in molasses, struggling and suffering as it tries to escape.
- **Majjhima Nikaya 129:** This sutta encourages abandoning attachment to both pleasurable and painful experiences, leading to liberation from the cycle of craving and aversion.

**Suttas on Hate and Aversion:**

- **Metta Sutta (AN 1.3)** This well-known sutta teaches the practice of loving-kindness (Metta) as an antidote to hate and aversion.
- **Angulimala Sutta (MN 82)** This sutta narrates the story of Angulimala, a notorious murderer who transformed through the Buddha's teachings, highlighting the potential for even the most hateful mind to change.
- **Itivuttaka 42:** This sutta describes how hatred burns like a fire, consuming both the hater and the hated.

These are just a few examples, and many other suttas explore the themes of desire and hate in different contexts. I encourage you to explore further based on your specific interests. Remember, the Buddha's teachings offer a profound understanding of these mental states and provide practical guidance for cultivating a peaceful and liberated mind.
