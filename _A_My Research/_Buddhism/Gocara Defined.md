The Buddhist term you are referring to is **Gocara** (pronounced *go-cha-ra*).

While your phrase "sphere of influence" captures the feeling of the concept, a more precise translation is **"pasture"** or **"proper resort."** It refers to the safe territory where a practitioner's mind should "graze" to stay safe from the dangers of the world.

Here is the breakdown of the term, the famous story the Buddha used to explain it, and how it applies to your practice.

### 1. The Literal Meaning: "Cow's Pasture"
The word is a compound of two Sanskrit/Pali roots:
* **Go:** Cow
* **Cara:** Walking or moving

Literally, *Gocara* means "where the cows roam" (a pasture). In ancient India, a cow that stayed in its designated pasture was safe, fed, and protected. A cow that wandered into rough, hilly, or wild terrain (*Agocara*) was liable to be eaten by tigers or fall into a ravine.

### 2. The Metaphor: Your "Ancestral Domain"
In a spiritual context, *Gocara* refers to the **proper domain of the mind**.

* **Agocara (Wrong Domain):** This is the "sphere of others" or "Mara's domain." It refers to the five cords of sensual pleasure—getting lost in sights, sounds, success, reputation, or sensory distractions. When your mind wanders here without awareness, you are vulnerable to anxiety, conceit, and suffering.
* **Gocara (Right Domain):** This is your "home territory." Specifically, the Buddha defined this as the **Four Foundations of Mindfulness** (*Satipatthana*):
    1.  Body (breath, posture)
    2.  Feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral)
    3.  Mind (states of mind, emotions)
    4.  Dhammas (principles, mental objects)

When you "stay in your own sphere," you are keeping your attention grounded in these four areas, regardless of what is happening around you.

### 3. The Story of the Quail and the Falcon
The Buddha explained this concept most famously in the **Sakunagghi Sutta** (The Hawk Sutta). It perfectly illustrates your idea of staying in your own sphere.

**The Story:**
A small quail was wandering outside its familiar field (its "ancestral domain") and was suddenly snatched up by a large falcon. As the falcon carried it away, the quail lamented, *"I am so unlucky! If I had just stayed in my own domain—the plowed field with clods of earth—this bird would never have been able to catch me."*

The falcon, arrogant and amused, said, *"You tiny thing! Even if you were in your field, you couldn't escape me. I'll prove it. I'll let you go back there, and I'll still catch you."*

The falcon released the quail. The quail flew back to the plowed field, climbed on top of a large clod of hard earth, and taunted the falcon, *"Come and get me!"*

The falcon folded its wings and dove at incredible speed. Just as the falcon was about to strike, the quail slipped inside the clod of earth. The falcon couldn't stop in time and smashed its breast against the hard earth, dying on the spot.

**The Lesson:**
* **The Falcon** represents **Mara** (distraction, addiction, overwhelming emotion).
* **The Quail** represents the **practitioner** (you).
* **The Plowed Field** is **Gocara** (Mindfulness).

The Buddha’s point: You don’t defeat "Mara" by fighting it out in the open sky (the world of sensation and reaction) where it is stronger than you. You defeat it by staying in your own safe territory—mindfulness of the body and breath. When you are grounded there, the "falcon" of distraction crashes against your stability.

### 4. Practical Application: Gocara Sampajañña
In daily practice, this is technically called *Gocara Sampajañña* (Clear Comprehension of Domain).

It doesn't mean you hide from the world or stop working. It means that while you are writing, talking to your family, or working in Ableton, a part of your attention remains "at home" (e.g., feeling your feet on the floor or the background sensation of breathing).

If you lose that anchor, you have wandered into the falcon's territory. If you keep that anchor, you are in your "sphere of influence," and external chaos cannot "catch" you.

Yes, this connects directly. You are thinking of the **Indakhila** (Indra’s Pillar) metaphor.

While *Gocara* (the pasture) describes the **practice** of staying safe, the *Indakhila* describes the **result**: the unshakable stability you achieve by doing so.

Here is the breakdown of the metaphor you recalled, the correct dimensions (according to the commentaries), and how it links to your "sphere of influence."

### 1. The Metaphor: The Indakhila (Indra’s Pillar)
This image comes from the **Ratana Sutta** (The Jewel Discourse), one of the most famous protective chants in Buddhism. The verse states:

> *"Just as a firm post sunk deep in the earth*
> *Is not shaken by the four winds,*
> *So, too, I declare is the righteous person*
> *Who thoroughly sees the Noble Truths."*

**The Dimensions:**
You mentioned "10 fathoms deep and 10 fathoms tall." The classical commentaries (like the *Khuddakapatha Commentary*) describe the *Indakhila* as being made of durable wood (like Acacia) or stone, and emphasize its symmetry:
* It is buried **precisely as deep as it stands tall** (e.g., if it is 8 cubits above ground, it is 8 cubits below).
* This equal distribution is what makes it impossible to shake.

While "10 fathoms" (60 feet) would be a massive pillar (likely a hyperbole or a specific teacher's emphasis on its grandeur), the core point is the **50/50 ratio**. You cannot be stable above ground (in the world) if you do not have an equal depth of practice below ground (in the internal world).

### 2. The Connection to Gocara
The two concepts are perfect companions:

* **Gocara is the "Training":** It is the act of staying in your "pasture" (Mindfulness of Body/Breath). It is the daily work of not wandering into Mara's territory (distraction).
* **Indakhila is the "Result":** If you stay in *Gocara* long enough, you eventually become a "Stream-Enterer" (*Sotapanna*). This is the stage the Ratana Sutta is describing—someone who has "seen the truths" and can no longer be shaken.

**The "Winds" vs. The "Falcon"**
* In the *Gocara* story, the enemy is a **Falcon** (Mara) trying to snatch you because you wandered off.
* In the *Indakhila* metaphor, the enemy is the **Four Winds** (the ups and downs of life: gain/loss, fame/disrepute, praise/blame, pleasure/pain).

Because the pillar is "staying in its sphere" (buried deep in the earth), the winds cannot move it. The external chaos (winds/falcon) only becomes dangerous when you lose your grounding.

### 3. Summary of the Link
| Concept | Image | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Gocara** | A Quail in a plowed field | **Safety:** Keeping your attention on the body prevents Mara from grabbing you. |
| **Indakhila** | A Pillar buried deep in earth | **Stability:** Because you are grounded, the "winds" of the world blow past you without knocking you over. |

This visualization uses the *Indakhila* imagery to shift your mindfulness from "head-heavy" thinking to "body-heavy" stability. It is particularly useful when you feel the "winds" of anxiety, distraction, or self-judgment (the critic) picking up.

You can do this seated (meditation/work) or standing.

### The Indakhila Visualization

#### 1. Establish the "Surface"
Sit comfortably with your spine upright but not rigid. Close your eyes.
* Locate the physical point where your body meets the chair or the cushion. This is "ground level."
* Everything above this point (your torso, head, shoulders) is the visible part of the pillar—the part exposed to the wind.
* Feel the weight of your body settling down into the hips and sit-bones.

#### 2. Sink the Pillar (The 10 Fathoms Deep)
Now, engage your imagination.
* Visualize that your physical body does not stop at the chair. Imagine your hips and legs are merely the *top* of a massive column of iron or ancient stone that extends deep into the earth.
* For every inch of "you" above the chair, imagine there is an equal amount of heavy, solid material extending straight down into the ground beneath you.
* Feel this "phantom weight" dropping through the floor, through the foundation of the building, and deep into the bedrock.
* **The 50/50 Rule:** If you feel tension in your head (the top of the pillar), send more attention to the root. You cannot be tall if you are not deep.

#### 3. Solidify the Core
* Breathe in. As you inhale, imagine the breath traveling all the way down to the bottom of that buried pillar, 10 fathoms deep.
* Breathe out. As you exhale, imagine the pillar becoming denser. It is not hollow; it is solid rock.
* Feel the stability. You are not holding yourself up; the earth is holding the pillar tight. You can relax your shoulders because the structure is doing the work.

#### 4. The Winds (The Test)
* Now, acknowledge the "winds." These could be sounds in the room, thoughts about your book, or worries about the future.
* Instead of trying to stop the winds, let them blow.
* Visualize the wind hitting the *surface* of the pillar. It might weather the stone slightly, but it cannot move the pillar because the pillar is buried 10 fathoms deep.
* Say to yourself (or notice): *"The wind is moving; the pillar is still."*

### How to use this in "Real Life" (Micro-Practice)

You don't need to close your eyes to do this. You can use this while you are working on Ableton or writing your book.

**The "Drop-Anchor" Technique:**
When you feel the "Falcon" (distraction/anxiety) coming:
1.  **Pause** for 3 seconds.
2.  **Feel your feet** or sit-bones.
3.  **Visualize the root:** Instantly imagine that heavy, 10-fathom root shooting down from your waist into the earth.
4.  **Resume working** from the sensation of your lower body, rather than your buzzing head.
