The **Medicare for All Act** proposes establishing a universal, single-payer national health insurance system in the United States, fundamentally shifting the country away from its current multi-payer public and private system [1, 2]. The legislation was first introduced in the House of Representatives by John Conyers in 2003 and was reintroduced in subsequent Congresses by Representative Pramila Jayapal, alongside parallel Senate bills introduced by Senator Bernie Sanders [3, 4].

**Key Provisions and Coverage**
The Act guarantees comprehensive coverage for all U.S. residents, including primary and preventive care, hospital services, dental, vision, mental health, prescription drugs, and long-term care [5-7]. Most notably, the plan **eliminates all cost-sharing**, meaning patients would pay no premiums, deductibles, or copayments at the point of service [6, 7].

To achieve a true single-payer system, the legislation **prohibits private insurance companies from selling coverage that duplicates the benefits provided by the government** [1, 5, 7]. Private insurance would be relegated to supplemental, non-essential coverage, effectively ending employer-sponsored health insurance, Medicare Advantage, and Medigap plans [1, 8, 9].

**Macroeconomic and Budgetary Impacts**
The sources highlight a critical distinction between the plan's impact on *federal spending* versus *overall national healthcare costs*:

* **Increased Federal Spending:** Because the government would absorb virtually all private healthcare costs, federal spending would rise dramatically. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (CRFB) estimates the program would require **$25 trillion to $35 trillion in new federal financing over a decade** [10, 11]. A Mercatus Center study projected a federal spending increase of at least $3.2 trillion [12].
* **Overall System Savings:** Despite higher federal costs, several analyses project that the total amount of money the U.S. spends on healthcare (National Health Consumption Expenditures) would decrease. A study by the Political Economy Research Institute (PERI) at UMASS estimates a **9.6% reduction in overall U.S. healthcare costs, saving $5.1 trillion over ten years** [13, 14]. Similarly, Physicians for a National Health Program estimated $350 billion in immediate annual savings, and a 2020 *Lancet* study projected a 13% drop in national healthcare expenditures (saving over $450 billion annually) while preventing 68,500 deaths each year [15, 16]. These savings would be driven by massive reductions in administrative overhead, the negotiation of lower pharmaceutical prices, and uniform provider payment rates [17, 18].

**Proposed Financing Mechanisms**
To fund the transition, the system would rely on existing public healthcare funds paired with significant new tax revenues, which would replace the premiums and out-of-pocket costs families and businesses currently pay [1, 19].

* The CRFB notes that raising $30 trillion could require aggressive measures such as a **32% payroll tax, a 25% income surtax, a 42% value-added tax (VAT), or doubling all individual and corporate income tax rates** [10, 20, 21].
* The PERI UMASS analysis proposes a mixed financing approach, including an 8% cut to business healthcare premiums (later transitioning to an 8.2% payroll tax), a **3.75% sales tax on non-necessities** (exempting groceries, housing, and education), a **0.38% net worth tax** on the wealthiest households, and taxing long-term capital gains as ordinary income [22-25].

**Impact on Providers and Patients**
For patients, the Act ensures the freedom to choose any participating physician or hospital without worrying about restrictive insurance networks [5, 26]. Because the financial barriers to care would be removed, overall demand for healthcare services is projected to increase by approximately 11% to 12% [27, 28]. The Congressional Budget Office warns that this **surge in demand could exceed supply, potentially leading to increased congestion, delays, and unmet demand** [29].

For healthcare providers, the transition means reimbursement rates would be standardized at Medicare levels, which are typically lower than private insurance payouts [30-32]. However, the PERI analysis suggests that any loss in per-visit revenue would be heavily offset by a drastic reduction in administrative and billing workloads, freeing physicians to log more billable patient hours [30].

**Labor and Transition Impacts**
Transitioning to a single-payer system would eliminate the need for much of the private health insurance industry and drastically reduce administrative staffing in medical offices, displacing hundreds of thousands of workers [33, 34]. To protect these individuals, the Act proposes a **"Just Transition" program**, which would allocate up to 1% of the program's budget to provide displaced workers with pension guarantees, up to 100% wage replacement for a set period, and job retraining or relocation support [5, 35, 36].
