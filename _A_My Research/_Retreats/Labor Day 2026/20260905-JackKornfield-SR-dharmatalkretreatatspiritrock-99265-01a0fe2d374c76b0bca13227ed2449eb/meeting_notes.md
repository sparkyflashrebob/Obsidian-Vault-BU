### Dharma Talk at Spirit Rock Retreat — Jack Kornfield
📅 October 2, 2026
👥 Main Teacher: Jack Kornfield | Facilitator/Introduction: James Baraz | Others mentioned: Howie, Terry, Don
### Outline
### The Nature and Purpose of Meditation Practice

- Meditation is described as fundamentally different from most human activities, which are goal-oriented and aimed at reaching a particular outcome.

- Drawing on Alan Watts, the practice is compared to making music or dancing, where the value lies in the experience itself rather than in reaching an end point.

- The invitation of retreat is not primarily to achieve deep concentration or dissolve into light, but to take one's seat gracefully in the midst of the full range of human experience, the 10,000 joys and 10,000 sorrows.

- Sitting in practice is framed as an opportunity to embody the qualities of a wise person or bodhisattva, cultivating a sense of freedom, compassion, and love that is understood to be innate rather than acquired.

- Settling into stillness does not mean achieving complete silence, but rather a gradual quieting that allows for clearer seeing and a sense of relief in body, heart, and mind.

### Guided Meditation on Mindful Loving Awareness

- Participants are invited to close their eyes, take deep breaths, and soften the body, releasing tension in the face, jaw, shoulders, arms, and hands.

- Attention is first directed to the physical body, noticing the full field of energy, aliveness, tingling, areas of stress or pain, and the natural rhythm of the breath.

- A gesture of gratitude and compassion is offered toward the body, acknowledging its effort and inviting it to relax with the reassurance that one is safe and on retreat.

- Awareness is then shifted to the heart, understood not merely as a physical organ but as the carrier of grief, longing, love, loss, tenderness, and joy.

- Compassion and kindness are extended to the heart in recognition of how much it has been carrying, with an invitation for it to soften and open.

- The mind is then brought into awareness as a busy, thought-generating faculty that cycles through images, words, and imaginings continuously.

- Gratitude is offered to the mind for its efforts to protect and care, and it is gently invited to rest, wrapped in kindness and appreciation.

- A key insight is introduced distinguishing the practitioner from their body, feelings, and mind, framing the true nature of the self as awareness or consciousness itself.

- Thoughts are described as clouds passing through the space of awareness, and emotions as waves arising and passing in the ocean of consciousness.

- Participants are guided to rest in awareness as their home, attending with gratitude to messages the body and heart may wish to communicate.

- The guided session is identified as a condensed integration of the Four Foundations of Mindfulness and the Brahma Viharas, combining mindfulness and loving-kindness practice.

### The Relationship Between Inner Practice and Engagement with the World

- Retreat practice is described as only half of the spiritual path, with the other half being active engagement in the world, expressed through the Zen metaphor of sitting and sweeping the garden.

- Quieting the mind and opening the heart are presented as preparation for returning to the world and responding to its needs with compassion and practical action.

- The global context is acknowledged, including climate change, mass displacement, ongoing armed conflicts, and the emergence of artificial intelligence as sources of collective concern.

- Jack Kornfield recounts being invited to speak at a spiritual festival in Ukraine for approximately 10,000 people seeking to maintain their inner lives amid war, describing the emotional landscape of fear, grief, exhaustion, outrage, and survivor's guilt.

- The central teaching offered in that context is the importance of not allowing external conflict and suffering to take over one's inner life, which is directly applicable to all practitioners returning to challenging circumstances.

- Practices discussed in that setting included breath and body awareness, releasing suffering, connecting with the suffering of others globally, grounding through nature, and cultivating presence as an antidote to fear-based thinking.

- Living in the present moment is emphasized as essential, particularly in times of difficulty, as a way of tending to reality and creating the conditions for a meaningful future.

- Retreat practice is reframed not as withdrawal from the world but as the cultivation of a space within oneself that remembers one's true nature and enables compassionate action upon return.

### The Meaning of Sustained Compassion and Identity

- A story is shared about an elderly man who visits his wife with Alzheimer's every morning despite her no longer recognizing him, illustrating unconditional love rooted in one's own knowing rather than in being known.

- His response, that she does not know him but he still knows who she is, is offered as a teaching on carrying one's humanity and love regardless of external recognition or reciprocity.

- This story is used to illustrate that the loving awareness cultivated in practice is not distant or abstract but is already present within each person and expressed in ordinary acts of devotion.

### Next Steps

- Practitioners are encouraged to continue deepening their retreat experience by remaining present with mindful loving awareness throughout the remaining days.

- Returning to retreat is welcomed for those who feel they have not yet fully integrated the teachings, with the understanding that repeated engagement with practice is a natural part of the path.

- Participants are encouraged to carry the qualities of compassion, presence, and loving awareness back into their daily lives and to act in whatever ways are available to them to contribute to the wellbeing of others and the world.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Meditation as Music and Dance**
- Jack Kornfield drew on Alan Watts' analogy that meditation is unlike most activities because it is not goal-directed
- Most activities aim at an outcome — cooking, building, teaching — but meditation is like making music or dancing, where the point is to be in the experience, not to reach the end
- The best musicians are not the fastest; the purpose of dancing is not to arrive somewhere
**The Four Foundations of Mindfulness and Brahma Viharas**
- Kornfield led a guided practice combining mindfulness of body, heart, and mind with loving-kindness
- Participants were invited to bring kind, compassionate attention to the body, acknowledging its effort and stress
- The heart was acknowledged as carrying grief, loss, longing, love, and joy — all to be held with tenderness
- The mind was recognized as busy, repetitive, and creative — to be thanked and invited to rest
- The practice was described as a compressed version of the Four Foundations of Mindfulness and the Brahma Viharas
**Loving Awareness as True Identity**
- Kornfield emphasized that practitioners are not their body, feelings, or thoughts
- These arise and pass away; the practitioner is the awareness that observes them
- Awareness was described as spacious, patient, and open — the true home of the practitioner
- Thoughts were likened to clouds in the space of awareness; emotions to waves of the ocean
**Sitting and Sweeping the Garden**
- Kornfield referenced a Zen teaching: two things are required — sitting (quieting the mind) and sweeping the garden (engaging with the world)
- Retreat practice is only half the work; the other half is going into the world and acting from an open heart
- This does not mean solving all the world's problems, but touching and mending where one can
**The Poly Crisis and Global Suffering**
- Kornfield named current global challenges: climate change, mass refugees, water scarcity, desertification, wars in Ukraine, Burma, and Sudan, and the rise of AI
- He acknowledged the tension between being in a peaceful retreat setting and the suffering present in the world
- He described being invited to speak at a spiritual festival in Ukraine for 10,000 people near Odessa
- The experiences reported by Ukrainian participants included fear, overwhelm, grief, exhaustion, outrage, and survivor's guilt
- The teaching offered was about not letting the external battle take over the heart
**Gratitude Practice**
- Kornfield introduced gratitude as a quality woven into mindful loving awareness
- Participants were guided to say "thank you" to their body, heart, and mind for their efforts
- The practice involved acknowledging what each carries and inviting relaxation and softening
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**On the Purpose of Retreat**
- Retreat is not about achieving deep concentration or dissolving into light, though that may happen
- The real invitation is to take one's seat amid the 10,000 joys and 10,000 sorrows of human life with a gracious heart
- The goal is a growing sense of freedom, compassion, and love — remembered as innate, not acquired
**On Presence and Fear**
- In times of difficulty, the mind of fear can take over; practice teaches how to tend the reality of the present
- Living in the present, rather than being consumed by fear of the future, is central to the teaching
- Tending the present with loving awareness is described as the way to create a beautiful future
**On Human Connection and Grace**
- Grace can arise anywhere — in a tire repair shop, in a clinic, in a war zone
- Shared stories and vulnerability create healing spaces in ordinary settings
- The poem "Everyday Grace" by Stella Nesanovich illustrated how ordinary places become holy ground through human connection
**On Identity and Compassion**
- Who one truly is, is consciousness — loving awareness — not the contents of body, emotion, or thought
- This awareness is described as the Buddha, the bodhisattva, Kuan Yin — holding all of experience
- Remembering this identity is what allows practitioners to go out and act with compassion in the world
**On Engaged Spirituality**
- Kornfield referenced the Dalai Lama and Maha Ghosananda as examples of maintaining humanity and loving-kindness regardless of circumstances
- Practices shared with Ukrainian participants included breath and body awareness, making altars, connecting with ancestors, placing hands on trees, and standing with others across borders
- The teaching is that quieting the mind and opening the heart naturally reveals what matters and what action to take

### Stories
**The Tire Shop Waiting Room**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, reading a poem by Stella Nesanovich titled "Everyday Grace"
- In a tire center waiting room, strangers share stories of illness, surgery, and loss — a woman shares her sister's death, and a woman named Mrs. Henry rises, gives her name, and takes her hand
- An ordinary day becomes holy ground through human connection and shared vulnerability
**The African-American Retreat at Spirit Rock**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- One of the first retreats held in the new meditation hall was an African-American retreat, attended by long-time practitioners including Alice Walker
- Alice Walker remarked that the land felt like fine ground, and that her ancestors were celebrating because the white folks were cooking for them
- Kornfield used this story to illustrate the welcoming and supportive nature of the retreat community
**The Spiritual Festival in Ukraine**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- He was invited to speak at a spiritual festival near Odessa for approximately 10,000 people seeking to maintain their spiritual lives during wartime
- He described his initial intimidation and eventual agreement to participate
- Participants reported fear, overwhelm, grief, exhaustion, outrage, and survivor's guilt
- The teaching focused on not letting the external battle take over the heart
**The Elderly Man and His Wife with Alzheimer's**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, set in a medical clinic
- An elderly man in his 80s came to have stitches removed, in a hurry to get to the nursing home to have breakfast with his wife
- His wife had Alzheimer's and had not recognized him for five years
- When asked why he still went every morning, he replied: "She doesn't know me. But I still know who she is."
- Kornfield used this story to illustrate the depth of loving awareness and devotion that is innate in human beings
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- First-time retreat participants were noted to be present, described as sitting in the back looking uncertain about what was happening
- Kornfield acknowledged that deep concentration states generally take more than a few days for most practitioners
- Participants were guided to notice areas of stress or pain in the body — shoulders, gut, hands, jaw — as part of the energy field of the body
- The mind was described as busy, repetitive, and tiresome, secreting thoughts the way the salivary gland secretes saliva
- Kornfield acknowledged that practitioners carry worries about what they need to do when they return from retreat
- Knee pain during sitting was mentioned as a common experience
- The challenge of not being taken over by the mind of fear during times of difficulty was addressed directly
- Survivor's guilt, exhaustion, grief, and overwhelm were named as experiences reported by practitioners in war zones, and acknowledged as versions of what all practitioners carry
