### The Gift of Hindrances – Spirit Rock Retreat Talk
📅 October 2, 2026

### Outline
### Acknowledging the Challenges of the First Days of Retreat

- Participants are reminded that feeling as though more time has passed than actually has is a common experience in the early days of retreat.

- The first couple of days are described as the most challenging and frustrating, while also containing poignant moments of presence and beauty.

- Remaining present through difficulty, regardless of the original motivation for attending, is acknowledged as a meaningful accomplishment.

- Common motivations for beginning a meditation practice include suffering, heartbreak, dissatisfaction with old coping mechanisms, encouragement from others, or a sense that deep transformation is possible.

- Wanting things to be different is identified as a near-universal experience among retreat participants, arising in both large and small ways throughout the day.

### Introduction to Dukkha and the Five Hindrances

- The Pali term dukkha is introduced as meaning not only suffering but also unsatisfactoriness and the experience of being separated from what one wants or loves.

- An image of a slightly misaligned wheel on an ox cart, or a modern grocery cart with a wonky wheel, is used to illustrate the nature of dukkha.

- The five hindrances, known in Pali as Nivarana, are presented as energetic qualities that obstruct the capacity to be present and at ease.

- These five qualities are identified as desire, aversion, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, and doubt.

- Hindrances are described as the dominant experience not only on retreat but in everyday life, which is why they are consistently taught across retreat settings.

- Millions of practitioners over thousands of years, including the Buddha himself, have encountered these same experiences, confirming that their arising is entirely normal and expected.

### Reframing the Hindrances as Part of Practice Rather Than Obstacles to It

- A common tendency is to label the arising of hindrances as bad or as evidence that one is doing something wrong, which leads to unnecessary suffering and mental spiraling.

- Hindrances are clarified as unpleasant but not inherently problematic, becoming a hindrance only when one is unaware of them, caught in them, or struggling to eliminate them.

- The teaching "what is in the way is the way" is offered as a foundational reframe, suggesting that the hindrances themselves constitute the meditation rather than interrupting it.

- The Buddha's instruction to simply know the state of the mind, whether scattered or unified, is cited as evidence that the practice does not require the mind to be otherwise than it is.

- How one relates to the hindrances is identified as the central key to practice, echoing a teaching offered earlier in the retreat about the relationship to thoughts.

- Rather than using meditation tools as a means to force hindrances away, the invitation is to meet them with kindness and support, creating conditions for a natural softening and letting go.

- Pema Chodron's teaching is quoted to reinforce that the central question of a warrior's training is not how to avoid uncertainty and fear but how to relate to discomfort.

- The purpose of practice is framed not as the pursuit of comfort but as the pursuit of liberation.

### The First Hindrance: Desire and Sensual Craving

- Desire as a hindrance refers to wanting something pleasing to the senses and carries a hidden belief that obtaining that thing will finally bring happiness or relief.

- This hindrance can manifest as a passing thought or escalate into an obsessive loop of craving, clinging, and grasping.

- Using the Buddha's water simile, desire is compared to water colored with dye, through which nothing can be seen clearly or as it truly is.

- When desire goes unchecked, the mind becomes convinced it should have what it wants immediately and constructs elaborate stories to justify that wanting.

- Unchecked desire can sever connection to oneself and others and attempts to stop the natural flow of changing conditions.

- Practicing with desire can involve feeling the pain of wanting things to be different, which has a disenchanting effect that reduces investment in the wanting.

- Giving space to desire may reveal underlying states such as boredom, grief, or loneliness, which can then be met with kindness and tenderness.

- Mindfulness alone, even without tenderness, functions as a pattern disruptor that weakens the pull of desire.

- Reflecting on impermanence, through questions such as "what if this were the last time," is offered as a practice that restores presence and reduces the dominance of wanting.

- Desire in itself is not considered bad, and the Buddha encouraged turning toward beauty; the hindrance arises specifically when grasping or clinging is present.

### The Second Hindrance: Aversion and Ill Will

- Aversion can manifest as irritation, frustration, annoyance, resentment, resistance, or complaining, and carries an energy of hostility or the impulse to push something away.

- Using the water simile, ill will is compared to water heated to a boil, through which clear seeing becomes very difficult.

- Aversion often covers over deeper experiences such as a yearning for peace, connection, safety, or recognition.

- Practicing with aversion can involve asking "what else is here," which widens perspective to include supportive conditions such as community, teachings, and the environment.

- Noticing one's relationship to aversion is important, as there is often a secondary aversion directed at the aversion itself.

- With less intense forms of aversion, simply noticing their presence is often sufficient to dispel them.

- The practice asks only that awareness be brought to the experience, shining a light on it and observing what unfolds.

### The Third Hindrance: Sloth and Torpor

- Sloth and torpor are described as a unified hindrance in which sloth refers to mental sluggishness and torpor refers to bodily heaviness.

- Using the water simile, this hindrance is compared to water clogged with algae and water plants, which destroys the mirror-like reflective quality of a calm mind.

- Beyond physical drowsiness, this hindrance can manifest as an inner resistance to engaging fully with practice, reflecting a lack of mental energy rather than physical exhaustion alone.

- Physical exhaustion, mental overstimulation, and the body's association of stillness with sleep are identified as common causes of sleepiness during practice.

- Retreat offers a unique opportunity to work with this hindrance because overriding sleepiness is so normalized and even glamorized in everyday culture.

- Practicing with sloth and torpor involves noticing where it is felt in the body and mind, with the understanding that the window of observation may be brief before sleep overtakes awareness.

- Developing intimacy with the characteristics and texture of sleepiness over time allows a practitioner to recognize it arising from a distance.

- Self-judgment around falling asleep during a waking practice is common and can be met with kindness directed at the judging mind rather than at the sleepiness itself.

- Practical remedies include taking deep breaths, opening the eyes to let in light, sitting without back support, standing meditation, walking meditation, and in some cases taking a nap.

- Sloth and torpor can serve a protective function, with sleepiness sometimes arising to shield the practitioner from an unpleasant experience that requires significant energy to process.

- Dropping in the question "if I were not feeling sleepy, what would I be feeling" is offered as a way to investigate what the sleepiness may be protecting against.

### The Fourth Hindrance: Restlessness and Worry

- Restlessness and worry represent an energetic imbalance in which there is too much alertness and insufficient tranquility.

- Using the water simile, this hindrance is compared to water blown into choppy waves by strong wind, preventing any clear reflection.

- Restlessness manifests as mental fidgeting, physical agitation, racing thoughts, jumping between topics, obsessive planning, and difficulty staying present.

- Worry manifests as dwelling on regrets about the past or anxiety about the future.

- The transition from the fast pace of daily life to the stillness of retreat naturally produces remnants of agitation, making this hindrance especially common in the first few days.

- Practice is described as a kind of purification in which slowing down and tending to the heart and mind brings to the surface what is obscuring one's true nature, including worries and anxieties.

- Practicing with restlessness involves noticing its physical manifestation in the body rather than engaging with the thoughts surrounding it.

- Investigating the attitude of the mind, asking whether greed, hatred, or delusion is present, and softening the body are offered as approaches to working with agitation.

- Opening the eyes during practice is recommended as a particularly effective disruptor of restlessness, as sight occupies approximately three quarters of attention.

- Walking meditation or other forms of movement are suggested when agitation is high, as forcing stillness in such moments can be counterproductive.

- Softening is distinguished from letting go, with the understanding that surrender and softening often arise naturally when the effort of resistance becomes exhausting, and that opening follows from softening rather than from direct effort.

### The Fifth Hindrance: Doubt

- Doubt as a hindrance is distinguished from healthy questioning, which the Buddha actively encouraged through the invitation to investigate teachings for oneself.

- Skeptical doubt manifests as uncertainty about the practice, about oneself, about whether one is doing it correctly, about the teachers, and about the value of the path, leading to indecision.

- Using the water simile, doubt is compared to murky or unsettled water, in which the reflective quality of the mind is ruined.

- Doubt is described as potentially the most insidious hindrance because when one is caught in it, it is difficult to recognize it as doubt, and it can carry a practitioner entirely off the retreat.

- The "rolling of the mat phase" is introduced as a term used by a teacher in Thailand to describe the stage of retreat when the impulse to leave becomes overwhelming.

- Recognizing doubt clearly and naming it as simply doubt is identified as the most effective antidote, though this is easier said than done.

- Community and the support of a trusted teacher or wise friend are highlighted as particularly valuable resources when doubt is present, as they can point out what the practitioner cannot see.

- Borrowing the faith and confidence of teachers, friends, and the broader community of practitioners is offered as a practical support for those who have not yet developed their own baseline confidence in the practice.

- Accumulated experience with the fruits of practice builds a kind of confidence that causes doubt to evaporate upon recognition.

### The Hindrances as a Framework for Life Beyond Retreat

- The hindrances are not limited to retreat settings but arise throughout everyday life in the same patterns encountered during formal practice.

- A common tendency in life is to pursue a never-ending quest for perfect conditions, believing that happiness depends on circumstances being just right.

- Part of the maturity of spiritual development is the recognition that no lasting perfect set of conditions exists and that happiness dependent on conditions is inherently unreliable.

- Skillfully working with the hindrances is identified as the actual process through which growth occurs, new capacities are developed, and greater freedom in life becomes possible.

- The relationship built with the hindrances on retreat directly supports the ability to meet them with greater ease and skill in daily life.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Dukkha – The Nature of Suffering**
- Dukkha is often translated as suffering but more precisely means unsatisfactoriness or being separated from what one wants or loves
- The literal image is a wheel of an ox cart with a slightly off-center axle, creating a bumpy ride; the modern equivalent is a grocery cart with a wonky wheel
- Dukkha arises in response to unpleasant physical and mental experiences
- The Buddha himself taught about this phenomenon, indicating he was not exempt from it
**The Five Hindrances (Nivarana) – Overview**
- The hindrances are five energetic qualities or visitors that hinder the capacity to be present and at ease
- They are: Sensual Desire, Aversion, Sloth and Torpor, Restlessness and Worry, and Doubt
- They are not bad in themselves; they are unpleasant but only become problematic when one is unaware of them, caught in them, or struggling to fix them
- The hindrances are the dominant experience both on retreat and in daily life
- The Buddha used the simile of a bowl of water used as a mirror to illustrate how each hindrance obstructs mental clarity
**Hindrance 1 – Sensual Desire**
- Wanting something pleasing to the five senses; hides a strategy of dealing with difficulty by wanting something else
- Carries the belief that obtaining the desired thing will bring happiness, relaxation, or resolution
- Can manifest as craving, clinging, grasping, or obsessive loops
- Compared to water colored with dye – anything seen through it takes on the color of the dye and cannot be seen clearly
- Can sever connection to oneself and others and tries to stop the natural flow of changing conditions
- Wholesome desire (chanda) – desire for practice – is distinguished from desire as a hindrance; the Buddha encouraged turning toward beauty; the hindrance arises specifically with grasping and clinging
**Practicing with Sensual Desire**
- Feel the pain of wanting things to be different – mindfulness of the pain makes the wanting disenchanting
- Paying attention to wanting may reveal underlying conditions such as boredom, grief, or loneliness
- Meet underlying feelings with kindness and tenderness
- If tenderness feels too far, simply being mindful disrupts the pattern
- Remember one's intention for practice
- Reflect on impermanence: "What if this is the last time?"
**Hindrance 2 – Aversion**
- Can manifest as ill will, anger, hatred, rage, irritation, frustration, annoyance, resentment, resistance, or complaining
- Has an energy of hostility, wanting to strike out or push away – described as a reptilian, snake-like response
- Compared to water heated to a boil – the heat makes it very difficult to see clearly
- Often covers over something deeper such as a yearning for peace, connection, safety, or to be seen
- Takes a lot of energy and can be quite painful
**Practicing with Aversion**
- Ask "What else is here?" – widen perspective to include supportive conditions such as Sangha, teachings, and physical comfort
- Notice one's relationship to aversion, including being aversive to aversion itself
- Meet aversion with care or at minimum with mindfulness
- With less intense forms, simply noticing is often enough to dispel them
**Hindrance 3 – Sloth and Torpor**
- Sloth is mental sluggishness; torpor is bodily heaviness; they often appear together
- Compared to water filled with seaweed and algae – the mirror-like quality of the mind is obscured
- Caused by energetic imbalance: too much tranquility, not enough alertness
- Can show up as drowsiness, mental fog, inner resistance to engaging with spiritual practice, or mental laziness
- Physical exhaustion, sleep deprivation, and mental overstimulation from daily life are contributing factors
- Overriding sleepiness is normalized and even glamorized in modern culture
- Sloth and torpor has a protective function – can shield from unpleasant experiences or overwhelming suffering
**Practicing with Sloth and Torpor**
- Notice where sloth and torpor is experienced in the body and mind
- Be aware that the window of observation before falling asleep may be short
- Notice and meet self-judgment with kindness
- Ask: "If I wasn't feeling sleepy, what would I be feeling?"
- Classical remedies: deep breaths focusing on inhales, opening the eyes, sitting without back support, standing meditation, walking meditation, walking backwards (used in teen retreats)
- Taking a nap is acceptable but should not become a habitual response every time the hindrance arises
**Hindrance 4 – Restlessness and Worry**
- Compared to water blown into choppy waves by strong wind
- Energetic imbalance: too much alertness, not enough tranquility
- Restlessness manifests as mental fidgeting, physical agitation, racing thoughts, jumping between topics, obsessive planning, difficulty staying present
- Worry manifests as dwelling on regrets about the past or anxiety about the future
- Particularly common in the first few days of retreat as the mind transitions from fast-paced daily life
- Practice is likened to a purification process that brings to the surface what clouds one's true nature
**Practicing with Restlessness and Worry**
- Notice the physical manifestation in the body rather than engaging with thoughts about it
- Observe the attitude of mind – is there greed, hatred, or delusion present?
- Soften the body as best as possible
- Notice comparing mind and bring mindfulness or compassion to it
- Open the eyes – sight occupies approximately three quarters of attention and can cut through agitation
- Walking meditation or movement to work with excess energy in the system
- Forcing stillness when agitation is present is compared to holding the lid on a boiling pot of water
**Hindrance 5 – Doubt**
- Healthy doubt prompts questioning and investigation; the Buddha encouraged students to question everything, including his own teachings ("Ehi Pasiko – come see for yourself")
- Doubt as a hindrance is skeptical doubt: doubt in the practice, in oneself, in the teachers, in whether one is doing it correctly; leads to indecision
- Compared to water that is muddy, murky, or unsettled
- Can be the most insidious hindrance because when caught in it, it is hard to see it
- Can carry a practitioner entirely off retreat
**Practicing with Doubt**
- The best antidote is to see it clearly for what it is: "This is doubt"
- Community (Sangha) and wise friends can help point out when one is caught in doubt
- Borrowing faith from teachers, friends, and the collective energy of the room
- Sufficient experience with the fruits of practice builds a baseline confidence that causes doubt to evaporate upon recognition
- Millions of practitioners over thousands of years provide a foundation of confidence in the worthiness of the practice
**The Hindrances as Gift**
- The hindrances help illuminate the process of thinking and entanglement with thought
- Investigating unpleasant thoughts reveals patterns of fighting and contending that would not be visible through pleasant thoughts alone
- Building a relationship with the hindrances on retreat strengthens practice and increases freedom in daily life
- The hindrances are not a retreat-only problem; they arise in life as well
- There is no lasting perfect set of conditions; happiness dependent on conditions is not reliable
- Skillfully working with the hindrances is where growth and new capacities develop
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Universality of Struggle**
- Practitioners across millions of people and thousands of years have experienced the same challenges encountered on retreat
- The first couple of days of retreat are consistently the most challenging and most frustrating, while also containing poignant moments of presence and beauty
- Feeling like one is the only one struggling is a common and well-documented experience; it is not the reality
**What Is in the Way Is the Way**
- The Buddha taught: "When the mind is scattered, know that the mind is scattered. When the mind is gathered, know that the mind is gathered." There is no instruction that it should be otherwise
- The hindrances are not in the way of meditation; they are the meditation
- The invitation is to meet whatever arises in a new way rather than fighting or bypassing it
**Relating to Discomfort as Spiritual Training**
- Pema Chodron is cited: "The central question of a warrior's training is not how we avoid uncertainty and fear, but how we relate to discomfort"
- The practice is not for comfort but for liberation
- How one relates to the hindrances is the key, not whether the hindrances are present
**Softening vs. Letting Go**
- Telling someone to "let go" is compared to telling someone to relax or smile on command – it does not work that way
- The process is: surrender leads to softening, softening creates the field in which opening can happen, and opening allows letting go to occur naturally
- Contention with experience is what perpetuates suffering; removing contention creates conditions for release
**Suffering in Society**
- Observing how people treat each other while driving is offered as a clear example of how personal suffering is brought into the world and perpetuates further suffering
- The overstimulated nature of modern life means that slowing down is often only associated with sleep, making retreat conditions particularly disorienting for the body and mind
- Overriding sleepiness is normalized and glamorized in contemporary culture, making retreat a rare opportunity to work with it skillfully
**Impermanence as a Contemplative Tool**
- Reflecting on "What if this is the last time?" transforms ordinary moments into poignant experiences
- This reflection does not eliminate wanting but can prevent wanting from being the dominant experience
**The Role of Community**
- Sangha provides support that makes practice possible in moments when individual effort falters
- Faith and confidence can be borrowed from teachers, friends, and the collective presence of practitioners in the room
- The shared experience of the hindrances, made visible through group exercises such as raising hands, counters the isolating belief that one is uniquely failing

### Stories
**Dawn Mauricio – Four-Week Self-Retreat in Hawaii While Dating Someone New**
Dawn Mauricio shares that a few months into dating someone new, she left for a four-week self-retreat in Hawaii. Despite loving the practice and being in a beautiful location, she made herself unhappy by constantly wanting the retreat to be over so she could contact the person she was dating. She experienced shame about this because she was a teacher. Eventually, exhausted by the incessant wanting, she began reflecting on impermanence – "What if this is the last time?" – which shifted the wanting from being the dominant experience to being one experience among many. She notes she never returned to that retreat center and later married the person she had been dating.
**Dawn Mauricio – Retreat in South Africa, Comparing Herself to Others**
During a spring retreat in South Africa, Dawn Mauricio opened her eyes during a difficult sitting and looked around the room. She became convinced for many days that everyone else was in on secret teachings she had missed and that she was failing and did not belong – despite having practiced for decades. She shares this as an example of how the comparing mind can arise even for experienced practitioners.
**Dawn Mauricio – Note About Coughing During a Sitting**
Dawn Mauricio received an anonymous note from a retreat participant complaining that there were 14 coughs and sneezes during a 50%-occupied 7 p.m. sitting, one every two minutes, and that a man behind the writer had brought a box of tissues. She uses this as an example of aversion and also notes that the detailed counting in the note actually demonstrated mindfulness, though the aversion prevented the writer from recognizing it.
**Dawn Mauricio – First Retreat in Thailand, Rolling of the Mat Phase**
On her very first retreat in Thailand, around day three or four, Dawn Mauricio planned her escape in detail – timing the bus, noting when the teacher would not see her leave. She was stopped only because the reception was closed and they had her passport. The next day she confessed to her teacher, who adjusted her practice and told her it was simply "the rolling of the mat phase" and that it passes. She stayed for the rest of the retreat, which she describes as life-changing, and she has been practicing for over twenty years since.
**Dawn Mauricio – Torn Achilles Tendon Recovery**
A little over a year before the retreat, Dawn Mauricio severely tore her Achilles tendon while playing basketball after stealing the ball on a breakaway. She was on crutches, then a knee scooter, then a walking boot. Initially the experience was novel and she enjoyed being cared for by her partner and friends. As the recovery extended and people returned to their routines, she became increasingly agitated – unable to go outside easily, living on the fourth floor without an elevator, hearing people outside enjoying Montreal's short summer. She began comparing her recovery timeline to others on YouTube, which she describes as great suffering. Eventually exhaustion led to softening, and she found gratitude for her practice because it had given her the tools to be with agitation rather than fight it.
**Dawn Mauricio – The 10:31 Sitting**
Dawn Mauricio shares that on one retreat, the 10:31 a.m. sitting was consistently her sleepy sitting. She tried every remedy – drinking coffee beforehand, sleeping in, doing movement before the sit – and still fell asleep every time. She eventually used that sitting as an opportunity to get intimate with what sloth and torpor actually felt like.
