### Outline
### Introduction to the RAINN Acronym and Its Broader Application

- Originally coined by senior teacher Michelle McDonald several decades ago, the RAINN acronym has since been widely popularized by teacher and psychologist Tara Brach.

- Commonly presented as a tool for working with intense or unpleasant emotions, Dawn Mauricio emphasizes that the practice is equally applicable to subtle experiences, pleasant emotions, and thought patterns.

- Guiding the morning session around this acronym, Dawn Mauricio frames the practice as a way of tuning into direct somatic experience regardless of the size or quality of what is being felt.

### The Role of Somatic Awareness in Sustaining Mindfulness Beyond Retreat

- Engaging deeply with the felt, bodily experience of positive or peaceful states during retreat can serve as a resource that is accessible in more difficult moments later in life.

- While such a somatic memory will not fully recreate the retreat experience, it can serve as a reminder of what is possible and prompt a return to mindfulness during stressful periods.

- Mindfulness itself is described as a disruptor that plants the seed for softening, opening, and the possibility of things being different.

### The Mind as a Sixth Sense in Buddhist Practice

- In Buddhist understanding, the thinking mind is considered a sixth sense alongside the five physical senses, making thought a natural and expected part of present-moment experience.

- A common misconception about meditation is that the goal is to achieve a completely blank mind, which Dawn Mauricio describes as unnatural and inconsistent with how practitioners relate to any other sense.

- Rather than attempting to suppress thinking, the practice invites meditators to be present with thoughts as they arise, recognizing that even content rooted in the past or future is occurring as a present-moment phenomenon.

### Breakdown of Each Letter in the RAINN Framework

- The letter R stands for Recognize, which involves simply noticing what is present in the moment, whether that is an emotion, a thought pattern, or something as subtle as the breath.

- Represented by the letter A, Allow refers to the willingness to stop fighting whatever is arising, without requiring that one likes or approves of the experience, and this non-resistance naturally creates more space around the experience.

- Investigate, represented by the letter I, is not a mental analysis but rather a curious, compassionate inquiry into the somatic qualities of the experience, such as its location in the body, whether it intensifies or fades, whether it moves, pulses, or has temperature.

- The first N stands for Non-identification, which involves recognizing that emotions and thoughts arise due to past conditions, are not under one's control, and are simply moving through the person rather than defining who they are.

- The second N can be understood as Nurture, which involves intentionally bringing tenderness and self-compassion to the experience, particularly when what is arising is challenging, through phrases such as "I love you, keep going" or a gentle physical gesture toward the heart or belly.

### Guided Meditation Using the RAINN Practice

- Participants are invited to settle into a comfortable posture of their choosing, with eyes closed, turned downward, or open toward a window, and to notice the points of contact between the body and the ground.

- Attention is drawn to the way the ground supports the body, and meditators are encouraged to relax into that support while inviting kindness and compassion as companions in the practice.

- Dawn Mauricio suggests that if compassion feels difficult to access, one may simply plant the intention for it to arise, even framing it as a wish for the future rather than a present requirement.

- Practitioners are guided to gather attention around a chosen anchor such as the breath or a part of the body, and then to bring awareness to whatever is most present, applying the RAINN acronym to that experience.

- The instruction allows for flexibility, encouraging meditators to cycle through all letters of the acronym or to dwell longer in whichever aspect feels most relevant, and to return to the anchor when no compelling experience is present.

- A reset option is offered for moments when experience feels overwhelming, involving opening the eyes to take in light, shapes, and color while continuing to notice the somatic dimension of the experience.

- A contemplative question is introduced toward the end of the sitting, asking what it would mean if this were the last time one would feel a particular feeling, think a particular thought, or breathe a particular breath, as a way of deepening the quality of mindfulness.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The RAIN Acronym**
- RAIN stands for: Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Non-identification/Nurture
- Coined by senior teacher Michelle McDonald, decades ago (approximately 40 years)
- Widely popularized by Tara Brach, psychologist and meditation teacher
- Traditionally presented as a tool for intense or unpleasant emotions
- Dawn Mauricio uses it more broadly: for subtle experiences, pleasant emotions, and thoughts
**R — Recognize**
- Simply noticing what is present: a thought pattern, an emotion, or something as subtle as the breath
- Can be as basic as identifying whether the experience is pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral
- The question posed: "What is here? What is happening right now?"
**A — Allow**
- Allowing what is present without needing to like it
- Paraphrased as: "Can I, just for a few moments, stop fighting what is here?"
- Allowing creates more space around the experience by reducing contraction and resistance
**I — Investigate**
- Not a mental or analytical investigation, but a somatic, curious inquiry
- Questions include: Does the sensation get stronger, weaker, stay the same, or go away? Does it move? Is it pulsing, hot, cold?
- Even numbness can be investigated: Where does it start and end? Are the edges frayed or smooth?
- Approached with compassion, not criticism — gathering information, not building a case against oneself
**N — Non-Identification**
- Recognizing that emotions and thoughts arise due to past conditions
- We do not have control over their arising; they move through us
- The shift in language: from "I am an angry person" to "Anger is here. There is anger"
- Impermanence illustrated: the bell rings, movement begins, and the anger is no longer present
**N — Nurture**
- When the experience is challenging, intentionally bringing in tenderness and compassion
- Phrases offered: "I love you. Keep going." and "This too."
- May include a physical gesture toward the heart or belly
- If compassion feels difficult, the suggestion is to plant the seed: "May I one day feel compassionate toward myself?"
**The Thinking Mind as a Sixth Sense**
- In Buddhism, the thinking mind is considered another sense alongside the traditional five
- The misconception that meditation aims for a blank mind is compared to trying to stop hearing or smelling during a session
- The practice is not about shutting down the thinking mind but being with it as a present-moment phenomenon
- Even if thought content is rooted in past or future, the thinking itself is happening right now
**Using RAIN to Preserve Positive Experiences**
- Dawn described using RAIN to anchor the somatic experience of peaceful or meaningful moments
- The intention: when stress returns later, a moment of mindfulness may allow recall of those sensations
- This recall reminds the practitioner what is possible and plants the seed for softening and opening
**Guided Meditation Structure**
- Participants settled into a comfortable posture with eyes closed, downward, or open toward a window
- Attention brought to body contact with the ground and the ground's support pressing upward
- Anchor established: breath, a body part, or phrases
- RAIN applied to whatever arose: dominant emotion, thought pattern, or subtle sensation
- Option to cycle through all letters or dwell longer in one
- If nothing compelling arose, participants returned to their anchor and applied RAIN when something pulled attention
- A reset option offered: open eyes, allow in light, shapes, and color, while still noticing bodily experience
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Mindfulness as a Disruptor**
- Mindfulness is described as a disruptor — the moment of awareness that plants the seed for softening, opening, and change
- Even a brief moment of mindfulness in a stressful situation can shift the quality of experience
**Impermanence as a Core Teaching**
- Emotions and thoughts, even intense or frequent ones, are not permanent parts of identity
- They are described as "moving through us" rather than defining us
- The bell-and-movement example illustrates how quickly an emotional state can dissolve
**Non-Identification and Identity**
- The teaching challenges the tendency to build identity around emotional states
- The language shift from "I am angry" to "There is anger" is presented as a practical and liberating reframe
- This has broad relevance for how individuals relate to mood, mental health, and self-concept
**Self-Compassion as Practice, Not Prerequisite**
- Compassion toward oneself is not required to begin the practice — the seed metaphor allows entry even when compassion feels unavailable
- This lowers the barrier for those who struggle with self-kindness
**The Body as a Repository of Experience**
- Somatic memory of peaceful or meaningful states can serve as a resource during difficult times
- The body, not just the mind, holds the record of what is possible
**Meditation and the Natural Mind**
- The comparison of suppressing thought to suppressing hearing or smell reframes the "blank mind" ideal as unnatural
- This insight is accessible and relevant for anyone new to or frustrated by meditation practice
**Stillness as Choice**
- Participants are reminded that the stillness of meditation is a choice, not a trap
- This framing supports autonomy and reduces resistance to the practice
