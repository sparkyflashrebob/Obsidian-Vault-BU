### Outline
### The Two Wings to Freedom in Buddhist Teaching

- Mindfulness is identified by the Buddha as a fundamental practice in the pursuit of peace for oneself and others, cultivated through present-moment awareness.

- Two essential wings to freedom are described, the first being wisdom and the second being compassion, both considered equally necessary for the journey toward liberation.

- Compassion is defined as love for all of it, encompassing love in the midst of sorrows, challenges, difficulties, and loss, as well as love in the midst of beauty, awe, and curiosity.

### The Brahma Viharas as the Divine Abodes of the Heart

- Four qualities known as the Brahma Viharas are introduced as the sacred practices central to this retreat, often described as the qualities of the awakened heart.

- "Brahma" is explained to mean sublime or noble, referring to something beautiful and sacred, while "Vihara" derives from the word for monastery, meaning a place of refuge, clarity, and safety.

- These practices are understood both as something to cultivate and as qualities already calling from within, likened to a deep sun of Buddha nature already radiant with unconditional love beneath layers of conditioning, confusion, and intergenerational trauma.

- The four qualities are named as Metta (loving kindness), Karuna (compassion), Mudita (appreciative joy), and Upekkha (equanimity), described as facets of one jewel representing the awakened and loving heart.

### The Meaning and Nature of Metta (Loving Kindness)

- The Pali word "metta" is traced to two source meanings, the first being "gentle rain," evoking an image of nourishment and moisture falling indiscriminately on everything it touches.

- A second source word, pronounced "mit," is explained to mean "fat with kindness," suggesting a wholehearted trust and faith in kindness as a guiding orientation.

- Metta is described as an energy of friendliness and warmth that sees the good in oneself and others even amid imperfections and challenges, offering a nourishing and softening quality to the heart and mind.

- This quality is identified as foundational for wisdom practices, as it is significantly more difficult to cultivate continuity of mindfulness when the heart and mind are in contracted, aversive, or craving states.

### The Metta Sutta and the Buddha's Teaching on Loving Kindness

- A version of the Metta Sutta, updated in language for Western lay practitioners, is shared as the essence of the Buddha's instruction on how to incline toward and practice with loving kindness.

- Qualities encouraged in the practitioner include being stable, clear, honest, gentle in speech, humble, contented, unburdened, simple, peaceful, calm, wise, and skillful.

- The aspiration "may all beings be at ease" is extended universally to all living beings without omission, whether weak or strong, great or mighty, medium, short, or small, seen or unseen, near or far, born or yet to be born.

- An image of a wise and resourced parent protecting their only child with boundless heart is offered as a metaphor for the quality of care with which loving kindness is to be radiated toward all living beings.

- Radiating kindness is described as extending upward to the skies, downward to the depths, outward and unbounded, freed from hatred and ill will, and sustainable in all postures of daily life.

### The Relationship Between Mindfulness and Kindness as Acts of Remembering

- The Pali word "sati," translated as mindfulness, is noted to literally mean "to remember," framing the practice of mindfulness as a continual act of recalling and returning to the present moment.

- Kindness is understood in the same way, as something innately available that is recalled and chosen in moments of awareness rather than something that must be created from nothing.

- A quote from teacher Christina Feldman is shared, describing the most profound shift a human being can make as the movement from aversion to kindness and from abandoning to befriending, representing a shift from patterns of suffering to patterns of well-being and peace.

### The Practice Structure of Loving Kindness Meditation

- Well-wishing phrases are explained as a skillful tool introduced by Buddhist scholars after the Buddha's death to help practitioners incline toward kindness when directly radiating love to all beings is not immediately accessible.

- Four short-form phrases are offered as the basis for practice, centered on wishes for safety, peace, health, and ease of well-being.

- The traditional sequence of practice moves from self, to a benefactor, to a dear friend, to a neutral person, to a difficult person, and finally to all beings, beginning where love is most easily accessible to build momentum.

- A study by Kristin Neff from Mindful Self Compassion is cited, finding that 78% of Western people are more able to offer compassion to others than to themselves, which motivates a modified sequence for this session.

- A simplified version is proposed for this session, beginning with a being that naturally evokes a smile, then using that momentum to include oneself, and finally opening the heart to all beings encountered through the mind's eye.

- Three anchors are offered for the practice, including the visual image or felt sense of the invoked being, the physical sensations in the area of the heart and torso, and the well-wishing phrases themselves.

### Guided Loving Kindness Meditation Session

- Practitioners are invited to bring to mind a being that naturally makes them smile, whether a dear friend, a pet, a wild creature, or someone whose presence in the world brings gladness.

- Phrases are offered first toward the chosen being, wishing them safety, peace, health, and ease, with the intention of cultivating goodwill and benevolence.

- The practice then expands to include both the chosen being and oneself together, using phrases such as "may you and I be safe" to begin including the self within the circle of care.

- Holding oneself in love and care is framed as a wise and important part of both practice and life, with phrases directed inward toward oneself.

- The final phase of the meditation invites the mind and heart to travel the earth, sending well-wishes to all beings, known and unknown, near and far, flourishing or struggling, young or old, human and non-human.

- Silence is offered in the final portion of the session, with practitioners encouraged to continue with phrases, hold themselves in kindness, radiate kindness from the heart, or return to mindfulness within the field of aspiration and care.

### Next Steps

- The loving kindness practice introduced in this session is to be continued on several other days of the retreat as part of the broader program.

- Practitioners are encouraged to carry the orientation of kindness as a moment-to-moment choice throughout the retreat, applying it both inwardly toward their own experience and outwardly toward the community.

- The heart practices are positioned as an ongoing thread woven throughout the retreat's journey toward peace, supporting and deepening the mindfulness and insight practices being cultivated.

### Detailed Subject Matter
- **The Two Wings to Freedom**
  - The Buddha taught that both wisdom and compassion are essential for liberation
  - Wisdom is cultivated through present-moment awareness and mindfulness practice
  - Compassion is described as love for all of it — in the midst of sorrows, challenges, loss, beauty, and awe
- **The Brahma Viharas**
  - "Brahma" means sublime or noble — something beautiful and sacred
  - "Vihara" derives from the word for monastery — a place of refuge, clarity, and safety
  - The four divine abodes are described as qualities of the awakened heart
  - They are likened to facets of one jewel — the jewel of cultivating a loving heart
  - The four are: Metta (loving kindness), Karuna (compassion), Mudita (appreciative joy), Upeksha (equanimity)
- **The Meaning of Metta**
  - One source word describes metta as "gentle rain" — nourishing and moistening everything it falls on, indiscriminately
  - Another source word, pronounced "mit," means "fat with kindness" — trusting or having faith in kindness
  - Metta is an energy of friendliness and warmth, seeing the good in self and others even amid imperfections
  - It is described as a nourishing, softening, friendly energy
- **Metta as Foundation for Mindfulness**
  - It is more difficult to cultivate continuity of mindfulness when the heart and mind are in a contracted, aversive, or craving state
  - When the heart and mind are nourished by metta, there is more ease in insight practice and concentration
  - Metta supports the conditions for mindfulness and concentration to arise
- **The Metta Sutta**
  - Presented as a slightly updated version for Western lay practitioners
  - Key instructions include: being stable and clear, honest and gentle in speech, humble, contented, unburdened, peaceful, calm, wise, and skillful
  - The aspiration extends to all beings — weak or strong, great or mighty, medium, short or small, seen and unseen, near and far, born and to be born
  - Radiating kindness is described as boundless, spreading upward to the skies and downward to the depths, outward and unbounded
  - Sustaining this recollection is called "the sublime abiding"
- **Well-Wishing Phrases**
  - Introduced by Buddhist scholars after the Buddha's death as a skillful means
  - Phrases give language to the mind to help it incline toward kindness
  - Planting seeds — flowers are not immediately visible but the action is still skillful
  - Short-form phrases used in this session: "May you be safe," "May you be peaceful," "May you be as healthy as possible," "May you live with ease"
- **Sequence of Beings in Practice**
  - Traditional sequence: self, benefactor, dear friend, neutral person, difficult person, all beings
  - Modified sequence offered in this session due to Western self-criticism patterns: being that naturally makes you smile → self → all beings
  - The idea is to start where love is already accessible and build momentum
- **Guided Meditation Structure**
  - Three anchors offered: visual image or felt sense of the being, physical experience in the torso/heart area, and the phrases themselves
  - Practice also described as useful for cultivating concentration and single-focus of mind
  - Session moved through: smile-being → self with smile-being → self alone → all beings → silent practice
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
- **Buddha Nature as Inner Sun**
  - Eve Decker described Buddha nature as a deep sun already radiant with unconditional love within each person
  - Conditioning, confusion, and intergenerational trauma are layers that obscure this innate radiance
  - Practice is the process of working from the outside inward, moving toward that inner light
- **Sati — Remembering as Mindfulness**
  - The Pali word "sati," translated as mindfulness, literally means "to remember"
  - Mindfulness practice is fundamentally about recalling and returning to the present moment
  - Kindness operates the same way — it is remembered, chosen, returned to
  - All humans have the innate capacity to be present and kind; conditioning moves us away from it
- **The Shift from Aversion to Kindness**
  - Described as the most profound shift a human being can make
  - Moving from abandoning to befriending, from creating patterns of suffering to cultivating patterns of well-being and peace
  - Heart practices are described as an essential thread in the journey to peace
- **Western Culture and Self-Criticism**
  - Self-criticism described as epidemic in Western culture
  - A study by Kristin Neff found that 78% of Western people are more able to offer compassion to others than to themselves
  - This cultural pattern informed the modified sequence of the loving kindness practice offered in this session
- **Kindness and Well-Being**
  - Choosing kindness over habitual self-criticism moves a person into the territory of "tend and befriend"
  - Kindness generates well-being and opens qualities of care, connection, creativity, collaboration, and openness
  - These are described as the qualities of well-being itself
- **Community as a Field of Love**
  - Extending friendliness to others cycles back to the precepts
  - Creates a community or field of love, safety, and warmth for all navigating retreat together

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- **Self-Criticism as a Mental Obstacle**
  - The automaticity of the critical mind is described as epidemic in Western culture
  - This automatic self-criticism can take charge before awareness arises
  - When awareness is present, there is a choice point — to follow habitual self-criticism or to choose kindness
  - The modified practice sequence was designed specifically to address this challenge
- **Contracted, Aversive, or Craving States**
  - These mental states make it more difficult to cultivate continuity of mindfulness
  - Metta practice is offered as a way to soften and nourish the heart and mind before or alongside insight practice
- **Mind Wandering During Practice**
  - Acknowledged as a common occurrence during sitting practice
  - Loving kindness practice with phrases offered as a tool to cultivate concentration and single-focus of mind when the mind is wandering
- **Health Acknowledgment in Phrases**
  - The well-wishing phrase "May you be as healthy as possible" reflects an acknowledgment of the unpredictability of the body
  - Health is wished for "as much as is available" — recognizing physical limitations and impermanence
