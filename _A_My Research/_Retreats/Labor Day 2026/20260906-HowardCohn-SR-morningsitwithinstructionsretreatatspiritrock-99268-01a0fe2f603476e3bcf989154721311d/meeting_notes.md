### Outline
### The Paradox of Meditation Practice and the Nature of Awareness

- Scattered attention is a common starting point in meditation, and the practice invites practitioners to work with two natural qualities of the mind rather than forcing change.

- Natural awareness is described as an ever-present given that cannot be switched off, yet it is often unappreciated and unstable due to the mind's tendency to wander among mental narratives.

- Gathering attention and harmonizing it with the physical body is presented as the first key quality, described as bringing awareness into the same location as the fathom-long body.

- Sustaining that gathered connection is identified as the second key quality, with the repeated return to gathering and sustaining forming the core of the practice.

- As attention narrows through this process, a settling of the nervous system naturally occurs, along with a feeling of comfort and stability referred to as ekagata or one-pointedness.

- A central paradox is introduced whereby narrowing attention to a single point paradoxically gives rise to an increasing experience of spaciousness, in which the dividing line between self and others begins to dissolve.

- This expansive quality is connected to the Tibetan concept of the sky-like nature of awareness, described as the underlying ground of both life and death.

- Loving awareness is characterized as a welcoming openness at the heart of every person that becomes obscured by the busyness of daily life, with the purpose of practice being to return to that inner spaciousness.

### Guided Meditation on Sky-Like Awareness

- A comfortable and luxurious posture is recommended as a foundation, allowing the body and attention to ease into one another until the distinction between them dissolves.

- Awareness is guided to be experienced as having no height, depth, color, form, or inside and outside, described as open, welcoming, and sky-like.

- Sounds are introduced as objects of awareness, with practitioners invited to let hearing happen by itself without naming, judging, or effortfully listening.

- Physical sensations are then brought into focus, described not as a solid body with parts but as flickering points of feeling like stars in a vast evening sky.

- Moods, emotions, thoughts, and images are subsequently included as objects of awareness, characterized as having no roots or home, like a bird's footprint in emptiness, arising and passing without impact.

- Awareness itself is described as perfectly still, immovable, and motionless, with no reaching toward or pushing away from any experience.

- Practitioners are invited to look within the nature of awareness and recognize it as clear, open, empty, not made of anything, yet containing all experience without being bound by it.

- The meditation closes with repeated encouragement to rest in this natural great peace, described as the effortless home of the weary, trying mind.

### The Sky Metaphor and Its Limitations

- The sky is clarified as a metaphor only, sharing with awareness the qualities of vastness and openness but not identical to it.

- The most important distinction between the sky and awareness is that awareness is conscious, cognizant, and capable of feeling affection, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, which the sky cannot do.

- Additional metaphors from other traditions are acknowledged, including the mountain from Advaita, the ocean from various teachings, and a flame or crystal representing lucidity.

- Leonard Cohen's phrase "if you don't become the ocean, you'll be seasick every day" is cited as a further illustration of the ocean metaphor for awareness.

- A practical reminder is offered that the experience of sky-like awareness is always available through and within the physical body, and practitioners are encouraged to anchor attention in bodily experience if the spaciousness feels overwhelming.

- Awareness is described as having no fixed location in the head despite conventional pointing, meaning there is always space available regardless of physical circumstances.

- A reframe is offered in which everyone present in one's life is understood as part of the space of one's awareness rather than being separate, encouraging a sense of accommodation and goodwill toward others.

### Integration of Loving Awareness into Ordinary Life

- Andy McGuire raises the challenge of integrating the teaching that one is consciousness or loving awareness with the reality of having desires, needs, and goals as an individual person.

- A concern is expressed that fully resting in loving awareness might remove motivation to accomplish anything or pursue goals.

- Howard Cohn responds that the answer is a matter of discovery rather than intellectual resolution, and that it unfolds organically rather than through thinking.

- As one becomes less bound by personal preoccupations, the natural expression of that openness is described as evolving into care for others, service, and skillful action that avoids harm.

- Motivation for one's life is said to gradually shift from a self-centered orientation toward a broader concern for family, community, and collective wellbeing.

- The question is encouraged to remain open as a living inquiry, as it surfaces underlying intentions and reveals where faith has been placed in things that do not satisfy the deepest longing.

- Andy follows up by asking whether awareness and the personal story of Andrew could be understood as two separate entities.

- Howard clarifies that they are not two entities but rather expressions of a single dance of consciousness playing with itself, and cautions against adopting a fixed idea about this.

### The Relationship Between Effort and Stillness in Practice

- A participant describes the experience of noticing that effortful attempts to slow thinking produce a temporary clearing that feels enjoyable, while slipping back into thinking feels slippery, warm, and easy like entering a hot tub.

- Returning to awareness is described by the participant as effortful and somewhat sad, raising the question of whether coming back to awareness could ever feel as natural as drifting away from it.

- Howard affirms that the ability to know the state of the heart or mind in any given moment is itself the essence of the practice, regardless of whether that state is easeful or difficult.

- Stillness produced by trying to stop the mind is characterized as brittle and short-lived, whereas stillness arising from non-judgmental openness is described as more durable and genuinely relaxing.

- Thoughts are reframed as discontinuous and as just another sense experience, meaning that natural quieting occurs in the gaps between them when the practitioner is not imposing effort.

- The involvement of a doer or controller is identified as introducing tension, while allowing and non-doing are presented as the conditions for a more stable and lasting quality of stillness.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Paradox of Meditation**
- The scattered mind is invited to use two natural qualities: gathering and sustaining
- Gathering attention means bringing awareness into the same location as the body
- Sustaining means maintaining that connection, returning again and again
- Narrowing focus paradoxically produces spaciousness and a dissolving of the sense of separation between self and other
**Ekagata — One-Pointedness**
- Described as connecting to a single point of life right where it touches the practitioner
- Produces a settling of the nervous system and a sense of stability in attention
- Acts as a doorway into the sky-like nature of awareness
**Mind Essence vs. Mind Activity**
- Mind is often understood as the thinking apparatus, but in the teachings it has two aspects: mind activity and mind essence
- Mind essence, or heart essence, is simply being aware
- This essence is clear, open, empty, not made of anything, yet contains all experience without being bound by it
**Sky-Like Nature of Awareness (Tibetan Tradition)**
- Awareness is described as having no height, depth, color, form, inside, or outside
- The sky is used as a metaphor for vastness, openness, and impartiality
- Key distinction: unlike the sky, awareness is conscious, cognizant, and caring — it responds with compassion to pain, joy to joy, and equanimity to the full range of experience
- Other metaphors mentioned: mountain (unshakable), crystal (lucid), flame/lamp (shining), ocean (Advaita tradition)
**Integration into Daily Life**
- The question of how to integrate the experience of being loving awareness with having desires, needs, and goals was raised
- Howard Cohn responded that the answer comes through discovery, not thinking
- As one feels less bound by personal preoccupations, motivation naturally evolves toward service, care, and not causing harm
- This shift is organic and gradual — practitioners are advised not to make sudden life changes immediately after retreat
- The individual self and awareness are not two separate entities; the self is described as an expression of consciousness playing with itself
**The Nature of Thinking in Practice**
- Trying to stop thinking can produce a momentary stilling, but it is brittle and falls apart quickly
- Organic stillness arises through non-judgmental openness and welcoming the arising and fading of thoughts
- Thinking is to be regarded as just another sense experience
- The act of noticing what the mind is doing is described as the essence of the practice
**The Body and Awareness**
- Awareness depends on the fathom-long body for its experience to be available
- Practitioners are encouraged to anchor attention in the body if the spaciousness feels overwhelming
- Awareness has no fixed location and is not confined to the head
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Awareness as the Ground of Existence**
- Awareness cannot be stopped — attempting to stop being aware reveals that awareness is the ground of existence itself
- This is not a philosophical position but something to be directly verified through experience
**The Sky-Like Nature and Its Limits as Metaphor**
- The sky metaphor points to vastness, openness, and impartiality but falls short because awareness is conscious and caring
- The Tibetan tradition holds that this loving awareness underlies the whole of life and death
- The point of practice is to return to this inner spaciousness, which is described as the very nature of life
**Awareness, Interconnection, and Relationships**
- Because awareness has no fixed location, everyone in one's life is understood to be part of the space of one's awareness
- This reframe is offered as an encouragement to cultivate goodwill toward those who appear in one's field
- There is always room in awareness, regardless of the sense of physical confinement
**Motivation and the Evolution of Intention**
- Practice can clarify whether one's intentions are placed in things that truly satisfy the deepest longing
- Motivation can shift from self-centered goals toward benefit for family, community, and the wider world
- This is described as an organic process, not something to be forced or intellectualized
**The Ocean Metaphor and Equanimity**
- The Advaita tradition uses the ocean to describe the mind: the world moves on the surface, but the depths remain unmoved
- Leonard Cohen's quote is offered as a teaching on the necessity of finding this depth
