# Dependent Origination: Seeing the Dharma
## Week 8: Closing Talk - Spontaneous Imagery and Dharma Practice
**Instructor**: Gary Buck (2015)  
**Parent Course**: [[Dependent Origination - Course Syllabus and Overview]]

---

## Weekly Inquiries, Practice Instructions & Source Readings

Having spoken about the usefulness of spontaneous imagery in Dharma practice

	I should also mention a bit about the downside\.

It seems that the reason that in some traditions

there is such a wariness about such imagery

is that it’s easy for us to get fascinated with or attached to it\.

For most of us because such imagery is something unusual

when it happens in our practice it’s easy to get excited about it\.

It can be seductive giving rise to wanting mind, wanting more\.

Or there can be a strong tendency to identify with the imagery

in ways that aren’t helpful\.

Such imagery can result in an inflated sense of self,

feeling that the imagery is an indication

that we ourselves are special in some way

as practitioners or spiritual beings\.

Or the imagery might be confusing\.

“Why is this happening to me?”

“What does this mean?”

“Can I really trust this image? Maybe it’s just Mara playing a trick on me?”

So it’s important to be on the lookout for these kinds of reactions

if imagery does arise in your practice\.

So I suggest that relative to such imagery,

you simply note it as a passing experience

without spending much time trying to figure it out,

while remaining open to the possibility

that it’s significance or meaning will make itself clear in time\.

It may be useful to remember that imagery is one medium

that the subliminal part of ourselves

uses to communicate to our conscious side\.

If the message is at first unclear to our conscious mind,

if it’s an important message,

it’s quite likely that our subliminal side will try again

until it’s message gets through\.

I’d like to speak a bit about Dharma practice

with this teaching of Dependent Origination in mind\.

I suggest to you, based on my own experience,

	that this process of Dependent Origination is most easily noticed

relative to sight and hearing\.

This is especially true for those early links

where our relationship to space,

inner and outer, subject and object,  are being structured

because the objects of these two senses

usually appear to us as being separate from us by a spatial gap\.

Yet mindfulness of these sense doors is usually less emphasized

in the way that mindfulness is taught\.

In the Tibetan tradition, they say the sight is the foremost sense

	in the way is shapes our sense of self

and our relationship to the world around us\.

As such, it’s also the sense which affords us the richest context

in which to explore these aspects of our experience\.

For me, once I realized this, I only sat with my eyes wide open for many years\.

I’d like to conclude tonight by saying a bit about

the nature of consciousness

in light of this view of Dependent Origination\.

At the deepest level of Dependent Origination,

	the seeds of consciousness begin with the first link,

that of avijja or ignorance\.

At this link, the bare structure of a field of perception

and its center, or point of reference,

get established in what I’ve described as

an explosion of energy at the heart center\.

One way that I’ve come to describe this event to myself

is as the creation of what could be called

the bubble of sensory perception\.

This bubble, so to speak, represents the domain

in which our sensory experience will unfold\.

If you’re familiar with the Don Juan literature,

	 I find an interesting parallel in the notion of the Island of the Tonal

within the Nagual Sea\.

It’s within this bubble, or on this Island of the Tonal,

that everything that is personal will then unfold\.

The bubble then evolves in specific ways\.

With vision, for example, with the advent of the next link of sankhara,

at the outer limits of the bubble

some parts of the periphery, of the visual field,

seem to move closer or further from us,

giving this sense that there is a visual depth of field\.

At the next three links, something interesting happens\.

It’s as if a new bubble begins to form within the larger bubble or field\.

Initially at the torso, then at the face,

and finally at the surface of the eye itself,

a new boundary or façade is experientially sculpted

out of subtle energy activity,

which we experience as the boundary of the physical body\.

This photo of a balloon within a balloon __SLIDE__

might give some imagistic notion

of what I’m describing from a subtle energy perspective\.

Of course the actual visual field is much larger

relative to the inner balloon of bodily boundary

than the photo depicts\.

As this process unfolds, knowing becomes encapsulated

within this inner balloon of the bodily boundary\.

And from that position, it is felt to be a knowing subject

separate from the visual field,

the outer balloon, of objects it knows\.

It has now become consciousness in Edinger’s sense of

knowing with an other\.

From this perspective, consciousness is not really a thing,

not a noun, but rather an activity, a happening,

or, as Andrew Olendzki says, an event\.

Prior to this fully dualistic form of consciousness

that forms the basis for what I call

the sense of self as sensory organism,

there is this sense of our being simply that which knows\.

This layer of our being is a subliminal aspect

of every sensory and psychological experience of self\.

Because it remains mostly subliminal, it ordinarily seems to us

to be continuous and unchanging,

unconstructed, not dependent on conditions,

Rather it seems to be just simply the way things are\.

Sensory experience and our psychological responses and reactions

to our sensory input

seem to be happening to that underlying sense of self\.

Because this layer of our sense of self is so deeply subliminal,

it rarely gets examined or called into question

until we start mindfulness practice,

and even then not until our mindfulness practice

reaches a particular depth and maturity\.

I’d like to suggest to you that it’s this largely subliminal layer

of our sense of self,

	and the early links of Dependent Origination which create it,

	that is responsible for this apparent sense we have of ourselves

as having continuity through time\.

The deepest layer of sense of self, especially self as that which knows,

is actually the source of our experience

of the ordinary __apparent continuity of self__\.

Beneath this subtle form of consciousness

lies the domain of our original nature\.

For this domain I like to reserve the term awareness,

or even better primordial awareness\.

You may recall that as we passed through the earliest links

of vijnana, sankhara, and avijja

during our deconstructive journey through the 12 nidanas,

the experiences I described were those of a deepening integration

of what we might call knowing, on the one hand,

and appearing, on the other\.

When avijja falls away and this integrative process is complete,

	what we’re left with is especially difficult to describe in words\.

This term primordial awareness is a pretty good term\.

It suggests that this is the earliest or original form of knowing\.

Another term used for this territory prior to avijja is suchness,

	another good term, and one that at least to my ear and mind,

is more suggestive of  the other aspect of this realm, that of appearing\.

So two good terms, but at least for me, each has the subtle flavor

of one or the other aspect of this utterly singular activity

that is both knowing and appearing at the same time\.

Of the two I like suchness better\.

First of all, we don’t generally tend to identify as much or as deeply

with appearing as we do with knowing\.

And in this that these words point towards,

there’s no identifying whatsoever\.

But maybe that’s just me\.

Maybe I just tend to identify more with knowing than appearing\.

Secondly, while we have words that are used as the opposite of awareness,

	such as unaware, I don’t know of any opposite in the English Language

for the word ‘suchness\.’

Even the word ‘nonesuch’ means something like “that which is unparalleled,”

 	which retains the flavor of being utterly singular\.

So with this brief commentary on the challenge

of finding good words to express

the subtle experiences we’ve been discussing

my own prepared words come to an end\.
