# Stories, Practice Experiences, and Recollections of Ajahn Sumedho

> *"When we are willing to look at our pettiness, our irritability, our greed, and our self-righteousness without judging or hiding them, the door to the Unconditioned swings wide open. Awareness itself is never grumpy."* — **Luang Por Sumedho**

---

## 🌾 The Teaching Method of Luang Por Sumedho (b. 1934)
Luang Por Sumedho’s teaching style is celebrated across the Buddhist world for its candid vulnerability, earthy self-deprecating humor, and relentless focus on the **witnessing position (*Buddho*)**. Rather than presenting himself as an aloof, perfected spiritual master, he openly shares his battles with doubt, anger, cultural shock, physical discomfort, and perfectionism—demonstrating how our very neuroses and emotional reactions are the ultimate fodder for insight.

---

## 🚢 1. Early Life, Disillusionment & Seeking Truth

### 1. The US Navy Medic & San Francisco Bookstores (1950s)
> During the Korean War era, young Robert Jackman served as a medical corpsman in the United States Navy stationed in the Pacific. While serving on ships and hospital wards, he witnessed the sheer fragility of human life, physical trauma, and the superficiality of modern consumer culture.
> 
> During shore leave in San Francisco, he would scour second-hand bookstores in North Beach looking for the very few English books on Buddhism available in the 1950s (such as D.T. Suzuki and Edward Conze). He realized that neither academic degrees nor military discipline could answer his existential question: *How do we find peace in a world bound to suffering?*

### 2. The Peace Corps in Borneo & Nong Khai Solitary Year (1966–1967)
> After serving in the Peace Corps teaching in Sabah (Borneo), he traveled to Thailand and took novice ordination at Wat Sri Saket in Nong Khai on the Lao border. 
> 
> For his first year, he lived completely alone in a tiny wooden hut (*kuti*), practicing intense concentration meditation for up to eighteen hours a day. With no teacher to guide him, his rigid American perfectionism took over: he drove himself into extreme mental tension, alternating between ecstatic states of absorption and deep despair when his focus slipped. Realizing that sheer willpower was only reinforcing his ego, he sought out a genuine master and was brought to Wat Pah Pong in Ubon Ratchathani to meet Ajahn Chah.

## 🌴 2. The Crucible of Wat Pah Pong (Under Ajahn Chah)

### 3. Arriving at Wat Pah Pong (No English, Total Surrender)
> When Sumedho arrived at Wat Pah Pong in 1967, he was the first Western disciple to live there. No one spoke English, the monastery had no electricity or running water, and life was brutally austere.
> 
> When Sumedho asked to stay, Ajahn Chah laid down an absolute condition: *"If you want to train here, you must stay for at least five years, obey all rules of the Vinaya, eat whatever is put in your bowl, and receive no special privileges because you are a foreigner."* Sumedho bowed and agreed—a surrender that transformed his life.

### 4. The Bitter Borupet Tea ("Fill 'er Up!")
> During an all-night meditation vigil at Wat Pah Pong, the novices brought out steaming copper kettles to help the monks stay awake. Sumedho, expecting hot black tea or sweet coffee, eagerly held out his large American mug and said, *"Fill 'er up!"*
> 
> The kettle was filled with hot *borupet*—a notoriously foul, intensely bitter medicinal jungle vine. Sumedho took an enormous gulp and gasped in horror: *"Waaaaa!"* 
> 
> Ajahn Chah looked over from his seat, chuckled warmly, and said: *"Drink it all up, Sumedho. Clinging to pleasant taste is dukkha."* Sumedho sat all night nursing the bitter brew, realizing how violently his mind fought unpleasant sensory contact.

### 5. Wearing Heavy Robes in 100°F Heat & Barefoot Alms
> In the scorching heat of Northeast Thailand, monastics must wear three layers of thick, hand-dyed cotton robes, fully wrapped around the neck and arms whenever leaving the monastery grounds. Every morning, they walked barefoot for miles along sharp gravel and mud roads on alms round (*piṇḍapāta*).
> 
> Sumedho would walk sweating profusely, his feet blistered, with his mind screaming in fury: *"Why do we have to wear these ridiculous heavy blankets in 100-degree heat? Why can't we wear sandals? The Buddha never intended us to suffer like this!"*
> 
> Then one morning, the insight struck him: *The heat is just hot. The gravel is just hard. It is my internal monologue, my hatred and resentment of the conditions, that is causing 100% of my suffering.* When he stopped resisting the heat, the suffering vanished.

### 6. Termites in the Kuti & "This I Cannot Endure"
> Living in a tiny wooden kuti during the monsoon season, Sumedho was plagued by swarms of mosquitoes, intense humidity, chronic dysentery, and termites eating the roof beams above him, dropping sawdust onto his head day and night.
> 
> Exhausted and feverish, he threw himself onto his sleeping mat in tears, shouting aloud: *"I CANNOT ENDURE THIS ANYMORE!"*
> 
> Right in that moment of absolute collapse, an inner voice asked: *"Who is the one saying 'I cannot endure'? Look at awareness right now."* 
> 
> He paused and noticed that the awareness knowing the thought *"I cannot endure"* was completely silent, peaceful, unburdened, and perfectly enduring the moment. That breakthrough became his foundational teaching on **the witness (*Buddho*)**.

### 7. Obsessing Over the Mango (The Food Test)
> In a forest monastery with only one meager meal a day, food becomes a powerful magnet for obsession. One morning on alms round, someone placed a perfect, ripe, fragrant yellow mango into Sumedho's bowl. 
> 
> For the next two hours during chanting and meditation, Sumedho couldn't stop thinking about eating that mango. He planned how he would peel it and savor every bite.
> 
> When they sat down to eat, Ajahn Chah looked across the hall, pointed at Sumedho's bowl, and motioned for the mango. Ajahn Chah took the mango, sliced it, and casually tossed it to a monastery dog. Sumedho felt a surge of murderous rage—followed immediately by a flash of awakening as he saw the absurdity of his clinging.

### 8. "I Like, But I Don't Want" (The Nursing Students)
> One afternoon, a class of beautiful young nursing students from the local medical college in Ubon visited Wat Pah Pong in crisp turquoise and white uniforms to pay respects. 
> 
> Sumedho felt an intense wave of romantic and sexual desire arise. Instead of suppressing it or feeling guilty, he watched the desire closely and noticed: *"I can appreciate beauty without having to possess it. I like this pleasant sight, but I don't have to want it."* 
> 
> This gave birth to his famous formula: **"I like, but I don't want"**—disentangling aesthetic appreciation from the grasping impulse of *taṇhā*.

### 9. Sweeping the Leaves & The "Lazy Monks"
> Every afternoon at 3:00 PM, all monks were required to sweep the extensive dirt pathways of Wat Pah Pong using long bamboo brooms. Sumedho, with his energetic American work ethic, would sweep vigorously, sweating profusely, while noticing some younger Thai monks sweeping languidly or pausing to chat under a tree.
> 
> Sumedho's mind fumed with self-righteous anger: *"I'm doing all the work! These guys are lazy! I'm the only real monk here!"*
> 
> Ajahn Chah walked up behind him and tapped him on the shoulder: *"Sumedho, you are not sweeping leaves to clean the dirt. You are sweeping leaves to clean your mind. Why are you looking at the other monks? Look at the defilement in your own heart."*

### 10. The Annoying Monk & Irrational Aversion
> For months, Sumedho was consumed by an overwhelming, irrational hatred for a particular Thai monk who cleared his throat loudly and chewed his food noisily during morning meals. Sumedho would sit in his kuti plotting imaginary arguments and fantasizing about telling the monk off.
> 
> Finally, he realized: *"This monk hasn't done anything to harm me. He is just existing. My mind is creating a monster out of throat-clearing sound waves."* He used this realization to master the contemplation of aversion (*dosa*) and compassion (*mettā*).

### 11. Surprise 2-Hour Dhamma Talks in Broken Thai
> When Sumedho had only been a monk for a few years and spoke halting, broken Thai, Ajahn Chah would suddenly point at him in front of assemblies of 500 Thai villagers and announce: *"Tonight, the Western monk Sumedho will give the Dhamma talk for the next two hours!"*
> 
> Paralyzed with terror and stage fright, Sumedho would sit on the high Dhamma seat sweating through his robes. But having no prepared notes and limited vocabulary, he was forced to drop his intellect and speak directly from simple, present-moment awareness. It permanently cured him of performance anxiety and taught him to trust the Dhamma rather than ego performance.

## 🏰 3. Pioneer in the West: Chithurst & Amaravati (England)

### 12. Walking Alms in the London Mist (Hampstead, 1977)
> When Ajahn Chah brought Ajahn Sumedho to London in 1977 to establish the first forest branch in the West, they stayed in a small flat near Hampstead Heath. In keeping with the 2,500-year-old Vinaya tradition, Sumedho walked out every morning in robes with an alms bowl through the streets of London.
> 
> Bewildered Londoners had no idea what a Buddhist monk was—some thought he was a Hare Krishna, others offered him empty beer cans, fish-and-chip wrappers, or bus tokens. Sumedho learned to hold the alms bowl not as a beggar seeking food, but as an open vessel offering the world a silent blessing of presence.

### 13. Rebuilding the Ruined Chithurst House (Cittaviveka, 1979)
> In 1979, the Sangha was gifted a derelict Victorian estate in West Sussex called Chithurst House. The house was uninhabitable: the roof had collapsed, dry rot infested the timbers, pigeons and bats nested in the ceilings, and there was no central heating in the freezing English winter.
> 
> Rather than viewing manual labor as a distraction from meditation, Sumedho instituted the Thai forest ethic: mixing cement, carrying timber, stripping lead paint, and enduring freezing cold became the primary meditation practice. Chithurst became Cittaviveka, the first Theravada Forest Monastery in the Western hemisphere.

### 14. "That Which Is Aware Is Not Grumpy"
> During a brutal English winter at Amaravati, Sumedho woke up at 4:00 AM with a head cold, an aching back, and an intensely grumpy mood. Walking down the freezing cloister toward the morning meditation hall, he observed his inner dialogue complaining bitterly about the weather, the monastery administration, and the cold floor.
> 
> Suddenly, a burst of insight illuminated his mind:
> *"Wait a minute. The mood is grumpy. The thoughts are grumpy. But is the **awareness** of grumpiness grumpy? No! Awareness is completely spacious, peaceful, bright, and unaffected."*
> 
> This insight became one of his most famous signature teachings: **identifying with awareness rather than the mood**.

### 15. The "I'm Right, You're Wrong" Fetter
> As an abbot managing complex monastery politics and differing opinions among monastics, Sumedho discovered that the subtlest and most toxic defilement was the desire to be **right**.
> 
> *"You can be 100% factually right about a situation, and yet 100% bound by suffering because you are clinging to your rightness. Self-righteous indignation (*sakkāyaditthi*) is the hardest drug in the world to give up. True peace is not proving you are right; it is having the humility to let go of winning the argument."*

### 16. Discovering the Sound of Silence (*Nāda*)
> While meditating in the deep silence of the Sussex forests, Sumedho noticed a subtle, continuous, high-pitched vibrational sound in consciousness—what the ancient Indian yogis called the *Nāda* sound or the "Sound of Silence."
> 
> He realized that unlike the breath (which changes and stops) or visual objects, this inner sound is **always present** whenever the mind becomes still. He developed it into a cornerstone practice for thousands of Western students: using the inner sound as an anchor to dissolve discursive thinking and rest in pure, unconditioned knowing.

### 17. "Until the Penny Drops" (Dropping the Master's Mask)
> After over forty years in robes, having established dozens of monasteries across Europe, America, and Australasia, Luang Por Sumedho reflected on how easy it is to become attached even to the persona of "The Wise Old Abbot" or "The Great Meditation Master."
> 
> In his final talks before stepping down as abbot of Amaravati, he emphasized:
> *"Do not attach even to the teacher. Do not attach to conventions, robes, rituals, or titles. Practice over and over again until the penny drops: there is no permanent 'me' to protect or improve. There is only this timeless, luminous awareness here and now."*
