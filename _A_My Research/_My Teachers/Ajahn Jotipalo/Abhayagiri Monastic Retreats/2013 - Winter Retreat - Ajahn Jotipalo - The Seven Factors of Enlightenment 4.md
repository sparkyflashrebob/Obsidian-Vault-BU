### Theme of the Dharma Talk
How wise, radical reflection (*yoniso manasikāra*) shifts the mind from ontological reification ("what is that thing out there?") to phenomenological process ("how is this experience being constructed in awareness?"), enabling the awakening factor of investigation (*dhamma-vicaya*) to dissolve suffering.

### Condensed Version of the Teachings
Investigation (*dhamma-vicaya*) is not speculative philosophy or academic analysis; it is radical phenomenological seeing. Drawing on Ven. Ñāṇananda, Ajahn Jotipalo distinguishes between ontological questions (which assume substantial entities, egos, and external objects) and the Buddha's phenomenological approach of seeing conditions arise and pass away in direct experience. The engine of this investigation is *yoniso manasikāra*—directing attention to the root (*yoni*), observing how feeling, perception, and conceptualization interact. When attention looks at the process rather than getting ensnared in content, the illusion of a solid self collapses, revealing the natural peace of emptiness (*suññatā*).

### Expanded Version of the Teachings
- **Ontology vs. Phenomenology in Early Buddhism**: Conventional thinking asks ontological questions: *"What am I? Where did I come from? What is the world made of?"* The Buddha explicitly dismissed these questions as a thicket of views (*diṭṭhigahana*) that leads to bondage. Instead, the Buddha took a radical phenomenological stance: observing experience purely as a dynamic stream of dependently originated conditions (*paccaya*).
- **The Meaning of Yoniso Manasikāra**: Literally "making in the mind according to the womb or origin." It means looking back into the source of a mental event. Instead of tracking the storyline of anger or desire, wise attention observes: *"What feeling tone is present? What perception sparked this? Is there clinging here?"*
- **Dhamma-Vicaya as Awakening Factor**: Investigation is nourished by wise attention. When nourished, it distinguishes between wholesome (*kusala*) and unwholesome (*akusala*) qualities, and between the conditioned (*saṅkhata*) and the unconditioned (*asaṅkhata*).
- **Entry into Emptiness (*Suññatā*)**: By examining experience without the filter of "I" and "mine," phenomena reveal their empty, transparent nature. The discourse culminates in the classic canonical realization: *"Destroyed is birth, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more for this state of being."*

## 3. Comparative Framework: Ontological Inquiry vs. Phenomenological Dhamma-Vicaya

| Inquiry Dimension | Ontological Approach (Conventional / Proliferative) | Phenomenological Investigation (*Dhamma-Vicaya*) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Core Question** | *"What exists? Who am I? Why is this happening to me?"* | *"How is this mental state arising? What are its conditions?"* |
| **View of the Self** | Assumes an enduring observer or owner behind experience. | Discovers bare processes operating without a core (*anattā*). |
| **Mental Activity** | Conceptual proliferation (*papañca*) and philosophical debate. | Direct observation of impermanence, conditionality, and release. |
| **Role of Attention** | *Ayoniso manasikāra* (unwise attention caught in appearances). | *Yoniso manasikāra* (wise attention examining the origin and cessation). |
| **Fruit of Practice** | Views, opinions, and subtle conceit (*māna*). | Dispassion (*virāga*), cessation (*nirodha*), and liberation (*vimutti*). |
