Today I would like to give an overview of the various forms of Buddhist Mental Development and Mental Training. 
Mental Development is called Bhavana

In Buddhism they are two different words, Bhavana & Sati, but frequently the terms mindfulness and meditation are used interchangeably.
 
Bhavana, is a Pali term that literally means "development" or "cultivating"  or "producing".  
The word bhavana normally appears in conjunction with another word forming a compound phrase such as citta-bhavana (the development or cultivation of the heart/mind) or metta-bhavana (the development/cultivation of lovingkindness). When used on its own, bhavana signifies contemplation and 'spiritual cultivation' generally.

An example is of a farmer who performs bhavana when he or she prepares soil and plants a seed. 
It suggests naturalness, everydayness, ordinariness. 
It also suggests hope: no matter how fallow it has become, or damaged it may be, a field can always be cultivated — endlessly enhanced, enriched, developed — to produce a favorable and nourishing harvest.

In the Buddhist Pali Canon bhāvanā is often found in a compound phrase indicating personal, intentional effort over time with respect to the development of a particular faculty. For instance, in the Pali Canon and post-canonical literature one can find the following compounds:

citta-bhāvanā, is translated as "development of mind" or "development of consciousness."  which refer to samadhi

kāya-bhāvanā, is translated as "development of body." which refers to insight.

mettā-bhāvanā, is translated as the "cultivation" or "development of benevolence." This word does not actually exist in the Metta Sutta but the sutta mentions one should "cultivate" (bhavaye) a limitless heart of metta.

paññā-bhāvanā, is translated as "development of wisdom" or "development of understanding."   

samādhi-bhāvanā, is translated as "development of concentration."

The development (bhāvanā) of samatha-vipassana is praised and admired.  In one sutta (MN151, Discourse on Complete Purity) the Buddha states that a person who has developed samatha-vipassana (or any of the seven sets of Enlightenment-conducive qualities) "can abide happy and glad, training day and night in wholesome states". 

Subsequently, Theravada teachers have made use of the following compounds:
samatha-bhāvanā, meaning the development of tranquility.
vipassanā-bhāvanā, meaning the development of insight.

These are the two major types of meditation taught in the Theravada tradition and the two I am most aware of.  They espouse different mental skills, modes of functioning or qualities of consciousness.
You may have heard of vipassana meditation.  This is the type of meditation espoused by S.N. Goenka and others.  Vipassana can be translated as "Insight", a clear awareness of what is happening as it happens.  Samatha is translated as concentration or tranquility as it is a state in which the mind is brought to rest, focused only on one item and not allowed to wander.  

Finally, the word bhavana is sometimes translated into English as 'meditation' so that, for example, metta-bhavana may be translated as 'the meditation on loving-kindness'. Meditation is properly called dhyana (Sanskrit; Pali: jhāna), as practiced in samādhi, the 8th limb of the eightfold path.
