## 1. Teacher Profile & Teaching Lineage

Ven. Luang Por Jumnien Silasettaho (widely known in the West as Ajahn Jumnien) is one of Thailand's most revered meditation masters of the Southern Forest tradition and the abbot of Wat Tham Seua (Tiger Cave Monastery) in Krabi, Southern Thailand. Having begun deep contemplative practice at the tender age of five under the guidance of forest teachers and traditional shamans, he entered the Buddhist monastic order as a young man and spent decades practicing dhutaṅga (austere forest wandering) across the jungles, caves, and mountains of Thailand.

Renowned for his boundless joy, warmth, and profound teachings on radiant emptiness (*suññatā*), mindfulness of the heart-mind (*citta*), and the Four Brahmavihāras, Luang Por Jumnien offers a unique bridge between deep samādhi absorption, radical non-attachment, and joyful, engaged presence in daily life.

## 3. Core Contemplative Pillars of Luang Por Jumnien's Teachings

### The Emptiness of the Citta (*Suññatā*)
Luang Por teaches that awareness itself is fundamentally luminous and unentangled. Sensation, perception, and thought enter through the sense doors like visitors entering a room. When the mind rests in its natural, open posture, phenomena are recognized as transparent and unpossessable.

### The Heavy Bags Simile (The Two Arrows)
In one of his most celebrated teaching metaphors, Luang Por illustrates the distinction between raw physical sensation (the First Arrow) and added mental anguish (the Second Arrow) through the image of a laborer carrying heavy sacks on his shoulders. The gravitational weight is a physical fact of nature; suffering is created purely when the heart fights the weight, blames external conditions, or manufactures a victimized "self." When identification is abandoned, the body carries the load in bare sensation while the heart rests in total ease.

### The Four Immeasurables (*Brahmavihāra*)
For Luang Por Jumnien, Mettā (loving-kindness), Karuṇā (compassion), Muditā (sympathetic joy), and Upekkhā (equanimity) are not mere mental exercises, but the natural, unconstrained radiance of an unburdened mind. Practice is not about becoming cold or detached, but about cultivating boundless goodwill that embraces every being without reservation.
