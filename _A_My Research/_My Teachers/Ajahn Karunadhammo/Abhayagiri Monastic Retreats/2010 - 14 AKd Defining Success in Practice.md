* **Canonical Setting**: The town of Kesaputta in Kosala, where the Kālāmas approached the Buddha seeking discernment amidst confusing sectarian claims (*AN 3.65*), alongside the classic monastic training forests where the Buddha instructed monastics to balance the enlightenment factors during low-energy cycles (*SN 46.51*).
* **Contemporary Recording Venue**: Angela Center, Santa Rosa, California. Afternoon session inside the main meditation hall during the 2010 Abhayagiri Thanksgiving Monastic Retreat.

### 2. Immediate Circumstances & Assembly
* **Setting the Scene**: Day 4 of the retreat at 1:30 PM—the notorious "graveyard shift" of meditation retreats. The initial enthusiasm of the first few days has waned, the body is digesting lunch, and meditators are contending with heavy post-meal lethargy, physical stiffness, and dullness (*thīna-middha*). ### 3. Central Contemplative Theme
* **Theme**: Redefining what constitutes genuine "success" in Dhamma practice. Moving beyond the instrumental, performance-driven mindset that views meditation as mechanical technique mastery (e.g., forcing breath focus, striving for *jhāna*, rating sits by pleasantness), and shifting toward qualitative awareness: understanding conditionality, embracing the present moment as it is, using the Four Bases of Success (*Iddhipāda*), and embodying palliative acceptance.

## 📖 Executive Teachings: Condensed & Expanded

### Condensed Version of the Teachings
Meditators frequently suffer from a "report-card mentality," measuring meditative success solely by the presence of pleasant, tranquil states and labeling sits plagued by restlessness or sluggishness as personal failures. During low-energy periods (such as mid-afternoon torpor), attempting to force still concentration (*samatha*) often compounds frustration. The Buddha taught in the *Bojjhaṅga* framework that sluggish states call for the energizing enlightenment factors: investigation (*dhammavicaya*), energetic application (*viriya*), and spiritual joy (*pīti*).

Drawing on the *Kālāma Sutta* (*AN 3.65*), Ajahn Karunadhammo corrects the modern misinterpretation that the Buddha advocated "just doing whatever feels good." True discernment requires objective benchmarks: assessing actions by whether they lead to harm or long-term welfare, honoring ethical precepts, consulting virtuous exemplars, and cultivating the *Brahmavihāras*. 

Ajahn Karunadhammo shares his transformative experience as a nurse: in clinical training, the subconscious standard of success was curing every disease and restoring a youthful body. When encountering chronic illness, geriatrics, and palliative care, that rigid benchmark bred anxiety and burnout. Success had to be redefined as comfort, presence, care, and acceptance. In Dhamma practice, we must make the identical pivot: abandoning the demand for an idealized meditative performance, and returning to Ajahn Chah's fundamental instruction—*knowing and letting go* through the lens of impermanence (*anicca*), unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*), and not-self (*anattā*).

### Expanded Exposition of Doctrinal Principles

#### 1. Skillful Adaptation to Low-Energy Cycles (*Bojjhaṅga Alignment*)
A persistent error in meditation practice is applying passive calming techniques when the mind is dull, drowsy, or depressed. In the *Āhāra Sutta* (*SN 46.51*), the Buddha compares the mind to a small, sluggish fire. If one throws damp grass and water on a weak flame, it is extinguished; similarly, attempting to develop tranquility (*passaddhi*), concentration (*samādhi*), and equanimity (*upekkhā*) when the mind is sluggish only induces sleep. 

Instead, a sluggish mind must be fed the energizing factors:
* **Investigation of Dhammas (*Dhammavicaya*)**: Actively inquiring into present physical sensations, questioning the nature of resistance, and observing the arising and falling of mental states.
* **Energy / Effort (*Viriya*)**: Uplifting somatic posture, expanding the field of awareness, and generating heroic endurance.
* **Spiritual Rapture / Joy (*Pīti*)**: Evoking wholesome reflections on generosity, virtue, or spiritual gratitude to gladden the mind.

#### 2. The Kālāma Sutta Benchmarks vs. Modern Relativism
The *Kālāma Sutta* (*AN 3.65*) is frequently cited in modern Western circles as a license for spiritual consumerism—"if it feels good to you, it's true." Ajahn Karunadhammo meticulously dismantles this shallow interpretation. When the Kālāmas asked how to distinguish truth from falsehood among competing sects, the Buddha did not advocate subjective whim. He asked them to evaluate actions by their observable karmic fruit:
1. **Root Assessment**: Are states rooted in greed (*lobha*), hatred (*dosa*), or delusion (*moha*) conducive to well-being or harm?
2. **Moral Conduct (*Sīla*)**: Does abstaining from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, and intoxicants lead to blameless ease and social welfare?
3. **Consensus of the Wise (*Viññū-ppasattha*)**: How do spiritually mature, virtuous individuals evaluate these behaviors?
4. **The Four Immeasurables (*Brahmavihāra*)**: Purifying the mind through boundless loving-kindness (*mettā*), compassion (*karuṇā*), appreciative joy (*muditā*), and equanimity (*upekkhā*).

Direct experience must always be calibrated against these clear canonical benchmarks.

#### 3. The Four Bases of Success (*Iddhipāda*) in Contemplative Practice
While classically associated with spiritual potency and meditative attainments (*SN 51.13*), the Forest Tradition applies the *Iddhipāda* as an operational roadmap for meditative development:

```
  [1. Chanda]         -->   [2. Viriya]         -->   [3. Citta]         -->   [4. Vīmaṁsā]
Spiritual Interest        Persevering Energy       Attentive Dedication     Discerning Evaluation
(Desire to Practice)      (Application of Effort)  (Wholehearted Presence)  (Reflective Investigation)
```

1. **Chanda (Wholesome Aspiration / Desire)**: The initial spark of inspiration, typically born from directly confronting the unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*) of worldly pursuits and aspiring toward genuine liberation.
2. **Viriya (Persisting Energy)**: The vital stamina that takes up wholesome themes and sustains contemplative inquiry through fatigue and distraction.
3. **Citta (Attentive Application / Heart-Dedication)**: Wholehearted absorption and dedication of consciousness to the spiritual work at hand.
4. **Vīmaṁsā (Discerning Evaluation / Review)**: The essential reflective capacity that examines: *Is this working? Does this practice diminish unwholesome states and cultivate clarity, or is it creating tension and pride?* Without *vīmaṁsā*, practice calcifies into dogmatic routine.

#### 4. The Trap of Technique Fixation
Ajahn Karunadhammo shares his early lay practice of doggedly trying to force the mind onto the breath at the tip of the nose. Because his instructions were presented as the single "correct" technique, he spent years in deep constriction, irritation, and despair. When he entered monastic life as an anagārika, senior monks encouraged him to set aside the rigid focal point, broaden awareness, and explore other contemplation methods (such as body contemplation and spacious awareness). 

Techniques are rafts, not shrines. When we measure our worth by how quickly we enter *jhāna* or whether we can sit motionless for two hours, meditation becomes another arena for ego amplification and performance anxiety.

#### 5. Knowing and Letting Go (*Ajahn Chah’s Legacy*)
All meditation practices—breath awareness, loving-kindness, body contemplation—serve a single ultimate objective: creating the spaciousness necessary to see things as they are. The ultimate benchmark of spiritual progress is not ecstatic visionary experiences, but whether the mind can meet the arising of conditions—pleasant or painful, exalted or base—with clear comprehension (*sampajañña*) and relinquish attachment. 

As Ajahn Chah taught: *"Just know, and let go."* When we observe the subtle twitch of craving seeking pleasant sensations or averting from pain, we discern the inherent stress (*dukkha*) of clinging and allow conditions to cease naturally.

## 📜 Personal Stories & Practical Illustrations

### The Nursing School Awakening: Redefining Clinical Success
Ajahn Karunadhammo recounts his years as a nursing student and clinical practitioner before ordination:
> *"During the first years of nursing school and clinical practice, there was an implicit definition of success: people becoming healthy, getting over illness, and returning to the optimal state of a 25-year-old body full of vitality. That was the medical paradigm we absorbed.*
> 
> *It wasn’t until my final year, during clinical rotations in geriatrics, chronic illness, and death and dying, that this paradigm shattered. Doctors and nurses who were highly skilled in acute medicine experienced profound discomfort and distress when patients became terminally ill and cancer treatments failed. Their benchmark for success was thwarted; they couldn’t produce a cure, and their discomfort rose accordingly.*
> 
> *Our nursing instructors gave us profound advice: 'You have to change your goal. You have to change what you define as success. If you enter palliative care with the expectation of curing the patient, you will be disillusioned, you will burn out, and you will withdraw emotionally—making you useless to the people you are trying to serve. Success must be redefined as comfort, presence, care, and acceptance.'*
> 
> *That insight stayed with me for life. In meditation practice, we must make the identical pivot. If our definition of success is that the mind must be entirely free of hindrances, radiant with jhāna, and perfectly blissful right now, we set ourselves up for chronic disappointment. Success is being willing to experience things exactly as they are—with comfort, presence, and clear comprehension."*

## 📜 Full Talk Transcript

**Ajahn Karunadhammo:**

Just to shake things up a little bit, Ajahn Pasanno has asked us to share the microphone in the afternoons here. After a little bit of debate, it ended up being my turn. About an hour ago, it was Ajahn Yatiko’s turn, but *anicca* rears its head! 

This is day four—or at least the third full day of the retreat—and it is 1:30 in the afternoon. Retreat lore has it that this is probably about the worst time of the retreat for most people. Typically, on the third or fourth day, the bloom is off the rose. The initial excitement of coming on retreat has started to wane a little bit. The mind still hasn't fully settled, and the residual energy of the world is still making its impact in the mind. And of course, 1:30 in the afternoon tends to be a dull time anyway: after the meal, physical energy hasn't quite picked up as it does later in the day. So I get to offer reflections during what is, for most people, the most difficult hour! 

I was a little hesitant when I heard we were doing reflections at 1:30 in the afternoon, thinking, "Oh gosh, that’s really a painful time for a lot of people." But when you reflect upon it in terms of practice, it may actually be one of the best times. 

In the Buddha's teachings on the Seven Factors of Enlightenment (*bojjhaṅga*), it is pointed out that when the mind is at a low point in energy—when there is dullness, tiredness, and a lack of clarity or alertness—the best thing to do is engage in active contemplation or reflection, rather than trying to force the mind to be still, concentrated, or calm. The mind is already in a low-energy cycle. That is often not the best time to try to develop tranquility (*passaddhi*), peacefulness, and concentration (*samādhi*). It is a time to bring in the energizing aspects of practice: focusing on the investigation of dhammas (*dhammavicaya*), bringing up energy (*viriya*), and cultivating spiritual joy (*pīti*) that arises from active reflection. If we can stave off the drowsiness to attune, listen, and reflect, it is a very skillful way to utilize this low-energy period.

I was thinking a little about what Ajahn Pasanno spoke about this morning in terms of right attitude in practice, and the idea of not being so driven by trying to be "successful" with a meditation technique. Instead, we can look toward the qualitative aspects of our experience and the contemplation of the mind. He referred to Ajahn Chah’s simple advice: basically *knowing and letting go*, rather than developing expertise in some specific mechanical technique. 

So I thought I would dovetail on that theme and offer some reflections around how we define success in practice—what a successful outcome actually is. 

We can get caught in a confusing array of questions: *What do I do with my meditation time? How do I spend this time fruitfully? Where do I choose to put my mind and effort? Different people say different things about what is important; whose advice do I follow? How can I figure this out for myself?*

This brings to mind the famous discourse from the scriptures where the Buddha visited a group of people known as the Kālāmas (*AN 3.65*). They were a reflective, spiritually interested community, but they had been visited by a succession of teachers of various sects. Each teacher would espouse their specific doctrine, declaring: "This alone is true, and everything else is false!" Then another teacher would arrive and proclaim an entirely contradictory view with equal certainty. 

The Kālāmas were confused: "How are we to know who is telling the truth and who is speaking falsely?" 

The Buddha is often misquoted on this point in modern times, with people asserting that the Buddha simply said: "Just do what feels right. Examine it for yourself, and if it works for you, accept it; if it doesn't, discard it." That is a significant misunderstanding of his advice. 

The Buddha certainly emphasized that they needed to examine teachings within their own direct experience. That was essential. But what is often omitted from the popular retelling is that he also gave them clear, objective benchmarks to weigh things against. It is not simply a matter of "what feels good," because following what feels good is precisely how we got into this cycle of suffering in the first place!

He offered a series of clear benchmarks. He asked them to look at actions and observe their long-term fruits, framing the inquiry around the basic moral precepts:
*"When greed, hatred, and delusion arise in a person, does it lead to their welfare or their harm?"*
The Kālāmas replied: *"To their harm, venerable sir."*
*"When a person kills living beings, takes what is not given, commits sexual misconduct, speaks falsely, or indulges in intoxicants—does that lead to long-term welfare or suffering?"*
They agreed: *"It brings harm to oneself and to others."*

He offered moral conduct (*sīla*) and its observable consequences as an unmistakable benchmark. He also directed them to observe the consensus of the wise—those individuals who are virtuous, well-regarded, and display wholesome qualities in the world. And he taught them the cultivation of the Four Brahmavihāras: boundless loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, and equanimity. 

He was showing them: yes, you must see for yourself, but you calibrate your personal experience against ethical integrity and long-term consequences.

That advice has been the hallmark of the guidance I have received over the years, particularly from Ajahn Pasanno. When I faced difficulties or struggles and asked for advice, I rarely received rigid commands like "do this, do that." There were suggestions and guidance, but the overarching instruction was always: *Examine it. Try it. See what works and what doesn't, and adjust as needed based on your own experience.* 

Everyone is different. We come to practice from different backgrounds, carrying different conditioning and different dominant hindrances. We have to examine what the current flavor or color of the mind is, and learn how to be with that skillfully.

I remember, years before becoming a monk, when I was practicing as a layperson, receiving rigid instruction regarding the "proper" way to practice *ānāpānasati*. I got terribly stuck for years because it simply wasn't working for me. I was doggedly trying to force the mind to watch the breath strictly at the tip of the nose and nowhere else. That was presented as the only valid way. Being loyal to that instruction, I persisted for years, developing immense frustration and tightness. The mind was forcing itself into a box that was far too narrow for my disposition at the time. 

It was only when I moved to the monastery as an anagārika and received advice to drop that rigid technique for a while that things began to loosen up. I explored different contemplations—such as broad bodily awareness—and began to see for myself what actually fostered peace. We need guidance, spiritual friendship (*kalyāṇamittatā*), and people to bounce things off of; but ultimately, understanding must arise from within our own reflective experience.

There is a helpful framework taught by the Buddha known as the Four Bases of Success or Power (*Iddhipāda*). While often discussed in the scriptures in relation to psychic potency, the Forest Tradition frequently applies them as practical guidelines for meditation:

1. **Chanda**: Wholesome inspiration, interest, or desire for practice. For most of us, this motivation arises from directly observing *dukkha*—seeing that our habitual ways of seeking happiness in worldly conditions have failed to bring lasting contentment. That dissatisfaction sparks a genuine search for a better way.
2. **Viriya**: Energy and persistence. Once interest is sparked, energy follows to sustain the investigation and persevere through difficulty.
3. **Citta**: Application of the heart-mind. Taking that interest and energy and wholeheartedly dedicating attention to the task at hand.
4. **Vīmaṁsā**: Discerning evaluation and review. Testing things out: *Is this working? Is it leading to wholesome qualities, or is it creating agitation?* This is where we make intelligent adjustments.

The crucial inquiry that Ajahn Pasanno touched on this morning is: *How do we define success anyway?* 

Is success the mastering of a technique so that we become the "best breath-watcher" in the room? Is it reaching the first jhāna in twenty minutes, moving to the second jhāna within an hour, and checking off meditative attainments like trophies? 

If that is our benchmark, we have fallen into a trap. We are so habituated to seeking pleasant sensations and avoiding unpleasant sensations that we judge our practice through that same commercial lens. It is so easy to think: *"My mind is dull, or full of restlessness, or lacking inspiration—therefore I am failing, and my meditation is terrible."* Because it feels unpleasant, we assume it must be wrong. But when we do that, we are simply caught in the worldly winds of craving pleasure and resisting pain.

We have to reframe what we define as a successful outcome. 

I remember back when I was in nursing school. During our early clinical training, there was an implicit definition of success: patients recovering, curing illnesses, and returning to the optimal state of a healthy twenty-five-year-old body. That was the medical model we absorbed. 

It was not until my final year, during clinical rotations in chronic illness, geriatrics, and palliative care, that this paradigm shattered. Doctors and nurses who were brilliant in acute medicine experienced acute distress when patients became terminally ill and treatments stopped working. Their benchmark of curing the patient was thwarted, and their level of discomfort rose dramatically. 

Our nursing instructors gave us profound advice: *"You have to change your definition of success. If you enter terminal care expecting a cure, you will be disillusioned, you will burn out, and you will withdraw emotionally from the patient. You must redefine success as comfort, care, presence, and acceptance."*

That advice applies directly to our meditation practice. If we enter meditation expecting that our mind should immediately become completely alert, peaceful, and permanently free of hindrances, we set ourselves up for chronic disappointment. We have to change our benchmark from an idealized performance to qualitative presence.

As Ajahn Chah taught, the core of the practice is simply *knowing and letting go*. It is the willingness to experience things just as they are. 

We cannot change the past causes and conditions that have ripened into our present experience. The sensations in the body and the moods arising in the mind—whether desire, irritation, dullness, or restlessness—are conditioned phenomena ripening in the present moment. Our primary duty is simply to *know the beast*—to recognize present-moment reality without immediately reacting against it or trying to force it away. 

We have many techniques in our toolkit: breath meditation, loving-kindness, body contemplation, and reflections on gratitude. These tools serve the purpose of opening the mind, creating spaciousness, and establishing receptivity so that we can see clearly. They are not ends in themselves.

When the mind settles, our investigation becomes very simple: examining experience through the three universal characteristics (*tilakkhaṇa*)—impermanence (*anicca*), unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*), and not-self (*anattā*). 

We begin to observe all phenomena, pleasant or painful, arising and passing away. We see that taking refuge in changing mental states is inherently stressful. When we get quiet, we can tune in to the subtle motion of the mind—that slight contraction or tension where the mind attempts to grasp at a pleasant state or push away an unpleasant one. And we see that these phenomena are operating according to conditions, completely devoid of an owner or a permanent self.

That is the ultimate benchmark: Are we seeing clearly? Are we observing the arising and ceasing of mental states without getting entangled in trying to control them? 

Take this reflection, investigate it in your own experience, and see what brings genuine peace.
