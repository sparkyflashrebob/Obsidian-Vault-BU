# Stories, Parables, and Recollections of Ajahn Chah

> *"All teachings are just similes, helping the mind to see the truth. When you see the truth for yourself, you can leave the similes behind."* — **Luang Por Chah**

---

## 🌾 The Teaching Style of Luang Por Chah (1918–1992)
Ajahn Chah was renowned for his direct, humorous, earthy, and uncompromising pedagogical style. Rather than relying on dense scholarly metaphysics or Pali scriptural analysis, he used everyday objects—broken drinking glasses, rocks, water buffalos, crooked forest trees, monkeys, and sweet mangoes—to point directly to the three marks of existence: **Anicca** (impermanence), **Dukkha** (unsatisfactoriness), and **Anattā** (not-self).

---

## 🪨 1. Classic Parables & Similes

### 1. The Glass Is Already Broken (*Anicca / Non-Attachment*)
> *"You see this goblet? For me, this glass is already broken. I enjoy it; I drink out of it. It holds my water admirably, sometimes even reflecting the sun in beautiful patterns. If I should tap it, it has a lovely ring to it. But when I put this glass on the shelf and the wind knocks it over, or my elbow brushes it off the table and it falls to the ground and shatters, I say, 'Of course.'*
> 
> *When I understand that the glass is already broken, every moment with it is precious. You don't have to wait for things to break to understand impermanence. When you see the end at the beginning, your heart is free."*

### 2. Is That Boulder Heavy? (*Upādāna / Letting Go*)
> While walking along a jungle trail with a group of monks, Ajahn Chah pointed to a massive boulder beside the path.
> 
> *"Is that boulder heavy?"* he asked.
> *"Yes, Luang Por, it's very heavy!"* replied the monks.
> 
> Ajahn Chah smiled and replied:
> *"Not if you don't pick it up."*

### 3. Still, Flowing Water (*Samatha & Vipassanā Together*)
> *"Have you ever seen still water? Have you ever seen flowing water? But have you ever seen **still, flowing water**?*
> 
> *Right there, where thinking ceases and wisdom arises. If you have only stillness, you get stuck in quietude and become attached to peace. If you have only flowing, the mind runs wild after thoughts and sense objects. But when the mind is both still and flowing, it is motionless within, yet fully aware of everything that moves and changes around it."*

### 4. The Crooked Tree in the Forest (*Acceptance & Equanimity*)
> Walking through the forest at Wat Pah Pong, Ajahn Chah pointed toward the canopy:
> 
> *"Look at that tree over there. It is crooked and bent. Look at that other tree; it is tall and straight. Another one is short and thick. Do the trees argue with each other? Does the crooked tree complain to the straight one? Does the straight tree look down on the crooked one?*
> 
> *Nature is just as it is. Trees don't suffer over their shape. It is only human beings who look at each other and say, 'You shouldn't be like that, you should be like this.' Understand people the way you understand the forest trees—each growing according to its conditions."*

### 5. The Mad Monkey (*Mind Wandering & Concentration*)
> Ajahn Chah compared the untrained monkey-mind to a wild monkey chained to a post:
> 
> *"The monkey runs to the left, runs to the right, jumps up into a branch, screeches, drops down, and pulls on the chain until it is exhausted. Why? Because it wants to roam free.*
> 
> *Mindfulness (*sati*) is the rope; your meditation object (*Buddho* or the breath) is the post. In the beginning, the mind thrashes wildly against the rope. Do not beat the monkey. Just keep it tethered to the post. Eventually, when it sees it cannot escape, it sits down quietly at the foot of the post."*

### 6. The Sweet Mango and the Sour Mango (*Direct Insight vs. Intellectual Theory*)
> A scholarly visitor once questioned Ajahn Chah extensively about theoretical Abhidhamma categories and asked him to describe the exact state of Nibbāna. Ajahn Chah picked up a ripe mango from a basket and held it out:
> 
> *"If I sit here and describe the flavor, sweetness, and texture of this mango to you for ten hours, will you truly know what it tastes like?*
> *No matter how many books you read about mangoes, you will never know the taste until you take a bite and chew. Practice is taking a bite of the mango."*

### 7. The Snake's Head and the Snake's Tail (*Pleasure and Pain / Duality*)
> *"Pleasure and pain, happiness and suffering, praise and blame—they are like the head and the tail of a venomous snake.*
> 
> *People love the tail (pleasure) because it looks harmless and soft. They try to grab only the tail and run away from the head (pain). But the tail belongs to the same snake! As soon as you grab the tail, the snake whips around and bites you with its fangs. If you want true safety, do not grab either the head or the tail. Let the snake go its own way."*

### 8. Going Against the Flow (*Khanti / Patient Endurance*)
> *"To practice the Dhamma is to row a boat upstream against the current. The current is our habitual greed, hatred, delusion, and comfort-seeking. Floating downstream with your moods is easy and requires no effort, but it sweeps you right toward the waterfall of suffering.*
> 
> *Going against the flow is hard. It requires patience (*khanti*), restraint of the senses, and willingness to endure friction. When you sit with discomfort and do not move, you are rowing against the current."*

### 9. Tying the Water Buffalo to the Stake (*Sense Restraint*)
> *"When a farmer leaves a young water buffalo untied, it wanders into the neighbor's rice paddies and ruins the harvest, causing quarrels and beatings. A wise farmer drives a strong post into the ground and ties the buffalo with a short rope.*
> 
> *Your senses (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body, mind) are the buffalo; external sights and sounds are the neighbor's crops. Mindfulness of the body is the post. Keep the mind tied close so it does not wander into the fields of craving."*

### 10. The Duck That Wants to Be a Chicken (*Contentment / Yathā-bhūta*)
> *"A duck has short legs; a chicken has long legs. A duck swims; a chicken scratches the dirt. If a duck gets upset and tries to stretch its legs to look like a chicken, it will only tear its muscles and break its bones.*
> 
> *If you want a duck to be a chicken and a chicken to be a duck, you will suffer endlessly. When you see that a duck is just a duck, and a chicken is just a chicken, peace is right there."*

### 11. Washing the Spit Off Your Face (*Praise and Blame*)
> *"If someone stands up, spits into the air toward you, and you get furious, scream, and fight them, who put the spit on your face? You grabbed it and smeared it on yourself!*
> 
> *If you simply recognize that words are sound waves floating through the air, they fall to the ground untouched. Nothing can stick to empty awareness."*

### 12. Carrying the Heavy Coconut (*Letting Go of Resentment*)
> *"If you pick up a heavy coconut and carry it in your arms all day, your arms will ache and your back will strain. You complain to everyone, 'Why is this coconut so heavy?'*
> 
> *Why don't you just drop the coconut? The coconut isn't making you suffer; your decision to hold onto it is what hurts."*

## 🌲 2. Monastic Life & Discipleship Stories

### 13. Moving the Heavy Log at Wat Pah Pong
> During the construction of the monastery, Ajahn Chah instructed all monks to carry heavy logs together through the forest. When he noticed some monks straining while looking over their shoulders to see if others were carrying equal weight, he paused the work:
> 
> *"If you carry the log with your hands and keep your mind free, the work is light. But if you carry the log in your head, comparing yourself to others and nursing resentment, even a twig will crush you."*

### 14. Termites in the Kuti & "This I Cannot Endure" (Ajahn Sumedho's Reflection)
> When young Ajahn Sumedho first lived at Wat Pah Pong, he suffered from malaria, sweltering heat, mosquitoes, coarse food, and termites eating the wood of his hut (*kuti*). Feeling furious, he went to complain to Ajahn Chah.
> 
> Ajahn Chah looked at him with profound kindness and said:
> *"If you run away from Wat Pah Pong because of the heat, where will you go? If you run from the mosquitoes, where will you go? You can escape the jungle, but you cannot escape your own mind. Learn to sit with 'This I cannot endure' until you discover the space of awareness that doesn't suffer at all."*

### 15. The Dead Leaf Falling
> Sitting beneath a tree during the dry season, Ajahn Chah pointed to a yellow leaf drifting down from the canopy:
> 
> *"Look at that leaf. When it was young, it was bright green and tender. Then it matured, turned yellow, dried up, and fell to the earth. Do you cry for the leaf?*
> *Your body is just like that leaf. It grows, ripens, ages, and drops. That is simply the way things are."*

### 16. The Blind Turtle in the Great Ocean
> Ajahn Chah frequently recounted the ancient simile of the blind sea turtle that surfaces only once every hundred years in a stormy, vast ocean where a single wooden ring is tossed by the waves.
> 
> *"The chance of that blind turtle sticking its head through the center of that ring is astronomical. Yet that is how rare it is to be born as a human being and encounter the Dhamma. Do not squander this precious opportunity on trivial worries."*

### 17. The Holy Water and the Water Buffalo
> In Thailand, lay villagers often visited the monastery asking monks to sprinkle them with blessed holy water for good luck and protection. Ajahn Chah would smile and ask:
> 
> *"If being sprinkled with water could wash away defilements and bring enlightenment, then the water buffalos who spend all day submerged in the river mud would already be Arahants! True purification comes only from training your own mind, not from magic water."*

### 18. Putting Sumedho on the Spot (Overcoming Performance Anxiety)
> When Ajahn Sumedho was a junior monk learning Thai, Ajahn Chah unexpectedly called upon him in front of large assemblies of monks and lay supporters, commanding him to give spontaneous Dhamma talks in Thai for hours with no preparation.
> 
> Sumedho struggled with intense performance anxiety, fear of failure, and ego defenses. Through this fierce yet compassionate training, Sumedho discovered that when you let go of trying to be an 'expert' or protect an image, the Dhamma speaks naturally through presence without self-conscious contrivance.

### 19. The Five Waters (Simile of the Five Hindrances)
> Ajahn Chah compared the mind to a pool of clear mountain water where you can clearly see stones and fish at the bottom:
> - **Sensual Desire (*Kāmacchanda*)**: Like water mixed with colored dyes—it dazzles the eye but obscures the depths.
> - **Ill-Will / Anger (*Byāpāda*)**: Like boiling water heated by a fire—it bubbles, steams, and scalds anyone who touches it.
> - **Sloth & Torpor (*Thīna-middha*)**: Like stagnant water overgrown with thick green algae and weeds—dark, murky, and immobile.
> - **Restlessness & Worry (*Uddhacca-kukkucca*)**: Like water whipped by a violent windstorm—choppy waves where no reflection can form.
> - **Doubt (*Vicikicchā*)**: Like muddy water stirred up in darkness—confused, murky, and impossible to navigate.
> 
> *"When the water is still, clear, and unagitated by the winds of defilement, you see everything precisely as it is."*

### 20. Mixing the Alms Food into One Pot / Bowl (*Dhutanga & Overcoming Food Attachment*)
> At Wat Pah Pong and Wat Pah Nanachat, monks followed the *pattapiṇḍika* ascetic practice—eating only from their alms bowls once a day. When returning from the morning alms round with sticky rice, spicy fish curries, soup, vegetables, and sweet desserts (like bananas in coconut milk), newly arrived Western monks (including Ajahn Sumedho and Ajahn Brahm) would try to cleverly partition their bowls using plastic bags or banana leaves to keep the sweet treats from touching the pungent curries.
> 
> Seeing this, Ajahn Chah would instruct that all the food offerings be dumped into one large vessel or mashed together in the bowl—curry, coconut dessert, rice, and soup stirred into a single, uniform stew. When the monks looked on with dismay, Ajahn Chah laughed and told them:
> 
> *"Why are you trying to keep them separate? When you swallow the food, it all goes into the exact same stomach pot anyway! Look at your mind right now—look at the craving for the sweet and the aversion to the pungent. We do not eat to entertain our tongues or chase pleasant feelings. We eat simply as medicine to sustain the body for Dhamma practice."*
