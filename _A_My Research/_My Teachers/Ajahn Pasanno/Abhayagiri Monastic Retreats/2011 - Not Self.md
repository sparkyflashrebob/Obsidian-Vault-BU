## 📖 Executive Summary & Core Teachings

The Buddha demonstrated the truth of Not-Self by interrogating each of the Five Aggregates (*pañcakkhandha*): physical form (*rūpa*), feeling (*vedanā*), perception (*saññā*), mental formations (*saṅkhāra*), and consciousness (*viññāṇa*):
* If the body were truly "self," one could command it: *"Let my body never age, never fall ill, and never die."* Because the body decays regardless of our desires, it cannot be considered an autonomous, sovereign self.
* The same holds true for feelings, perceptions, thoughts, and moments of awareness.

Ajahn Pasanno clarifies the crucial term **Nibbidā**: often mistranslated as "disgust" or "revulsion," *nibbidā* genuinely means **disenchantment** or **disillusionment**—the sober waking up from a hypnotic spell. When a child realizes a magic trick was just sleight of hand, they are not filled with hatred; they simply lose interest. True disenchantment naturally matures into dispassion (*virāga*) and liberation (*vimutti*).

## 📜 Full Talk Transcript

**Ajahn Pasanno:**

Last night we chanted the *Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta*—the Buddha’s second sermon, expounding the characteristic of Not-Self. 

In this discourse, the Buddha systematically examines the Five Aggregates: form, feelings, perceptions, mental fabrications, and consciousness. 

He asks: *"Is form permanent or impermanent?"* 
*"Impermanent, venerable sir."* 
*"And is that which is impermanent pleasant or painful?"* 
*"Painful, venerable sir."* 
*"And is it fitting to regard that which is impermanent, painful, and subject to change as: 'This is mine, this I am, this is my self'?"* 
*"No, venerable sir."*

Notice the absolute empirical rigor of this inquiry. If your body were you, you could say: "Stop aching right now! Stop aging!" And it would obey. But it doesn't obey, because it belongs to nature.

When one sees this clearly with right wisdom, there arises *nibbidā*. 

Translators have sometimes rendered *nibbidā* as "disgust," but that carries the wrong psychological flavor. It is not emotional revulsion or aversion. It is disenchantment. It is seeing through the mirage. 

When you discover that what looked like an oasis in the desert is just hot air bending light over dry sand, you don't get angry at the sand. You simply stop running toward it looking for a drink! 

Through disenchantment, passion fades (*virāga*). Through the fading of passion, the mind is liberated (*vimutti*).
