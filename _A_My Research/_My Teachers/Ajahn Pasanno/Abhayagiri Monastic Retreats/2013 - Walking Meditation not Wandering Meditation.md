## 💡 Teachings Overview

### Central Contemplative Theme
**Establishing a Defined Caṅkama Path, Composure, and Mental Pauses at the Turns**.

### Condensed Version of the Teachings
Ajahn Pasanno clarifies the difference between 'walking meditation' and mere 'wandering meditation.' A true caṅkama practice utilizes a defined path of 15 to 25 paces. Physical composure—hands clasped in front, eyes gently downcast six feet ahead—naturally calms the mind. Stopping physically and mentally at each turn allows the practitioner to check presence before stepping back, creating an unshakeable momentum between sitting and walking.

## 📊 Wandering Stroll vs. Formal Caṅkama Meditation

| Dimension | Wandering Stroll (Aimless Movement) | Formal Caṅkama Meditation (Thai Forest Method) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Spatial Field** | Meandering without boundaries, looking at scenery. | Prescribed path (15–25 paces) walked back and forth. |
| **Physical Demeanor** | Arms swinging, looking around, casual posture. | Composed: hands clasped in front, gaze cast 6 feet ahead. |
| **Turning Point** | Fluid, unconscious turning while lost in thought. | Deliberate physical and mental stop to re-establish presence. |
| **Mental Anchor** | Dispersed in daydreaming and sensory stimuli. | Anchored in touch sensations at the soles of the feet. |
| **Spiritual Fruit** | Restlessness (*uddhacca*) increases. | Sturdy samādhi that carries seamlessly into sitting. |

## 📜 Full Talk Transcript

**Ajahn Pasanno:** So we could switch to a period of walking meditation. Just go out and find a flat spot somewhere—about 20 paces is good. It could be 15, could be 25 paces.

Walking meditation means finding a flat spot that is fairly clear of roots, rocks, and obstacles—a walkway, a quiet path, or a level area—and picking a path to walk back and forth. It is walking meditation as opposed to wandering meditation.

When it is on a prescribed path like that, you can establish awareness in the posture and in the movement. The mind is more readily able to settle. Compose the body: clasp the hands in front, and keep the eyes gently downcast, looking about six feet in front of you. When the body is composed, it helps the mind compose itself.

Wherever the end of your designated path is, stop and turn around. It is helpful to stop both physically and mentally: really stop and check, 'Am I present? Am I aware? Am I with the walking meditation?' And then walk back.

It is very similar to sitting meditation: you are taking a simple rhythm and allowing that to be the basis for cultivating mindfulness. Developing that continuity of awareness—from sitting to walking, and from walking back to sitting—builds a momentum of attention that is extraordinarily fruitful.
