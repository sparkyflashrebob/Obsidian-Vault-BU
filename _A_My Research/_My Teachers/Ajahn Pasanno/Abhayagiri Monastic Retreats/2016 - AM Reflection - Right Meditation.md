* **Location & Setting:**

  * *Modern Venue:* Angela Center, Santa Rosa, California. Saturday morning of Day 9—the final full day of the retreat before tomorrow's closing transition.
* **Circumstances & Context:**
  * On the final full day, the mind naturally becomes vulnerable to restless forward-planning: wondering what awaiting emails exist, dreading traffic, anticipating conversations, or feeling anxious about maintaining meditative stillness at home.
  * Ajahn Pasanno grounds retreatants in the true nature of *Right Meditation* (*Sammā-Samādhi*). Practice is not a temporary hypnotic bubble that bursts when you leave the hall; it is learning to track suffering to its root and abiding in the cessation of dukkha.
* **People Involved:**
  * *Lead Teacher:* Ajahn Pasanno.
  * *Retreatants:* Meditators standing on the threshold between retreat stillness and the return to householder life.
* **Theme of the Dharma Talk:**
  * Demystifying Right Meditation (*Sammā-Samādhi*). Seeing that genuine samādhi has the cessation of suffering (*nirodha*) as its object. Using the forest parable of cutting the poison oak vine to show how wisdom strikes at the root of craving rather than hacking endlessly at surface symptoms.
* **Condensed Version of the Teachings:**
  * On the final full day, the mind instinctively wants to lean forward into planning or backward into regret. Right Meditation brings it back to the center. In the *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN 117), the Buddha explains that noble Right Concentration is not an isolated trance technique; it is a unified stillness supported by Right View, Right Intention, Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood, Right Effort, and Right Mindfulness.
  * The Buddha is the Supreme Physician: diagnosing the disease of *Dukkha*, identifying its root pathogen in craving (*Samudaya*), confirming the cure of cessation (*Nirodha*), and prescribing the medicine of the Path (*Magga*). When clearing poison oak at the monastery, a foolish person hacks furiously at the leaves and gets covered in toxic rashes; a wise woodsman tracks the vine down to where it emerges from the soil and cuts the single root. In meditation, we track our suffering down to the root of craving (*taṇhā*) and cut it with clear seeing.
* **Expanded Version of the Teachings:**
  * Worldly people imagine that meditation is an escape from life—a quiet cave where one hides from unpleasant facts. But the Buddha’s *Sammā-Samādhi* is the exact opposite: it is an unshakeable, luminous clarity that can walk into any assembly or situation with quiet confidence.
  * When we build structures at Abhayagiri—like the new reception hall and library—we ensure the foundation is poured properly so the building does not collapse under stress. In meditation, Right Concentration provides the solid foundation that allows the mind to contemplate *dukkha* without flinching. When we stop trimming surface branches and cleanly sever the root of grasping (*upādāna*), the entire choking vine of suffering collapses.

## 📖 Stories, Parables, and Similes Offered

### 1. The Forest Monks and the Poison Oak Vine
* **The Narrative:** In the rugged chaparral and redwood forests of Abhayagiri Monastery, monks regularly clear firebreaks and maintain forest paths. Invasive poison oak vines often take root at the base of trees and crawl thirty or forty feet high into the forest canopy, strangling the branches and dropping toxic oils onto the trails.
* A novice woodsman who doesn't know what he is doing attacks the vine from above: he grabs a machete, hacks at the leaves, gets exhausted, gets covered in severe blistering rashes, and breaks his tools. And two weeks later, the vine is growing back more vigorously than ever!
* An experienced forest monk does not waste his energy fighting the branches. He puts on gloves, drops to his hands and knees, peers into the brush, and asks: *"Where is this vine coming out of the ground?"* He follows the stem down to the single root emerging from the soil, takes a pair of hand pruners, makes one clean, decisive snip at the base, and walks away. Within days, the entire thirty-foot choking vine withers, turns brown, and falls harmlessly from the tree.
* **The Teaching Insight:** This is the heart of Right Meditation. In daily life, we spend decades furiously hacking at surface leaves: arguing with our spouses, complaining about our jobs, fighting our moods, and blaming the government. We get covered in the toxic rash of anger, while our suffering continues to grow! The Buddha tells us: stop hacking at the branches. Drop into mindfulness, trace the suffering down to where it emerges from the soil of the heart—craving (*taṇhā*) and self-clinging (*upādāna*)—and cut it at the root with discernment.

### 2. Building the Abhayagiri Reception Hall
* **The Narrative:** Reflecting on the ongoing construction projects at Abhayagiri: building the new community reception hall, kitchen, and library.
* **The Teaching Insight:** In heavy construction, you cannot rush past the concrete foundation to start painting the walls. If the rebar is missing or the concrete is poor, the entire building will sag, crack, and collapse. In spiritual life, *Sammā-Samādhi* supported by the eightfold path is that reinforced foundation. When the foundation is solid, your peace of mind stands firm through any worldly earthquake.

## 📜 Complete Edited & Formatted Transcript

**Ajahn Pasanno:**

This is the last full day of the retreat. 

Naturally, there are a variety of thoughts and moods moving through people's minds. Some people feel: *"The last day! I've really got to make something happen before time runs out!"* Others feel: *"Oh no, the last day... I have to go back to my complicated life tomorrow."* And for many, the conceptual mind has already started packing bags and planning future schedules. 

It is the habitual nature of the untrained mind to constantly want to lean forward or backward, to project, to plan, and to construct. 

Right Meditation brings us right back to the still center.

When we develop the Buddha's path, we cultivate an unshakeable, quiet confidence. A person grounded in the Dhamma can walk into any assembly of people, into any group or crisis, and feel completely at ease and comfortable. Why? Because they are anchored in qualities that are universally wholesome for all beings.

In the suttas—specifically the *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* (MN 117)—the Buddha gives a profound definition of *Ariyo Sammā-Samādhi*: Noble Right Concentration. 

He explains that Right Concentration is not an isolated technique. It is *cittassa ekaggatā*—unification of mind—equipped with, surrounded by, and supported by the other seven factors: Right View, Right Intention, Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood, Right Effort, and Right Mindfulness. 

Furthermore, true *Sammā-Samādhi* is not an altered hypnotic trance or a zoning out. Its defining characteristic is that it has *nirodha*—the cessation of suffering—as its ultimate object. In your meditation, whatever method you use, you are paying attention to the cessation of stress.

One of the great ancient epithets of the Buddha is that he is the *Supreme Physician* (*Tikicchaka*). 

He relates to our human condition with the direct, empirical clarity of a great doctor:
1. First, he examines the symptom and declares the illness: **Dukkha** (stress, friction, disease).
2. Second, he isolates the underlying pathogen: **Samudaya** (the fever of craving, grasping, and demanding that conditions be otherwise).
3. Third, he delivers the prognosis: **Nirodha** (the complete cessation and recovery from the disease is possible).
4. Fourth, he prescribes the course of treatment: **Magga** (the medicine of the Noble Eightfold Path).

At Abhayagiri, we often clear forest brush and maintain fire safety. Out in the woods, poison oak vines frequently take root at the base of massive trees and crawl thirty or forty feet high into the canopy, choking the tree and dropping toxic oils onto the trails.

If someone doesn't understand the nature of the plant, they attack the branches: they stand underneath with a machete, frantically hacking at the leaves. They get exhausted, they get covered in blistering rashes, they suffer terribly, and the vine just keeps growing!

An experienced woodsman doesn't waste energy fighting the foliage. He puts on gloves, gets down on the ground, peers through the brush, and asks: *"Where is this vine coming out of the soil?"* 

He traces the stem down to the single root in the earth, takes his pruners, makes one clean cut at the base, and walks away. Within days, the entire thirty-foot choking vine withers, turns brown, and falls harmlessly from the tree.

In our spiritual lives, we spend decades furiously hacking at surface leaves. We fight with our emotions, we complain about difficult people, we get angry at our knees, and we argue with our thoughts. We get covered in the toxic rash of aversion and frustration!

The Buddha tells us: stop hacking at the branches. 

Track the vine back to where it emerges from the soil of the heart. Look directly at craving (*taṇhā*)—the demanding, grasping urge of "me and mine." 

When you use the sharp pruners of mindfulness and discernment to cut the root of craving, the entire choking vine of suffering withers and collapses. 

That is Right Meditation. That is the path to freedom.
