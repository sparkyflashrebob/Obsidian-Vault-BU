## 📖 Executive Teachings: Condensed & Expanded

### Condensed Version of the Teachings
While secular culture often equates Thanksgiving with culinary indulgence and commercial shopping, the contemplative tradition views gratitude (*kataññutā*) as a supreme spiritual blessing (*Maṅgala Sutta*, *Sn 2.4*). 

Ajahn Pasanno contrasts the worldly hunger of "never having enough"—which fuels chronic dissatisfaction and consumerism—with the Buddhist jewel of contentment (*santuṭṭhi*). Contentment is not passive complacency; it is the discerning capacity to appreciate and make skillful use of the conditions we currently enjoy. When we deliberately recollect the generosity of our parents, spiritual teachers, ancestors, and the community supporting this retreat, the heart fills with blameless gladness (*pāmojja*), creating the optimal emotional soil for deep samādhi.

## 📜 Full Talk Transcript

**Ajahn Pasanno:**

Today is Thanksgiving Day, and this is our Thanksgiving retreat. 

I didn't grow up in the United States, so Thanksgiving was not part of my childhood upbringing in Canada. But over the years living here, I have come to appreciate the spirit of Thanksgiving as a wonderful secular holiday—one that can be deeply enriched by Buddhist contemplation. 

In the *Maṅgala Sutta* (*Sn 2.4*), the Buddha lists thirty-eight supreme blessings that bring auspiciousness to human life. Among them, he places *santuṭṭhi* (contentment) and *kataññutā* (gratitude). 

In the *Aṅguttara Nikāya*, the Buddha states that two kinds of people are extremely rare in this world: 
The first is the person who initiates a kind action without any prior obligation—the one who helps first (*pubbakārī*). 
The second is the person who recognizes the goodness that has been done for them and responds with genuine gratitude and reciprocation (*kataññū-katavedī*).

The worldly mind is chronically infected with the disease of scarcity: looking at life and seeing only what is broken, what is missing, what is irritating. We look at our bodies and complain about an ache; we look at our meditation and complain about a hindrance. 

Gratitude is a conscious reorientation of attention (*yoniso manasikāra*). 

We reflect upon the enormous chain of kindness that allows us to sit here this morning: 
* The generosity of the Angela Center staff;
* The lay supporters who provided our food;
* Our parents who gave us physical life;
* Our teachers, Ajahn Chah, and the unbroken lineage of practitioners who preserved the Dhamma across twenty-six centuries;
* And our own healthy bodies and sane minds.

When we bring these blessings into conscious awareness, the mind softens. The tightness of dissatisfaction gives way to a spacious, grounded well-being. 

Let us practice today in the genuine spirit of Thanksgiving.
