## 📖 Executive Summary & Core Teachings

When practicing breath meditation, if the mind begins sinking into sluggishness or drowsiness (*thīna-middha*), simply watching the gentle rise and fall of the breath becomes "soporific"—acting like a sleeping pill. 

Ajahn Pasanno explains that meditators must actively lift the mind (*paggaṇhāti*). Rather than passively submitting to dullness, one introduces active contemplation: changing to walking meditation, reflecting on death (*maraṇassati*), expanding the visual field of light (*āloka-saññā*), or investigating physical posture. The five hindrances are not moral stains; they are specific energetic imbalances requiring intelligent calibration.

## 📜 Full Talk Transcript

**Ajahn Pasanno:**

Beginning the third day of our retreat, a theme that is always essential for our meditation is working with the Five Hindrances (*pañca-nīvaraṇa*). 

The hindrances are sensual craving, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, and skeptical doubt. 

A very common occurrence on retreat is that around Day Three, physical lethargy and sluggishness set in. People sit down, try to watch the in-and-out breath, and within five minutes their head is bobbing and their chin is resting on their chest! The breath has become soporific. 

When the mind is low on energy, passive breath-watching simply puts you to sleep. 

The Buddha taught that in low-energy states, you have to actively lift the mind up (*paggaṇhāti*). You bring in an active reflection: you contemplate the 32 parts of the body, you reflect on the fragility of life, you do walking meditation, or you stand up. 

Do not allow the mind to wallow in dullness. Recognize the hindrance, apply the skillful antidote, and restore the natural alertness of the heart.
