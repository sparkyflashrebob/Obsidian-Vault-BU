## 🏛️ Structured Contextual Metadata

* **Location & Setting:**
  * *Canonical Setting:* Echoing discourses on sickness and intense pain delivered by the Buddha and senior disciples, such as the *Pātāla Sutta* (SN 36.4), where the Buddha redefines the mythic "bottomless abyss" of ancient cosmology as untamed painful feeling, and the *Anuruddha Sutta* (SN 52.10), where monastics visit Venerable Anuruddha when he is plagued with severe bodily illness.
  * *Modern Venue:* Angela Center meditation hall, Santa Rosa, California. Morning of Day 4, where the physical rigors of long hours of silent sitting meditation begin generating significant bodily aches, knee pain, back spasms, and mental resistance among retreatants.
* **Circumstances & Context:**
  * Having received a written request from retreatants asking for explicit guidance on practicing with acute physical pain and somatic distress.
  * Ajahn Pasanno shares deeply from his own personal monastic history in Thailand—where he was hospitalized repeatedly for serious illnesses, surgeries, and broken bones—offering practical, non-heroic tools for deconstructing physical agony without falling into emotional despair or seeking distraction in sensual escape.
* **People Involved:**
  * *Lead Teacher:* Ajahn Pasanno.
  * *Retreatants:* Practitioners encountering somatic thresholds, knee/back pain, and the habitual fear reactions that accompany intense sensation.
* **Theme of the Dharma Talk:**
  * Finding a reliable refuge amidst intense physical pain. Deconstructing painful feeling (*dukkhā vedanā*) through mindfulness, somatic loving-kindness (*mettā*), and the cultivation of internal wholesome ease (*sukha*), thereby preventing the mind from plunging into the "bottomless chasm" of aversion and despair.
* **Condensed Version of the Teachings:**
  * Ordinary people speak of the deep ocean or a volcanic pit as a "bottomless chasm" (*pātāla*). But the Buddha taught that in the Noble One's discipline, painful physical feeling (*dukkhā vedanā*) is the true bottomless chasm when met without mindfulness. An untrained mind immediately constructs grief, fear, and self-pity (falling into the abyss), and knowing no other alternative, desperately grasps at sensual pleasures as a numbing escape.
  * The antidote is the training of Venerable Anuruddha: anchoring awareness in the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (*Satipaṭṭhāna*). Pain is merely the First Arrow—a natural biological impact. The Second Arrow—our mental hatred, storytelling, and resistance—is entirely avoidable. By using the breath to pervade the body with wholesome ease (*pīti/sukha*) and wrapping somatic tension in real loving-kindness (*mettā*), we learn to hold pain without drowning in it.
* **Expanded Version of the Teachings:**
  * In the *Sallatha Sutta* (SN 36.6), the Buddha notes that nobody wakes up and asks: *"How many arrows can I get shot with today?"* Pain is inherently unpleasant. Yet when uninstructed persons feel pain, their immediate reaction is to weep, tense up, and wonder *"Why me?"* Because their only reference point for relief is sensory indulgence (*kāmasukha*), they feed addictive desires. 
  * Spiritual practice provides an entirely different pathway: we cultivate internal resources through Ānāpānasati (the second tetrad, breathing in experiencing joy and calming mental fabrications). We do not use meditation to magically vaporize pain, but to develop the spaciousness to hold it. When loving-kindness (*mettā*) is brought down from an intellectual thought into the visceral knots of the stomach, chest, and joints, the somatic defensive contraction softens, neutralizing the second arrow of dukkha.

## 📊 Core Doctrinal Principles & Practice Frameworks

### Two Responses to Painful Feeling (*Vedanā*): Worldly Abyss vs. Noble Refuge

| Contemplative Dimension | The Untrained Reaction (*The Bottomless Chasm*) | The Noble Disciple's Cultivation (*Satipaṭṭhāna Refuge*) | Sutta Reference |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **First Impact (*First Arrow*)** | Bodily pain, illness, burning, aching sensation | Bodily sensation recognized simply as *rūpa* and *dukkhā vedanā* | SN 36.6 (*Sallatha*) |
| **Mental Reaction (*Second Arrow*)** | Sorrow, grieving, lamentation, beating the breast, panic | Complete absence of mental grief; recognizing sensation as not-self (*anattā*) | SN 36.6 |
| **Underlying Tendency (*Anusaya*)** | Underlying tendency to resistance/ill-will (*paṭighānusaya*) is reinforced | Resistance is recognized immediately and abandoned with patience (*khanti*) | SN 36.6 |
| **Coping Strategy** | Desperate pursuit of sensual distraction (*kāmasukha*) or numbing | Cultivation of spiritual ease (*sukha*), joy (*pīti*), and somatic *mettā* | MN 118, SN 36.4 |
| **Abiding Place (*Vihāra*)** | Sinking into victimhood, helplessness, and the bottomless pit | Abiding firmly anchored in the Four Foundations of Mindfulness | SN 52.10 (*Anuruddha*) |

## 📖 Stories, Parables, and Similes Offered

### 1. The Bottomless Chasm (*Pātāla Sutta*, SN 36.4)
* **The Narrative:** In ancient folklore, sailors spoke with dread of the *Pātāla*—a mythical, bottomless oceanic trench where ships were sucked into perpetual darkness.
* **The Teaching Insight:** The Buddha clarified that the real bottomless chasm in human life is uninstructed painful feeling. When physical agony or sickness strikes and we meet it with aversion, anxiety, and blame, our mind enters free fall. We lose our footing, sink into emotional despair, and grab blindly at anything to stop the fall—usually sensory addictions that plunge us even deeper into suffering.

### 2. Venerable Anuruddha in the Sick Ward (SN 52.10)
* **The Narrative:** Venerable Anuruddha was lying in the sick ward, afflicted with intense, agonizing bodily pains that racked his physical frame. Several senior monastics visited him and marveled at his calm, bright demeanor, asking: *"Where does the Venerable One's mind abide that such painful feelings do not obsess your mind?"*
* **The Teaching Insight:** Anuruddha answered: *"My mind dwells firmly established in the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (*Satipaṭṭhāna*). Because my mind rests in mindfulness of the body, feelings, mind, and dhammas, acute bodily pain does not invade my heart."* Mindfulness creates an impenetrable container within which physical pain can occur without becoming emotional suffering.

### 3. Ajahn Pasanno’s Years of Illness in Thailand
* **The Narrative:** During his decades living as a young Western monk in the northeast of Thailand, Ajahn Pasanno developed a monastic reputation for being perpetually afflicted by physical illness, tropical diseases, repeated hospitalizations, surgical interventions, and wearing plaster casts.
* **The Teaching Insight:** When Ajahn Pasanno and Ajahn Amaro joined together to establish Abhayagiri Monastery in Northern California, physical pain was not a theoretical philosophy—it was a constant companion. He discovered firsthand that trying to fight pain with willpower only knots the belly and exhausts the nervous system. The breakthrough came from learning how to soothe the body through the breath, pervade painful areas with gentle spatial awareness, and cultivate genuine somatic kindness.

## 📜 Complete Edited & Formatted Transcript

**Ajahn Pasanno:**

As we begin our fourth day of the retreat, it feels as though people are settling in well. 

I received a specific request to speak about practicing with physical pain. That offers a very valuable opportunity to reflect on physical pain as an inevitable part of contemplative practice.

In the suttas, the Buddha lays out very clear frameworks for understanding feeling (*vedanā*). He notes that pleasant feeling is to be understood as impermanent; neutral feeling is to be understood as conditioned and changing; and painful feeling is to be understood as an arrow (*salla*). 

Nobody wanders around looking for how many arrows they can get shot with today! That is not how practice works. We do not seek out pain for its own sake. 

However, because we have a human body, physical impingement and bodily pain are simply unavoidable. In the *Sallatha Sutta* (SN 36.6), the Buddha explains that when the uninstructed person experiences physical pain, they immediately add a second arrow: they grieve, sorrow, lament, beat their breast, and panic. They shoot themselves with mental suffering (*dukkha*). 

The Buddha offered the striking image of the "bottomless chasm" (*pātāla*). 

Ordinary people say that the deep ocean is a bottomless chasm. But the Buddha said that in the Noble One's discipline, the true bottomless chasm is unmindful painful feeling. When physical pain strikes, the untrained mind feels helpless, overwhelmed, and enters free fall. 

Furthermore, because the ordinary person knows of no other escape from painful feeling than sensual pleasure, they frantically reach out for sensual stimulation, indulgence, and distraction to numb the pain. That dynamic is the root of so much of our compulsive behavior and addiction.

How, then, do we establish a refuge when intense pain arises on the cushion or in illness?

In the *Anuruddha Sutta* (SN 52.10), Venerable Anuruddha was severely ill, racked with intense bodily pains. Other monastics came to visit him in the sick ward and asked: *"Venerable Sir, how is it that you remain so bright and serene? What is your abiding place (*vihāra*) that these agonizing bodily feelings do not obsess your mind?"*

Anuruddha replied: *"My mind is firmly established in the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (*Satipaṭṭhāna*). When the mind is anchored in mindfulness of the body, feelings, mind, and dhammas, painful bodily feelings cannot overwhelm the heart."*

This is not mere theory. For those who have known me over the years, you know that during my time in Thailand I had a reputation for always being ill—always going to hospitals, having surgeries, and wearing plaster casts. When Ajahn Amaro and I set out to establish Abhayagiri in California, working with severe physical pain was a daily necessity. 

I learned that you cannot fight pain through brute force. If you try to grit your teeth and dominate pain with willpower, your stomach knots up, your breathing becomes constricted, and you burn out your vital energy.

Instead, we learn to cultivate wholesome ease (*sukha*) and joy (*pīti*). 

In the *Ānāpānasati Sutta*, the Buddha moves from the body into the second tetrad: training oneself to breathe in experiencing joy, breathing in experiencing pleasure, and tranquilizing the mental formations. This is a deliberate training. We use the rhythm of the breath to soften the areas surrounding the pain. We relax the shoulders, open the chest, and learn to pervade the whole body with a subtle sense of ease. 

We are not trying to pretend the pain isn't there; rather, we are expanding our awareness so that the pain is no longer the only thing in the room.

Along with this comes the somatic cultivation of loving-kindness (*mettā*). 

Mettā must go beyond merely reciting mental wishes in the head. Bring it down into the physical body! When our knee aches, or our back burns, or our stomach is tight and knotted up, what is our immediate reaction? It is aversion, resistance, and irritation. 

Somatic mettā means holding those aching, screaming sensations with the same tenderness, gentleness, and non-violence that you would offer to a frightened, crying child. You do not push it away; you allow it to be there without adding fear or blame.

When we practice this way, we discover that pain is just physical sensation—heat, pressure, pulsing, and vibration. It is simply conditionality at work. When we do not add the second arrow of mental resistance, we step back from the edge of the bottomless chasm. 

We discover an unshakeable refuge right in the midst of physical affliction.
