## 🧭 Structured Contextual Metadata

* **Speaker**: Ajahn Asoko (Venerable monastic disciple in the Thai Forest Tradition of Ajahn Chah).

* **Host / Invitations**: Ajahn Amaro (Abbot of Amaravati Buddhist Monastery) invited Ajahn Asoko to address the assembly upon returning to the UK.

* **Theme**: Cultivating selflessness; traveling with Luang Por Sumedho at age 88; moving from sensory agitation to inner stillness; investigating personal preferences and willfulness; the Thai Forest practice of *Siyasala* (renunciation / sacrifice / *cāga*); serving the community as a doorway out of suffering.

* **Circumstances**: Delivered to the monastic and lay community at Amaravati Buddhist Monastery shortly after Ajahn Asoko returned from an extensive international teaching tour accompanying the venerable Luang Por Sumedho.

* **Condensed Version of the Teachings**: Returning to the tranquility of Amaravati after intense international travel, Ajahn Asoko reflects on the remarkable endurance of Luang Por Sumedho at 88 years old. He contrasts the outer whirlwind of airports, schedules, and public engagements with the unchanging sanctuary of inner silence. In monastic life as in lay practice, the mind constantly agitates around preferences: wanting solitary retreat time, wanting a preferred room or forest hut (*kuṭi*), or seeking ease. Ajahn Asoko urges practitioners to question whether indulging likes and dislikes actually generates lasting happiness. Drawing upon his decade at Wat Pa Rattanawan living alongside the revered Luang Por Ganha, he highlights the practice of *siyasala* (Pāli *cāga*—sacrifice, self-abandonment). Volunteering for humble communal tasks (cleaning bathrooms, chopping vegetables, washing dishes) without personal preference goes directly 'against the stream' (*paṭisota*), systematically dissolving ego and revealing the joyful freedom of selflessness.

* **Expanded Version of the Teachings**: The illusion of self thrives on the endless defense of preferences. When our plans are disrupted or we are asked to help with communal burdens, an immediate somatic contraction often surfaces: our stomach is tight or knotted up, our shoulders tense, and an internal grievance forms: *'Why do I have to do this? This isn't what I wanted.'* Ajahn Asoko reminds us that this resistance is simply an impersonal survival habit. In nature, a deer runs from a predator, shakes off the shock, and resumes grazing. Humans, however, rehearse the threat endlessly: *we think about the tiger when there is no tiger.* By deliberately stepping forward to serve—offering physical energy for the welfare of others without demanding recognition—we untie the knots of self-cherishing. Sacrifice is not grim martyrdom; it is the joyful shedding of a heavy burden.

## 🌿 Contemplative Reflection & Everyday Integration

In bringing Ajahn Asoko's reflections on selflessness into everyday life:
1. **Going Against the Grain in Small Things**: When a chore needs doing at home or in the workplace—taking out the trash, doing the dishes, answering an inconvenient request—notice the instant hesitation: *'Why me?'* Notice how our stomach is tight or knotted up. We can take a breath, let the belly soften, and step forward freely, using the chore as a vehicle to dismantle ego.

2. **Investigating the Habit of Preference**: Throughout the day, catch the mind demanding that conditions conform to its wishes (temperature, food, internet speed, other people's behavior). Remind ourselves: *'This is just a preference entering the senses. My peace does not depend on having my way.'*

3. **Practicing *Siyasala* (Generous Renunciation)**: Reflect on the paradox that clinging to self makes us miserable, while sacrificing our self-centered demands brings an immediate lightness of heart.
