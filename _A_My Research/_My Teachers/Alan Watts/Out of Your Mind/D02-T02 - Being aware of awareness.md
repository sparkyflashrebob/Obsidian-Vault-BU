### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Watts critiques the cultural rule that an experience isn't real unless recorded, written, or printed in the newspaper—driving people even to seek headlines through crime. Like the sounding box of a violin, human cortical self-awareness gives resonance to happiness; yet knowing that we know quickly spirals into anxiety over whether we calculated every variable. Just as shoppers leave a supermarket carrying real food while mourning the $30 worth of paper they spent, modern consciousness elevates symbolic counters over tangible living wealth.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Examining our modern obsession with documentation, Watts notes how social convention implies you don't truly love someone unless you can compose ecstatic letters like Héloïse and Abélard, and a party didn't quite happen without a tape recorder. Worse still, children crave newspaper validation even at the cost of crime, mistaking echoes for reality. Yet Watts acknowledges the real gift of reflexive awareness: like singing in the bathtub or the hollow body of a cello, our nervous system lets us know we are happy—adding resonance to bare sensation.

The evolutionary catch arises when self-awareness turns against instinct. Recalling the verse about trying to see the 'I' that knows me when I know that I know, Watts traces how distrusting spontaneous intuition in favor of forethought and calculation creates bottomless anxiety—because the variables of life are always incalculable. Using money as his clearest parable, Watts pictures someone pushing a cart full of groceries out of a supermarket while feeling poorer because they handed over $30 of paper receipts. Treating paper symbols as primary wealth while demoting actual nourishment to mere necessity encapsulates how profoundly symbolic consciousness has inverted reality.
