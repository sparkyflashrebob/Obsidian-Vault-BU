### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Before exploring consciousness directly, Watts invites us to examine the unconscious worldviews built into everyday Western language and logic. Two inherited models dominate modern thought: the Biblical 'ceramic' image (God as potter shaping passive clay) and the scientific 'fully automatic' image (blind mechanical matter). Contrasting Western 'making' with Asian 'growing,' Watts shows how organic growth unfolds from inside out rather than being assembled from outside in.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts opens the seminar by noting that its real subject matter—'what there is'—is too fundamental for an ordinary title. Before pointing directly at reality, he excavates the invisible assumptions embedded in Western common sense, grammar, and logic. He uses the word *myth* not to dismiss falsehoods, but to name the foundational root-images through which a culture interprets existence. Today's crisis stems from inheriting two incompatible yet equally alienating myths: the *ceramic model* (rooted in Genesis, where an external technician—potter, carpenter, or architect—breathes life into passive dust) and the *fully automatic model* of mechanistic science.

To expose how deeply the ceramic model shapes our intuition, Watts contrasts what an American child asks (*'Who made me?'* or *'How was I made?'*) with what a Chinese or Hindu child would ask (*'How did I grow?'*). Making proceeds from the outside in, assembling passive parts like a sculptor chipping stone or a potter throwing clay. Growing, by contrast, works from the inside out: a living embryo complicates itself organically all at once. Because the ceramic image separates maker from made and clay from form, we unconsciously grow up feeling like constructed artifacts rather than organic expressions of the cosmos.

## Stories, Analogies & Anecdotes Offered

- **American Child vs. Chinese Child**: Watts contrasts a Western child asking its mother *'How was I made?'* or *'Who made me?'* with a Chinese child asking *'How did I grow?'*, illuminating the cultural gulf between craft-making and organic unfolding.
- **Genesis Dust & Potter's Clay**: Watts unpacks the Genesis image of Yahweh sculpting Adam out of dust and breathing life into inert clay, noting also Jesus as both Son of God and son of a carpenter—linking divinity with artisan technique.
