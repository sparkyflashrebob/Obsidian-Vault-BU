### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Shifting from medieval comfort inside crystalline planetary spheres to modern interdependence requires a perceptual flip like moving beyond 'one, two, three, many' counting. Introducing the Japanese Kegon/Huayan Buddhist concept **jiji muge**—pictured as a dawn spider web studded with dewdrops where each drop reflects every other drop infinitely—Watts bridges mystical vision with everyday linguistics: just as 'bark' changes meaning completely beside 'tree' versus 'dog,' every facial feature, thought, and galaxy exists exactly as it is because everything else—even a wooden roof-beam overhead—holds its place in the total web.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Why does radical interdependence feel frightening when first encountered? Because unlearning old assumptions—just as Renaissance Europeans panicked when astronomers shattered crystal planetary spheres, or as tribes counting 'one, two, three, many' struggle to enumerate four corners of a table—temporarily shakes familiar psychological supports. Yet once absorbed, a vastly richer reality comes into view.

To illustrate cosmic mutuality, Watts invokes Japanese Buddhist philosophy—**jiji muge** (Huayan's unimpeded mutual interpenetration of all phenomena)—imaged vividly as a vast dawn spider web glistening with dewdrops where every drop holds the sparkling reflection of all other drops endlessly. Grounding this poetic vision in simple English grammar, Watts recalls the homonym **bark**: its meaning doesn't sit locked inside four letters, but changes instantaneously depending on whether the sentence mentions a tree or a dog. Exactly the same holds for you sitting in this room: your clothes, neuroses, family history, and very pulse depend smoothly on the distant stars and even the knotty timber beam bracing the Sausalito studio! Touching an isolated finger numb to empty wind vs. waking instantly upon contact demonstrates how awareness itself only lights up in relationship—because *'it takes two.'*
