### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Observing stream of consciousness directly reveals that thoughts pass like waves leaving no trace—until rapid looping makes a moving spark resemble a solid glowing ring (like whirling a lit cigarette in the dark) and inserts a commentator ('thought, thought, thought—*thinker!*'). Echoing Tang master Jōshū's description of childlike mind as 'a ball in a mountain stream' ('walk or sit, never wobble!'), Watts uses logic and cybernetics: criticizing a sentence *while* uttering it creates runaway feedback, smashing your thumb with the hammer instead of driving the nail—the true root of Buddhist *kleśas* (confusions). He traces our belief in a permanent watcher-self to misleading engraving metaphors (John Locke's *tabula rasa*, impressions stamped on wax) inherited from ancient ceramic myths splitting passive clay from imposed shape.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Exploring why human minds feel stuck when real thoughts come and go effortlessly like ocean waves, Watts exposes two tricks of attention. First, endlessly recycling familiar anxieties blurs discrete sparks into an apparent solid ring—just as spinning a glowing cigarette tip in blackness draws a luminous hoop—giving rise to a phantom watcher ('thought, thought, thought, thinker!') splitting concrete living actions into 'feeling feelings' and 'thinking thoughts.' Second, speaking about speaking *mid-act* overloads the nervous system with excessive feedback: hesitating over a hammer blow makes you strike your thumbnail cleanly instead of the nail, matching Jōshū's counsel on keeping mind flowing like 'a ball in a mountain stream' without wobbling.

This simultaneous self-monitoring breeds what Buddhism terms *kleśas* ('disturbing confusions'). Humans fall uniquely prey to this snag because silent language (words, numbers, images) lets a *second* commenting thought-stream pretend it sits apart as an impartial judge over the first. Reinforcing this illusion are faulty metaphors of memory: pencil marks on paper, wax seals, and John Locke's *tabula rasa* ('blank slate')—all disguising an ancient ceramic superstition that a passive 'mind-stuff' accumulates fixed scratches over time.
