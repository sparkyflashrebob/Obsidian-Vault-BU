### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Turning from philosophy to concrete institutions, Watts sketches a Japanese Zen temple complex: distinct from Western congregation parishes (noting jokingly how Japanese-American temples copied Protestant hymns like *'Buddha loves me, this I know'*), traditional temples blend ancestral memorial services, tourist restaurants, and serious training grounds centered in the *Sōdō* ('Sangha hall'). Inside, monks eat, meditate, and sleep on six-by-three-foot *tatami* mats beneath Bodhisattva Mañjuśrī (wielding *prajñā*'s illusion-cutting sword) under an administrative abbot (*Kanchō*) and formidable spiritual *Rōshi*. Unlike Christian churches eager for converts, gatekeepers turn arrivals away repeatedly—forcing monks wearing mushroom straw hats, white *tabi*, *geta* or *waraji* sandals, and chest-boxes to bow silently over their boxes for days of winter vigilance before earning an audience.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Moving cleanly into lived sociology, Watts demystifies Japanese Zen complexes as seminary campuses ringed by noble ancestral sub-temples. Unlike Western Sunday churches, ordinary Japanese drop in casually for pilgrimages (hundreds flooding Eiheiji's 4 a.m. chanting hall), weddings, and ancestral requiems—whereas Westernized immigrant temples ironically imitated Protestant Sunday schools ('for the *sūtra* tells me so!'). Today many picturesque Kyoto sub-temples supplement thin stipends by hosting gourmet vegetarian kitchens or preserving art.

At a monastery's beating heart stands the *Sōdō* (*Sangha-dō*, communal hall), lined down both sides with raised six-foot-wide *tatami* platforms where each monk owns one mat—sleeping, dining, and meditating beneath central Bodhisattva Mañjuśrī ('Manju') grasping the wisdom-sword of *prajñā*. Though independent Rinzai temples skip popes and bishops, internal order separates practical abbot (*Kanchō*) from dreaded spiritual guide (*Rōshi*). Applying could hardly differ more from Western church hospitality: a candidate arriving in broad mushroom-hat, short black robe, white *tabi* socks, high *geta* clogs, and portable chest-box is briskly turned away as an unwelcome burden—obliged to rest his forehead motionless atop his wooden box all day and sit sleepless at night in the guest lodge until perseverance wins an introductory summons.

**Alan Watts:**

[00:00] Having discussed basic principles of what Zen is about, I'm passing on to the more practical side of it. A Zen monastery is not a monastery in the Christian sense. It's more like a theological seminary. Except that, it practices more than it teaches. A typical institution consists of a campus, and on the campus there are many buildings. First of all, around the edges, you will invariably find independent temples that were founded in times past by noble families.

[00:48] Because one of the things that the Buddhists did when they came to the Far East was they exploited ancestor worship. This was very clever of them. This being the great religion of China. The Buddhist priests performed services like requiem masses for the repose of the souls or for good reincarnation, reincarnations for one's ancestors, and they made quite a thing out of that. And so they have memorial services for the departed, and that's one of the principal functions of temples in Japan.

[01:34] People don't go to temple in the same way as Westerners go to church. They make pilgrimages to temples, and say at a great temple like Eiheiji, you will find on a Sunday morning, or practically any morning, a swarm of about 500 people attending the 4am service of chanting, chanting the Buddhist scriptures. But they are kind of in and out of their temples.

[02:08] They have special services. They have memorial services, weddings, funerals, or everything like that. But they don't have a parish kind of church community as we find it in the West. Although when Buddhism, through the Japanese immigrants exports itself to the United States, they immediately copy the Protestant church institution and sing, Buddha loves me, this I know for the sūtra tells me so. It's terrible.

[02:40] And all the young Nisei, who have never been in Japan, the one thing they can't stand is sutra chanting, because they don't know what it means, and the priests don't know what it means a lot of the time. But it's beautiful to listen to, and they haven't got an educated Western here yet to appreciate that kind of Oriental music.

[03:08] Well now, aside from these many temples, each of which is in charge of a priest with his family, and some of them are having a hard time making a go of it these days, so they've become restaurants for very elegant food, for museums, and all sorts of things. Now the central, the guts of the Zen temple is what's called the Sōtō.

[03:34] So in Japanese is the Sangha, the order of followers of the Buddha, though simply means hall. So the Sangha hall or Sōtō is the center. And this consists of a number of rooms, but the main one, the actual Sōtō itself, is a large, long, spacious room with platforms on either side and a wide passage down the center.

[04:06] The platforms are six feet wide, and each contains a number of tatami mats, which are measure six by three. And every monk is assigned to a mat, and on a shelf behind the mat against the wall, he has all his possessions, which are very simple. And so the mat is his sleeping place and his meditation place. There is an image of the Bodhisattva Mañjuśrī in the hall, more or less in the center of the passage between the platforms.

[04:48] Mañjuśrī is a Bodhisattva, they call him Manju in Japan, who holds in his hand a sword. And this sword is the sword of wisdom or prajñā, which cuts us under all illusions. That is the dwelling place and the meditation place of the monks. And then they have of course kitchens and library, and they have special temples that the monks use for various services.

[05:23] Then aside from that, there are the quarters of the Kanchō, who is the abbot or administrative head of the temple, and then the quarters of the Roshi, who is the spiritual teacher. There isn't in the Zen, not in the Rinzai Zen school at any rate, exactly a hierarchy. Every temple is independent. There is no Pope, no Archbishop, but there is a fraternal relationship between all the temples of the Rinzai sect.

[05:55] The Sōtō sect have a little bit of a hierarchy, but still on the whole, the Kanchō or administrative head of the temple is the big boss. The Roshi is the respected boss the man everybody is terrified of, at least on the outside at any rate. Now if you want to get into one of these institutions and study, they make it difficult.

[06:21] It's so different from the welcome attitude you get when you go into a Christian church. Here they repel you. Westerners of course are treated with a certain amount of courtesy that is not ordinarily accorded to Japanese. But even then it's made difficult because they realize that a Westerner who's taken the trouble to learn Japanese and to get himself over the oceans and to live under unfamiliar conditions is certainly pretty serious about it. And there are a number of Western Zen monks.

[06:55] So funny, there's one at A.H.G., who comes from San Francisco, and he's tremendously tall, and to see him with all the others is quite amusing. Anyway, the formal approach is that you arrive in your traveling gear at the gate, and the Zen monk's traveling gear is most of picturesque. He wears a great mushroom on his head, an enormous straw hat about so wide, and then he has a black robe rather shorter than a kimono, and he has long white tabi socks underneath, and geta, which are the wooden sandals with bridges on them to keep you high up a bit.

[07:41] Or he may wear just plain Waraji, which are straw sandals. Then he carries on the front his little box in which are his eating bowls, his razor, his toothbrush, and such necessities of life. Well, when he arrives, he's told that the monastery is very poor, and they can't afford to take on any more students, and that the teacher is getting old, and it might tax his strength, and things like that. So he has to sit on the steps, and he puts his traveling box in front of him.

[08:21] He takes off his big hat, and he lays his head on the box, his forehead, and waits there all day. [08:45] In olden times, this went on for at least a week or ten days to test this fellow out. Then finally, the assistant to the Roshi comes and tells him that the Roshi maybe would have a talk with him.
