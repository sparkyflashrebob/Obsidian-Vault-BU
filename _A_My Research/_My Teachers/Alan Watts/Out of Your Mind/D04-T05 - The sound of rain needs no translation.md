### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Synthesizing *The Web of Life* across three images—selective game-grid, transactional web, and spider-trap of selfishness—Watts shows how pushing disillusionment all the way through turns Shakespeare's bleak *Macbeth* lament upside down (as R. H. Blyth noted, once called 'sound and fury signifying nothing,' it doesn't seem half bad!). Sharing a Japanese Zen master's rebuke to an aspiring Buddhist novelist ('Don't sugar the pill—write to the Great Sky!') and his praise for Lewis Carroll's nonsense alongside any scripture, Watts delivers the luminous line: *'The sound of the rain needs no translation.'* Ultimately, existence weaves endlessly intricate patterns—molecules, kaleidoscopes, clouds, gold arabesques inlaid on medieval armor—simply because, as the Pennsylvania Dutch say, *'it's for nice.'*

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Summing up the triple metaphor of the web—selective filter turning survival into strained deadly seriousness, mutual transaction like buying and selling, and the spider's hungry trap—Watts pivots suddenly into liberation via **R. H. Blyth's** sly remark on *Macbeth*: if existence truly is 'a tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing,' then when put that candidly, it actually feels strangely exhilarating and pressure-free!

Illustrating how Zen rejects solemn moralizing, Watts shares two memorable exchanges with Japanese masters. First, a master scolded a friend trying to smuggle Buddhism into commercial fiction ('sugar the pill'): *'Write it to the Great Sky!'*—for the finest literary masters are masters of pure nonsense, using *Alice in Wonderland* (*'Twas brillig, and the slithy toves...'*) as readily as a sutra. Second, watching scholars labor over English Zen translations, a Japanese teacher laughed that it was pointless if one misses direct seeing: any book—Bible, dictionary, or nonsense verse—suffices, because **'the sound of the rain needs no translation... Evening rain, it is the banana leaf that speaks of it first.'** Everything comes alive as effortless, fascinating **pattern**: turning supper into skeleton and nerves, peering into kaleidoscopes, or commissioning an armorer to etch intricate gold arabesques on steel simply because, in Pennsylvania Dutch phrasing, *'it's for nice.'*
