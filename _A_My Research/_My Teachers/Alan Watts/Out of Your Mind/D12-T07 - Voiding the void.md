### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Clarifying that Nāgārjuna's *śūnyatā* targets conceptual cages around God or *Brahman*—never world-hatred—Watts likens Buddhist dialectic at ancient Nālandā University to modern experimental 'T-groups' ('blab-labs') stripped of cozy answers. Patiently drawing out each student's secret compass—religious certainties, ethical prejudices, hidden props—the serene teacher knocks every support away until fright explodes into *Nirvāṇa* ('phew!'). Crucially, Nāgārjuna pushes one step further: never make an idol of emptiness either (*void the void*), because 'you cannot nail a peg into the sky.' Contrasting colorful Western hymnody ('wisdom from above... saints and angels') with Zen's man 'with no tile overhead and no inch of ground below,' Watts proves falling endlessly without crashing is genuine groundless peace.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Demystifying Nāgārjuna's rigorous dialectics, Watts clarifies what *śūnyatā* ('emptiness') actually empties: every rigid human concept pretending to scoop infinite water inside a conceptual sieve. At great medieval Indian academies like Nālandā, masters sharpened Gautama's own conversational method into sustained collective inquiry resembling modern psychological T-groups ('blab-labs')—with one vital twist: the happily grounded teacher offers zero replacement creeds.

Instead, questioning uncovers every student's hidden emotional anchor—dogmas, moral certainties, philosophies of the 'good life'—and dismantles them thoroughly until terror turns inside out into vast breathless relief (*Nirvāṇa*, colloquially *'Phew!'*). Yet even here lurks one final temptation: worshiping 'The Void' as a bleak cosmic idol. Nāgārjuna therefore demands *voiding the void* (*śūnyatā-śūnyatā*): as Zen texts put it, you cannot pound a nail into blue sky, nor crash onto concrete when nothing holds you overhead. Contrasting comforting Victorian hymns praising 'a truth unchanged, unchanging' with the Chinese poem about having 'above not a tile to cover the head, below not an inch of ground on which to stand,' Watts displays the clean exhilaration of dropping all metaphysical insurance.
