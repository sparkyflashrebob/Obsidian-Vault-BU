### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Surveying India's classical life blueprint before modern schooling created restless career confusion, Watts walks through *Brahmacarya* (apprenticeship) into *Gṛhastha* (householder life), balanced around *Artha* (political/economic citizenship, mapped in the *Arthaśāstra*) and *Kāma* (aesthetic and sensual mastery taught unashamedly in the *Kāma Sūtra* so youths learn tender art rather than behaving 'like mere baboons'). Surrounding both sits *Dharma*—primarily meaning *method* or rightness rather than grim moralistic law (so Buddha-Dharma means Buddha's practical method). Together, citizens uphold the world game (*lokasaṅgraha*) via personal vocation (*svadharma*) until handing household leadership to adult children and heading out to the forest as a *Vānaprastha*.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Looking sympathetically at hereditary apprenticeship before mass schools prompted adolescents to agonize 'what should I be?', Watts sketches India's classical *āśrama* arc. After youthful *Brahmacarya* (apprenticeship/studentship), a citizen enters *Gṛhastha* (householder life) resting upon two dignified pillars: *Artha* (civic duty, statesmanship, and commerce codified in the *Arthaśāstra*) and *Kāma* (aesthetic refinement in cooking, dress, poetry, and erotic love—celebrated frankly in the *Kāma Sūtra*, which Watts recommends to every teenager entering puberty so love becomes an art rather than crude fumbling).

Governing this civic theater stands *Dharma*—not heavy Western 'righteousness,' Watts clarifies, but practical *method*, game rules, and personal vocation (*svadharma*), through which every worker joins *lokasaṅgraha* ('upholding the world game'). Crucially, Indian culture built an honorable graduation door: once children reach maturity and inherit the household reins, parents step cleanly out of town duties into *Vānaprastha* ('forest-dweller')—closing the evolutionary circle from primitive hunter back into nature with seasoned maturity.
