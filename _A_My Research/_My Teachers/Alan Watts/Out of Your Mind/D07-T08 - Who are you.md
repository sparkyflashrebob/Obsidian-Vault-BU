### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Dismantling inner war between 'higher' and 'lower' selves—memorably seeded when his mother scolded 'Alan, that's not like you!'—Watts warns that wrestling the ego treats a mirage as solid. Similarly, schemes like Moral Re-Armament collapse because vague ideals ('absolute honesty, absolute love') cannot guide subtle concrete choices without splitting minds into paralysis. Drawing on a Daoist maxim—'When the wrong man uses the right means, the right means work wrongly'—Watts illustrates sudden non-deliberative breakthrough through Winthrop Sargeant laughing spontaneously before a Kyoto priest, a Zen master releasing an army officer's trapped 'goose in the bottle' by shouting his name at the door, and D. T. Suzuki sitting quietly for sixty serene seconds at Columbia before simply answering, 'Yes.'

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Continuing his assault on inner civil war, Watts exposes how childhood rebukes—*'Alan, that's not like you!'*—cleave human beings into warring factions ('better moments' vs. naughty shadow). Yet fighting a supposed 'lower self' validates its reality; awakening shows the 'lower self' to be a mirage through which one walks effortlessly without grabbing a stick to beat thin air. Nor do movements like Moral Re-Armament help by testing thoughts against 'absolute honesty' and 'absolute love': concrete life dilemmas defy vague abstract slogans.

If you are already split, trying to fix yourself triggers the Daoist paradox: 'When the wrong man uses the right means, the right means work in the wrong way.' Real integration strikes unexpectedly from outside deliberate plotting—as when journalist Winthrop Sargeant kept giving intellectual definitions of himself to a Kyoto priest until a sudden joke triggered a spontaneous laugh ('There you are!'), or when a master solved an officer's riddle of a full-grown goose trapped in a glass bottle by abruptly calling him at the doorway ('There, it's out!'). Crucial nuance: spontaneous Buddha-mind does not mean forced speed—D. T. Suzuki calmly held a Columbia lecture hall in silent poise for a full minute without fidgeting before offering a simple, unembarrassed 'Yes.'
