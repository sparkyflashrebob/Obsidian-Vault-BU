### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Turning to the *Vānaprastha* / *Śramaṇa* (from which the word *shaman* may even echo), Watts shows why finding out *who you really are* requires leaving daily company: every interaction ('student listening to me lecture, me playing teacher') reinforces shared social masks ('Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Sailor'). Shedding social mirrors, the *Vānaprastha* adopts *mauna* (total speech silence for a month or year): silent internal labels dissolve until sunsets blaze with shocking vividness and flowers glow enchanted! Because abandoning mental categories also strips cautious moral discrimination (risking getting swept cheerfully along by a mob!), the seeker relies temporarily on a seasoned *guru* taking responsibility for their *karma*. Stepping outside name, clothing, and respectable society—wearing rags, ashes, or nakedness—makes them 'upper outcastes,' mirroring India's marginalized Indigenous 'lower outcastes' (*Harijans*).

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Exploring the radical step from householder into *Vānaprastha* (*Śramaṇa*), Watts asks why a person who played society's respectable roles cleanly still needs woodland solitude to ask, *'Who am I really?'* Because human identity operates reciprocally: listeners coming to a hall quietly cast Watts as 'teacher' while casting themselves as 'students,' just as colleagues and spouses continually mirror back expected personas. Stepping off stage breaks the mutual trance.

The classic doorway is *mauna*—taking complete vows of silence for months or a year. Past several weeks, not only spoken chatter ceases, even silent inner word-tagging winds down; deprived of dull conceptual filters, sunsets ignite with overwhelming color and ordinary blossoms glow electric. Yet stripped of familiar social caution, a silent sage resembles an unguarded child who could walk straight into a street riot and join the crowd blindly—which explains why traditional novices seek an experienced *guru* to assume stewardship over their *karma*. Discarding family name, fine attire, and neat grooming for yellow rags, ashes, or bare skin places these wanderers cleanly 'beyond the pale' as revered 'upper outcastes'—standing diametrically parallel to India's Indigenous 'lower outcastes' (*Harijans*).
