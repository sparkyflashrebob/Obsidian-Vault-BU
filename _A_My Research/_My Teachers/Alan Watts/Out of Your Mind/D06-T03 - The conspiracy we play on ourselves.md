### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Asked how to regain open childlike vision, Watts answers cleanly: every technique asked by a separate 'I' is part of the hypnosis itself, echoing Jewish, Christian, and Sufi teachings that illumination arrives as unmerited grace. Society installs its rigid value system (*māyā*) like a post-hypnotic suggestion ordering the subject to forget having been hypnotized. Yet blaming parents or past trauma misses the deeper insight that *time flows backward*—just as the final word of 'I love you' or 'I hate you' alters every word before it, the living present alone gives the past its authority. Saying realization takes dozens of difficult lifetimes simply announces, 'I prefer not to look right now.'

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts tackles the inevitable practical question: if the adult ego misses the child's open sky, how do we recapture it? The catch is that 'how do I get it?' preserves the exact illusion of an isolated agent in charge of enlightenment. Both trying and aggressively trying *not* to try tighten the knot. Hence Western mystics speak clumsily of 'the gift of God'—meaning insight can never be won by egoic effort. Worse, our value hierarchy (*māyā*) functions like a stage hypnotist's post-hypnotic command: we were trained not merely to obey cultural rules as useful conventions, but to believe they are absolute Reality.

Instead of encouraging endless psychoanalytic blame toward guilty parents, Watts flips time's arrow around: 'the past flows back from the present.' Just as forgiveness heals old wounds, or a later note alters a melody's opening chord, or the verb at the end of a German sentence—or 'love' versus 'hate' after 'I'—transforms what preceded it, only our *present* allegiance keeps inherited hypnosis alive. Claiming awakening requires arduous decades or lifetimes acts like telling a reader a book is impossibly hard: it dampens curiosity and politely postpones seeing right now.
