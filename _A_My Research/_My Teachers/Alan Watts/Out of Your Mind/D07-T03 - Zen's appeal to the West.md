### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Explaining Zen's magnetizing promise of instant insight—contrasted with years on a psychoanalyst's couch or endless reincarnations in solemn asceticism—Watts cites Saburō Hasegawa: 'It may take three seconds, or thirty years.' After clarifying that Zen trainees resemble theological students free to marry or take parish temples rather than lifelong Catholic monks, he retells three classic *mondō* encounters from the *Mumonkan* (*The Gateless Gate*): Jōshū telling a seeker after breakfast, 'Go wash your bowl' ('don't gild the lily'); the riddle of the cow's horns, body, and four legs passing through a corral while its tail gets stuck; and Master Baizhang appointing the monastery cook as abbot after the cook kicks over a water pitcher without asserting or denying what it is.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Western seekers raised on slow therapeutic journeys or multi-lifetime spiritual ladders find Zen startling because it holds open the door to waking up *this very second*—as painter-philosopher Saburō Hasegawa quipped, 'three seconds or thirty years.' Before unpacking classic koans (*mondō* Q&A exchanges) from the 13th-century collection *Mumonkan* (*The Gateless Gate*), Watts clarifies a common institutional misunderstanding: Zen 'monks' in Japan are closer to seminary students who may graduate into married temple leadership rather than taking permanent celibate vows.

Walking through three sharp *Mumonkan* gems, Watts likens Zen dialogues to jokes: explaining a punchline kills its electric click. When an earnest student begs Jōshū for higher dharma instruction and admits he has eaten breakfast, Jōshū snaps, 'Then go wash your bowl'—calling out the student who probably already rinsed his bowl and was still craving extra spiritual polish ('putting legs on a snake or a beard on a eunuch'). Next comes the cow clearing an entire enclosure except for her little tail—symbolizing the tiny sliver of self-consciousness that keeps snagging free flow. Finally, Master Baizhang tests students to name a water pitcher without affirming or denying anything; while a stiff scholar tries verbal gymnastics ('you can't call it a piece of wood'), the monastery cook marches forward, kicks the pitcher over, and walks out—winning the abbey (though the wry commentator jokes he traded a cushy kitchen post for an abbot's headache!).
