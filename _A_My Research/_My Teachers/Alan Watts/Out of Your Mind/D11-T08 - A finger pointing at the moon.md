### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Warning against making mental idols of 'God' or 'Ātman'—since whatever encompasses everything lacks an outside boundary or shape—Watts introduces Zen's timeless proverb: Buddhist teaching is a finger pointing at the moon, yet most believers cling to sucking the finger for comfort. Recalling how Gautama 'chopped off the finger' through his majestic 'thunderous silence' toward metaphysical riddles, Watts explains why traditions must grow like living oaks dropping fresh acorns rather than worshiping one original seed. Humorous warnings against slogging through sprawling *Prajñāpāramitā* volumes climax with Zen monasteries' lively annual accordion-book 'reading' (*tendoku*) and Chuang-tzu's quip: a good dog isn't measured by barking, nor a wise person by endless talking.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Continuing his critique of frozen belief, Watts points out why neither Hebraic iconoclasm nor Advaita concepts suffice once mental pictures replace direct reality: claiming anything *specific* about the all-encompassing whole makes no sense without an external edge to define its shape. True Buddhism undermines conceptual idolatry at every turn, remembering Zen's sharpest warning: doctrines merely point a finger at the moon, whereas fearful believers turn that finger into a pacifier and refuse to look upward. Just as Gautama met metaphysical yes/no traps with the resounding 'noble silence' (later called 'thunderous silence'), real teachers smash comforting beliefs to crack the eggshell so living perception can hatch.

Addressing modern Buddhist revivalists eager to crawl backward to 'original primitive Buddhism'—much like fundamentalists claiming to recover pure first-century Christianity unmediated by culture—Watts compares living tradition to a flourishing oak scattering new acorns rather than stuffing leaves back inside an old nut. Advising students gently not to drown inside vast monsoon-season *sūtra* libraries (beyond readable gems like the *Dhammapada*, *Diamond Sūtra*, and *Laṅkāvatāra*), Watts closes joyfully with D. T. Suzuki's photograph of Zen monks fanning accordion-folded volumes like slinkies once a year—honoring sacred books respectfully while remembering words stay secondary to wakeful life.
