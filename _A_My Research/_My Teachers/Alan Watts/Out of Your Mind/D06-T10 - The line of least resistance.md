### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Closing Session 6, Watts compares Christ's birth in a 'pigsty' (obscure stable) and a Japanese friend's quip—Christ was the carpenter's son while Buddha was a prince's—to underline how the great secret hides exactly where solemn people refuse to look: in sheer nothingness. Far from draining energy or love of gardens and children, seeing you are this creative zero unleashes vibrant energy precisely because nothing stands in the way. Tracing apophatic wisdom across traditions—the sixth-century Syrian monk writing as Dionysius the Areopagite (*Theologia Mystica*, echoed in *The Cloud of Unknowing* and accepted even by Aquinas), Nāgārjuna's *Śūnyatā*, and Śaṅkara's insight that 'fire does not burn itself'—Watts urges taking the line of least resistance without inventing an imaginary Father-figure at the end: 'No holds. Look Mama, no hands!'

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Bringing *The Inevitable Ecstasy* to its apex, Watts reminds listeners why sacred wisdom deliberately hides in unprestigious places—just as the Christ child appears not in a throne room but in an animal stall (prompting his Japanese friend's joke contrasting the carpenter's son and Prince Siddhārtha). To worried moralists who protest that resting in sheer nothingness must destroy compassion, gardening, and human joy, Watts answers from direct paradox: plain static 'somethingness' cannot create; *only* empty openness unleashes unobstructed energy and creative glee.

He then demonstrates remarkable cross-cultural unanimity around *agnosia* (sacred unknowing). Neither eternal torpor nor restless speed, the ultimate exceeds every mental image—precisely as taught in the 14th-century English *Cloud of Unknowing* and its 6th-century Syrian source, *Theologia Mystica* attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite (which Watts himself translated in 1943). Even St. Thomas Aquinas endorsed Dionysius' sweeping via negativa, agreeing that anyone claiming to behold a defined vision of God sees only a lesser creature. Standing alongside Nāgārjuna's *Śūnyatā* and Śaṅkara's dictum that the ultimate knower cannot become an object to itself ('fire doesn't burn itself'), Watts concludes simply: follow direct reality along the line of least resistance, stop smuggling comforting projections onto the void, and witness—*'No holds. Look Mama, no hands!'—how splendid it turns out to be.*
