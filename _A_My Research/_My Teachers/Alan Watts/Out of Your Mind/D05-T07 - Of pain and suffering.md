### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Applying the sense of non-dual happening to bodily pain, Watts argues that framing suffering as 'me' fighting 'a pain' automatically fuels agony through frantic thrashing—just as trying to outrun a migraine carries the headache along in the running. Much of pain's terror lies in learned social taboos and prior dread. Recalling R. H. Blyth's witty reply about vegetarianism—'plants have feelings, yes, but they don't scream so loudly'—Watts points out how hospitals and modern manners suppress screaming and natural bodily purging like childhood vomiting for institutional convenience, driving raw pain inward where resistance magnifies it.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Having anchored listeners in the unitary sensation of happening, Watts turns directly to the central problem of Buddhism: pain and suffering. Our ordinary language sets up an immediate battlefield—splitting experience into a helpless victim on one side and an invading 'pain' on the other. Much of pain's destructive force arises precisely from this inner struggle: like someone sprinting to escape a migraine, the panic carries the throb right along inside every frantic stride.

Watts highlights how social conditioning compounds physical discomfort with anticipatory dread and moral embarrassment. Quoting his Zen scholar friend R. H. Blyth's dry joke about why vegetarians spare screaming animals yet harvest quiet cabbages, Watts observes how deeply human institutions dislike raw audible distress. Hospitals, nurseries, and polite society run for the peace of staff and bystanders—tabooing screams, groans, and even wholesome physical releases such as vomiting up bad food. Taught from childhood that natural bodily releases are 'disgusting' and noisy agony is improper, we clench inward against localized sensation, converting straightforward physical signals into chronic interiorized misery.
