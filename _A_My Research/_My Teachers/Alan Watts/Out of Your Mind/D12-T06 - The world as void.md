### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Dismantling the myth of the ego as a static filing cabinet storing past memories, Watts presents memory itself as reverberating present-moment brain echoes—where millisecond retina flashes never register unless remembered slightly afterward, and crossed timing signals produce *déjà vu*. Because past memories and current sights unfold inside the same flowing life-process without a side-standing observer, letting go yields dizzying freedom—illustrated startlingly by a surviving kamikaze pilot who felt ecstatic liberation at the instant of expected annihilation. Transitioning historically to 100 BCE–200 CE Mahāyāna Buddhism, Watts introduces the *Prajñāpāramitā* literature, the chanted *Hṛdaya* (*Heart Sūtra*: 'Form is emptiness, emptiness is form'), and Nāgārjuna's incisive *Mādhyamaka* / *Śūnyavāda* philosophy.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Probing deeper into why 'the separate thinker' dissolves upon inspection, Watts dismantles Victorian psychology's picture of memory as an archival safe-deposit box guarded by an ego clerk. Perception itself requires vibrational lingering: a millionth-of-a-second flash passes unregistered unless retinal rods and neural echoes reverberate long enough to 'make an impression.' Even distinguishing yesterday's recollection from today's visitor depends purely on distinct present-time timing cues—which occasionally misfire as *déjà vu*! Since past cues and present sensations ripple within one indivisible process, no neutral librarian stands apart reading files.

Illustrating what happens when someone truly accepts self-vanishing—recalling a WWII Japanese kamikaze pilot whose missed dive triggered not patriotic rage, but godlike exhilaration once total non-existence felt inevitable—Watts bridges cleanly into classic Indian Mahāyāna philosophy. Emerging prominently between 100 BCE and 200 CE across the *Prajñāpāramitā* ('Wisdom Gone Beyond') scriptures and distilled daily in the chanted *Hṛdaya* (*Heart Sūtra*), Nāgārjuna's *Mādhyamaka* ('Middle Way')—often misunderstood by Christian missionaries as grim nihilism (*Śūnyavāda*)—actually declares *śūnyatā* (voidness/relational openness) and luminous sensory *form* to be completely inseparable.
