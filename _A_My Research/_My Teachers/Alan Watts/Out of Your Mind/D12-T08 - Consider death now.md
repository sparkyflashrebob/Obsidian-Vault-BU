### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Challenging dull blank-mind caricatures of emptiness—showing what empty actually feels like by asking what your own head looks like to your eyes—Watts cites Huìnéng's rebuke of sensory deprivation, comparing over-spaced yogis to nitrogen-raptured divers handing masks to fish or astronauts needing auto-return timers. True lightness comes not from blanking senses, as D. T. Suzuki quipped ('everyday experience, two inches off the ground') and Chuang-tzu taught ('walk without touching the ground'), but from dropping the Zen koan's phantom 'Who drags this corpse around?' Exposing how doubling language ('think my thoughts,' 'feel feelings,' 'sightseeing') sets up neurotic feedback howl like holding a phone mouthpiece to its receiver or pointing a TV camera at its own monitor, Watts shows how Nāgārjuna's honesty toward inevitable death silences chronic self-defense.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

To banish misconceptions that *śūnyatā* equals blankness inside a sensory deprivation chamber or drifting white fog, Watts offers a witty phenomenological demonstration: ask yourself how the inside of your head looks to your own eyes right now! It looks like the bright room in front of you—meaning empty awareness is transparent openness rather than black nothingness. Hence Sixth Patriarch Huìnéng sharply scolded practitioners plugging eyes and ears: artificial trance mimics nitrogen narcosis in deep scuba divers so blissed-out they hand regulators to passing fish!

Instead of numbing senses, Zen aims at what Suzuki called living 'two inches off the ground,' echoing Chuang-tzu walking cleanly without dragging dead weight. Why do our bodies feel like heavy burdens? Because our syntax hallucinates an extra homunculus asking 'Who carries this corpse around?' Doubling verbs with nouns—'feeling feelings,' 'hearing sounds,' 'thinker thinking thoughts'—mirrors holding an old telephone handset directly against its receiver or aiming a studio camera into its monitor: screeching feedback oscillation, known psychologically as trembling anxiety ('worried because I worry'). Facing death straightforwardly right *now* snaps that feedback loop, freeing vital energy currently squandered defending a phantom.
