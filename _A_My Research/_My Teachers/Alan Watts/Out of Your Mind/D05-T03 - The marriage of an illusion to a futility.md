### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Distinguishing the real living organism (a concrete feature of the cosmos) from the abstract 'ego' (like an inch or line of longitude), Watts dissects what people actually feel when they point inside to 'I.' Two illusory halves fuse together: an incomplete social caricature of oneself built from mirrors and gossip, and a chronic muscular strain (*saṅkoca*) around the eyes, jaw, breath, and belly—trying futilely to force spontaneous nerves by clenching muscles, like pulling on an airplane seatbelt to lift a jet off the runway.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts draws a sharp boundary between the living organism—a real focal process inseparable from its ecosystem—and the 'ego,' which is an abstract social convention of the same logical order as an hour, a pound, or the equator. Mistakes arise when we reify that convention as a tangible interior commander. On inspection, the ego consists first of an intellectual and emotional *image* of ourselves—wildly incomplete (omitting nervous reflexes, circulation, and cosmic interdependence) and distorted by secondhand mirrors and social feedback.

Yet people insist they *feel* a sensitive center inside the skin corresponding to 'I.' Because the head and back stay out of direct view while eyes look outward, we project an invisible watcher behind the eyes. What we actually feel there, Watts diagnoses, is *saṅkoca* (contraction): chronic habitual muscular tension acquired while trying to obey commands like 'pay attention,' 'concentrate,' or 'pull yourself together.' Squeezing eyelids to see better or tightening the diaphragm and pelvis to hold emotions in is as futile as tugging an airplane seatbelt during takeoff. Thus the ego stands exposed as 'the marriage of an illusion to a futility': an imaginary portrait bound to pointless physical straining.
