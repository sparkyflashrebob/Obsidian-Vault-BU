### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Rescuing the words 'play' and 'game' from Western scorn—where a flirt complains 'you're only playing with me!'—Watts separates *seriousness* (solemnly braced against lurking tragedy, characteristic of soldiers, courts, and somber churches) from *sincerity* (wholehearted delight, like Jascha Heifetz 'playing' a violin classic). The Supreme Self neither worries nor matters in utilitarian profit terms: like good-vs-evil squares on a chessboard, higher mathematicians hunting pure elegance, kids tossing pebbles into rippled ponds, knitter rhythms, and roaring cosmic jazz, every form from bees to giraffes exists solely to see 'how far out this play of rhythm can go.'

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

To counter listeners offended by hearing life called 'play,' Watts carefully distinguishes triviality from art. Yes, in romance 'you're only playing!' signals fickle trifling—yet when Jascha Heifetz 'plays' violin, nobody calls it trivial. The real divider lies between *seriousness* and *sincerity*: people wear stony serious faces at parades, law courts, and solemn services because anxiety whispers *things might turn tragic!* Asking whether God is serious brings an immediate laugh—since infinite Self transcends threat, its weaving of black and white chess pieces (good and terrible alike) is non-serious *līlā* played with complete sincerity.

Consider what humans gravitate toward the moment urgent chores stop: tossing pebbles for concentric ripples, pure mathematics chasing useless crystalline elegance, practicing instruments, shaking dice cups, dealing cards, knitting, breathing, and singing together. Every organism performs its signature dance—the flower dance, the bee dance, the giraffe dance, the human sonata or fugue—meaning existence isn't racing toward some grim pragmatic finish line, but jamming like cosmic jazz simply to stretch its wings and enjoy how wondrously rhythmic it can play.
