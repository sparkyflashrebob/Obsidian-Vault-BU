### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Turning to Krishnamurti's warning that every effort toward liberation merely boosts the ego, and Gurdjieff's discipline of constant 'self-remembering,' Watts asks where hyper-vigilant self-watching actually leads. Recounting Japanese Zen swordsmanship training—where a student taught to stay constantly alert ends up hopelessly stiff *thinking about* being alert until he learns relaxed whole-body readiness like water poised inside a barrel—Watts concludes that yoga and concentration simply demonstrate *how not to use the mind*. Like falling asleep or digesting food, awakening cannot be forced by willpower; demanding exercises serve mainly as an *upāya* trick for people who lack the faith to jump into the water on their own.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Reviewing Krishnamurti and Gurdjieff side by side, Watts exposes the paradox hidden inside intense spiritual exertion. Krishnamurti tells seekers outright that no effort can produce freedom, since any project aimed at liberation strengthens the very ego seeking enlargement; yet even trying to 'be aware without judgment' recreates the exact same bind. Similarly, Gurdjieffian 'self-remembering'—forcing oneself to notice picking up a piece of paper—turns ordinary awareness into anxious self-surveillance.

To explain why, Watts retells the classic Japanese Zen swordsmanship lesson: a swordsman constantly scanning for attack grows taut, rigid, and easily defeated because thinking *about* alertness slows down spontaneous response. True readiness resembles a relaxed cat or water sitting quietly in a barrel—ready to spurt instantly from whichever hole opens, committed to no preconceived direction. Accordingly, disciplined concentration teaches mostly by exhaustion: *how not to use the mind*. Like sleep or digestion, awakening happens by letting go rather than straining, and strenuous spiritual diving-lessons exist simply to bring hesitant seekers to the edge of the board until someone nudges them into trusting the water.
