### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Opening with a classic *Punch* magazine gag—a soldier complaining 'It hurts every time I shake my leg!' and the doctor snapping 'Don't shake it!'—Watts examines our strange attraction toward poking loose tooth sockets, high-speed thrills, and dangerous sensations. Repetitive practices (*Oṃ*, *mudrā*, or Sufis chanting their own name until it turns to pure sound) shatter verbal meaning to reveal raw vibrational life, 'like a kaleidoscope jazzing.' Yet guardians of convention warn of nihilism, and acute depression distorts that emptiness into cold 'patent leather and plastic flowers smelling faintly of soap'—still evaluating mechanically rather than seeing what simply *is*.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts investigates why humans repeatedly flirt with painful or risky vibrations—from children poking an empty dental socket with their tongue to adults chasing speed and extreme sport. Far from pure perversity, curiosity sharpens focus on the sheer *flavor* of sensation. Contemplative traditions deliberately harness steady repetition—chanting *Oṃ*, cycling *mudrās*, or Sufis repeating their own names endlessly—until familiar labels collapse into sheer nameless energy, leaving only 'the whole world playing at doing all this, like a kaleidoscope jazzing.'

When conventional guardians hear talk of cosmic play lacking moral solemnity, they panic over Shakespeare's 'tale told by an idiot.' Watts distinguishes genuine luminous mysticism from clinical depression's bleak caricature: when depressed, people feel the world turned mechanical—automated strangers, enameled tin, jukeboxes, and synthetic plastic flowers reeking dimly of soap instead of spring petals. Even that bleak vision still imposes a bitter value contrast ('mechanical versus organic'), mistaking numbness for insight rather than stepping clear of both labels.
