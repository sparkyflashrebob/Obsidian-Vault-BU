### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Drawing a razor-sharp distinction between *explicit* conflict and *implicit* harmony, Watts unmasks adversarial politics and Cold War arms races as mutually dependent performances where sworn enemies keep each other in business. Sharing a personal memory of walking beside Lake Zürich with C. G. Jung—who noted how swans commence courtship by fighting furiously before melting into lovemaking—Watts links Tweedledum and Tweedledee's agreed battle, Sanskrit *Māyā* (creative measurement/mother/matter), and Ballantine's Ale's three Borromean rings to prove unity constantly sustains every apparent quarrel.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Examining human conflict through the lens of *explicit* versus *implicit* rules, Watts observes that fierce competitors—rival corporations or even Cold War Russia and the United States—denounce each other loudly on the explicit stage while secretly relying on one another's hostility to sustain jobs, identity, and vigilance behind the scenes. Only spectators naïve enough to swallow surface propaganda mistake stage wrestling for pure hatred.

To make this vivid, Watts shares an enchanting afternoon anecdote with psychiatrist **C. G. Jung** at Jung's lakeside retreat on Lake Zürich. Strolling past monogamous swans along the shoreline, Jung remarked how regularly those graceful birds open mating rituals by flapping and fighting fiercely until suddenly the quarrel dissolves seamlessly into lovemaking—illuminating why human tensions often mask hidden longing! Echoing Lewis Carroll's Tweedledum and Tweedledee agreeing beforehand to have a battle, Watts connects the weaving loom (*over-under-over-under* forming cloth, linking Latin *mater* / *matter* with Sanskrit *Māyā*, cosmic measurement and illusion) to **Ballantine's Ale's three interlocked steel rings**: remove any single ring and the remaining two fall apart completely, proving wholeness lives in irreducible triadic or dual interdependence.
