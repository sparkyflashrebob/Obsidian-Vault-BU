### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Exploring how spiritual pride even turns 'tolerance' into a subtle game of being *more* tolerant than others, Watts explains why Gautama saw trap and trapped were identical—leading him to formulate the *dharma* not as static creed, but as a dialectical therapeutic method. Clarifying Pāli versus Sanskrit spellings (*dhamma*/*dharma*, *kamma*/*karma*), Watts unpacks the First Noble Truth (*dukkha*—frustration or chronic dis-ease opposite *sukha*) alongside *anitya* (impermanence) and *anātman* (no enduring owner). Once someone truly sees there is nothing fixed to grasp and no fixed hand doing the grasping, moralistic scolding becomes unnecessary.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Before outlining the Four Noble Truths, Watts skewers spiritual vanity—noting how 'tolerant' inclusivist sects one-up narrow churches simply by bragging *they* are more tolerant! Gautama's breakthrough cut cleanly through all such traps: trying to rescue 'oneself' keeps the ego intact, whereas seeing that trap and trapped are identical dissolves both at once. Accordingly, the Buddha formulated the *Dharma* (Pāli *Dhamma*) not as fixed creedal dogma like Western theology, but as a dialectical dialogue—a living conversation where early 'teachings' act merely as opening provocations.

Employing ancient oral mnemonic numbering for the First Noble Truth, the Buddha diagnoses *dukkha* (bitter/chronic friction, opposing *sukha*'s sweet ease) by pairing it with two structural realities: *anitya* (*anicca*—ceaseless flux throughout physical and spiritual realms) and *anātman* (*anattā*—no substantial 'you' inside holding onto things). Far from issuing grim moral commandments, Buddhism cuts grasping at its root: seeing that clinging to experience is literally an illusory hand clutching smoke makes compulsive struggle dissolve of itself.
