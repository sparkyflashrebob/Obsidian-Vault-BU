### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Framing any talk on Zen as an affectionate 'hoax' trying to do what poetry does—say the unsayable—Watts cites Zen's four classical hallmarks (transmission outside scriptures, no dependence on words, direct pointing to mind, seeing one's nature). Postwar Westerners gravitated toward Zen for three reasons: rare religious humor, freedom from moralizing dogma ('spiritual ophthalmology' rather than preaching), and D. T. Suzuki and R. H. Blyth's focus on earthy Tang-dynasty Chinese Zen rather than ultra-formal sitting regimes. Rejecting exclusive obsession with *zazen* (noting Buddhism honors walking, standing, sitting, and lying), Watts closes with Paul Reps' delightful mistranslation: mishearing a master's broken-English sermon 'Don't act bad act' as a sublime Daoist paradox, 'Don't act, *but* act!' (*wu-wei*).

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts begins *The World as Just So* with poetic humility: talking about Zen shares the artist's impossibility of expressing in words what evades all language—pointing at the moon (`*tat tvam asi*`, `*tathatā*`) rather than worshipping the finger. Reciting the four traditional statements attributed to Ch'an, Watts diagnoses why postwar Americans fell in love with Zen: unlike solemn Western moralizing, Zen overflows with belly laughs; like physics or an oculist checking eyesight ('spiritual ophthalmology'), it adjusts perception rather than scolding virtue.

Crucially, Watts explains the gulf between modern Japanese monastic *zazen* orthodoxy and early Tang-era Chinese Zen (700–1000 CE) popularized in English by D. T. Suzuki, R. H. Blyth, and Watts himself. Recalling R. H. Blyth's gentle irritation when a Japanese master claimed he would spend his last half-hour on earth doing formal *zazen*—ignoring Buddhism's four dignities of walking, standing, sitting, and lying—Watts quotes the classic Zen formula: when hungry, just eat without pondering ten thousand worries; when tired, just sleep without dreaming endless schemes (echoing his school motto *Age dum agis*, 'Act while you act'). He ends with a comedic East-West gem: Paul Reps (*Zen Telegrams*) thrilled over hearing a master advise 'Don't act, but act!'—pure *wu-wei*—not realizing the master's heavy accent had merely sermonized, 'Don't act bad act!'
