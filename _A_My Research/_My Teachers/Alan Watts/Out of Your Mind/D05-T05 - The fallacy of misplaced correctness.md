### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Watts exposes Alfred North Whitehead's 'fallacy of misplaced concreteness'—assuming every verb requires a separate noun-doer, as in saying '*it* is raining.' Whether magnifying matter through microscopes or chasing galaxies with telescopes, scientists find only patterns within patterns, never inert 'stuff' ('stuff is simply patterns seen out of focus'). Turning around to catch the seeker—whether labeled 'matter,' 'mind,' or *Ātman*—yields only the game of hide-and-seek: spiritual gurus assign possible 'Exercise A' goals to keep students chasing impossible 'Exercise B' targets until they stop searching for their own head.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Exploring why we feel a ghost-like thinker must hide behind every thought, Watts attributes the illusion to Indo-European grammar: just as 'it is raining' invents an imaginary 'it' to do the raining, we invent a separate knower behind knowing and a doer behind deeds. Echoing Whitehead's *fallacy of misplaced concreteness*, Watts notes that modern physics dissolved solid 'stuff' into interlocking mathematical patterns; what looks like solid matter is merely high-frequency pattern seen out of focus, like a spinning kapok blur.

In the same way, our word *universe* ('one turn') describes the single turn outward to witness existence, whereas we keep trying to make *two* turns to catch what is looking. Gurus understand this game of hide-and-seek intimately. Clever teachers offer graded hierarchies—doable concentration tasks ('Exercise A') alongside impossible paradoxes ('Exercise B')—like martial-arts belts or Masonic degrees, letting students taste partial mastery so they persist until they finally exhaust themselves. Then comes the Zen realization: you cannot take hold of it and you cannot get rid of it, because you are Buddhas from the beginning, and searching for the True Self (*Ātman*) is like hunting everywhere for a head you never lost.
