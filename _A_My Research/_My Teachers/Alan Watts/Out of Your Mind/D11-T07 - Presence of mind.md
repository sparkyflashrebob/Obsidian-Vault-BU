### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Exploring the Eightfold Path's meditative peaks—*samyak-smṛti* (total present-mindedness) and *samyak-samādhi*—Watts illustrates *smṛti* through southern Buddhist walking practice ('lifting the foot... perception of lifting... consciousness of the perception'), joking that it resembles 'The House That Jack Built.' Practicing that watchfulness exposes a startling insight: ordinary focal attention operates with narrow blinkers, and since remembering or planning still occurs *right now*, absent-mindedness is actually impossible! Comparing *samādhi* to skilled horsemen, fluid drivers, or perfectly attuned dancers, Watts closes by clarifying why Buddhism is not dogmatic 'common-or-garden atheism'—rather than shouting 'there is no God,' Buddhism turns to non-conceptual realization beyond all cosmic idol-making.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Rounding out the Eightfold Path, Watts explores *smṛti* (Pāli *sati*—recollection, present-mindedness, echoing Gurdjieff's 'self-remembering') and *samādhi*. Humorously nicknaming Theravāda walking meditation—noticing lifting the foot, the perception of lifting, the tendency toward perception, and consciousness thereof—'The House That Jack Built,' Watts points out what sincere students quickly discover: spotlight consciousness can track only tiny slivers at once. Even better, observing closely exposes an emancipating secret—since memories of yesterday and plans for tomorrow happen exclusively inside *this present moment*, truly being absent-minded turns out to be impossible.

Such total presence ripens into *samādhi*: neither stiff zombie trance nor empty stupor, but seamless integration like an experienced cowboy unified with his horse, a skilled driver feeling the car as his own limbs, or dancers gliding together without shoving. Reaching that threshold where the separate ego fades, Buddhists avoid both devotional idol-worship and combative atheism—recalling an English Parliament anecdote about bishops, prayer books, and MPs confessing belief in 'some sort of a something somewhere,' Watts distinguishes vague guesswork and negative dogma from non-conceptual realization.
