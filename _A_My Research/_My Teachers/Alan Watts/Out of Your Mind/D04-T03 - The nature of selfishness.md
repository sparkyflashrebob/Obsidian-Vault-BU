### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Watts recommends two shockingly honest practices acting like fertile manure: meditating calmly on death/skeletons, and admitting total unvarnished selfishness rather than hiding behind pious confessions (*'we have erred and strayed like lost sheep'* while secretly expecting applause for trying). Yet pushing selfishness ruthlessly reveals a funny catch: saying *'I love you so much I could eat you'* exposes that we never find an isolated 'me' inside—every craving points straight back outward into the other, proving the self we love is the whole cosmos! Celebrating Jung's **shadow** integration, Watts distinguishes timid 'good' people from genuinely *holy* ('whole') figures like Jesus, Freud, and the stormy-yet-sunny ocean.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Opening Session 4 with disarming candor, Watts prescribes two practices as psychological fertilizer ('like manure fertilizing plants'): meditating unflinchingly on skeletons and never waking up, and testing the hypothesis that you are completely selfish. Polite church confessions—memorized beautifully from Episcopal liturgy (*'there is no health in us... we shall mend with God's grace'*)—often act as sly con games, praising ourselves subtly for admitting sin while still posing as pure souls.

If you dare look squarely at genuine self-love, however, a startling paradox unfolds. Even sensual affection ('I love you so much I could eat you!') rings far truer than pompous 'pure disinterested love'—yet try finding that tiny 'me' supposedly demanding gratification! You can never spot the isolated subject; every pleasure, beauty, or person you desire lives across the mirror as 'other,' proving through total honesty that the self you secretly adore *is* the whole universe. Embracing **C. G. Jung's concept of the Shadow**—making peace with the unacknowledged rascal, scapegoat, and 'fly in the ointment'—transforms merely tame 'good' behavior into wholeness (*holy* = *whole*). Like the ocean, glorious in sunlight yet terrifying in storm—or tumultuous historical catalysts like Jesus and Freud—genuinely integrated spirits reconcile bright and dark extremes within creative vitality.
