### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Anticipating an audience protest—'That sounds fine theoretically, yet I would still panic if told I am dying!'—Watts applies his method directly to the panic itself. Society traps us in a double-bind: conditioning us to frantic survival while shaming us as cowards if panic shows. Rather than burying mortality under polite euphemisms (or dismissing Tibetan thigh-bone trumpets, skull *kapālas*, Baroque bone chapels on the Via Veneto, and monks' desk skulls as 'morbid'), Watts urges letting 'the heebie-jeebies' run freely. Since true courage often coexists with trembling fear, accepting panic without pretending calm brings the very breakthrough dying reveals.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts steps cleanly ahead of skeptical listeners who understand detachment intellectually yet know viscerally they would freeze in panic before a terminal diagnosis. Far from dismissing that terror, Watts flips it into the teacher: because survival panic is involuntary, feeling ashamed that you are terrified ('I ought to be brave!') traps you once again at cross-purposes with yourself. Seeing you cannot forcefully turn off panic teaches the exact same lesson as facing death itself.

Modern society sweeps death offstage as a killjoy 'skeleton at the banquet,' scorning medieval memento mori skulls, Roman Capuchin bone chapels on the Via Veneto, and Tibetan ritual thigh-bone trumpets or silver-and-turquoise skull cups as morbid. When listeners complain that facing finitude gives them 'the heebie-jeebies,' Watts replies warmly: then work with the heebie-jeebies! Our culture suppresses public terror largely out of fear that soldiers will freeze in crisis—forgetting that courageous action frequently happens in the company of trembling fear, and that terror's real taboo touches its hidden ecstatic edge.

## Full Verbatim Transcript

**Alan Watts:**

[00:00] Now, let's get practical. You say, okay, I understand what you are saying theoretically. But I know that I would be terrified if somebody was going to tell me that I'm going to die. And that I would look frantically around for some doctor, some sort of something. That this panic to live is in us in an uncontrollable way.

[00:27] And this is part of the reason why we say we have an instinct to survive. The instinct is this panic. So let's take another step. Now, in the same way as I showed you steps about realizing that you don't have an ego, you say to yourself in the ordinary way when you feel that panic, you feel a bit ashamed of it. Even though you've been taught that you should do everything possible to survive.

[00:59] See what a bind you're in here? So one feels, oh goodness, I must face this thing calmly and bravely and not be in this panic. But the point of the fact is, you are in a panic and you can't stop it. Now, that's very important because this is another way of showing you the same thing that death is showing you that you can't do anything about it. This is when you finally realize you can't do anything about the death.

[01:30] You could have solved all that before by understanding you couldn't do anything about the panic. But if you think all the time I'm supposed to stop this panic, then all that happens is you're across purposes with yourself again. The panic is, of course, put off in the ordinary way. We all know we're going to die. But it's sufficiently far off so that we can put it all of our minds.

[02:03] And anybody who does put it into our minds in the ordinary way is taken to be a skeleton at the banquet, a Cassandra and gloomy so that the old fashioned preacher of bygone days who preached about death and those monks who kept skulls on their desks and all that sort of thing is regarded today as very morbid.

[02:41] Why, in the Baroque times, there was a fashion for a while of making tombstones with marvellous sculptures of skeletons and bones all over. And on the Via Veneto in Rome, there is a Capuchin church where down in the crypt there are chapels made where the altar furnishings and everything are made entirely from the bones of departed monks.

[03:08] Then we have among Tibetans and in Buddhists graveyard meditations and they have trumpets in Tibetan Buddhism made of human thigh bones and they have cups, ritual cups made of the domes of human skulls, richly worked in silver and turquoise. And we say all that is very morbid. So, from this point of view, you can see first of all theoretically how death can solve its own problem.

[03:53] Now, if you say I can only see it theoretically and I can't go the whole way with you, then I will ask you what is blocking you. Will you say it gives me the heebie-jeebies and the horrors? I say, alright, so death is not the problem, the heebie-jeebies is the problem. So, let's deal with the heebie-jeebies in the same way as with death.

[04:15] You cannot stop the heebie-jeebies. You think you should. I say, don't. The heebie-jeebies are very valuable. Not that they will stop you from dying, but because from them you will learn the same thing as you would learn from dying. But the social pressure on you to resist the heebie-jeebies is terrific. Now, why must you do that? Why is everybody saying these heebie-jeebies, these fears, etc., are not permissible?

[04:52] You wonder about that. And the reasoning behind all that is not very clear because it seems to be saying, well, if you have all these fears and things like that, you won't be very good soldier. You won't be able to act competently in a crisis. You'll get the heebie-jeebies instead and you won't know what to do. Well, nobody has ever really proved that because actually people who we would call very courageous are, in fact, often quite frightened.

[05:30] And courageous action is not necessarily a consequence of having no fear. Sometimes it might be, but it isn't always so. The real reason why the heebie-jeebies are suppressed has more to do with its orgiastic aspects.
