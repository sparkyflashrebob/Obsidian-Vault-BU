### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Turning to Zen history, Watts situates Ch'an inside Mahāyāna Buddhism—honoring the Bodhisattva ideal (often pictured playfully as Sengai's fat belly-laughing Hotei/Budai or even a prostitute) over monastic retreat from ordinary life. Tracing Chinese origins back to Kumārajīva's translation circle (c. 414 CE), Daosheng's affirmation of sudden awakening via the *Nirvāṇa Sūtra*, Bodhidharma, Huike, Sōsan (*Xinxin Ming*), and Sixth Patriarch Huineng (Enō, d. 715), Watts unpacks Zen's critique of sitting like a 'stone Buddha.' Stories of Tanka splitting a wooden altar Buddha to warm himself on a freezing night (ironically raking ashes for *śarīra* jewels) and Yunmen answering 'What is Buddha?' with 'A dried turd!' illustrate that true purity means moving through water without leaving tracks or swimming harmoniously *with* life's stream.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Moving from comic anecdotes to historical context, Watts explains that Zen belongs squarely inside Mahāyāna Buddhism—where awakening blossoms right inside family, streets, and daily labor rather than retreating to mountain caves. Hence Zen artists portray enlightened Bodhisattvas not as stiff ascetics, but as wandering ragamuffins like Sengai's painting of Hotei (Budai), stretching awake unbothered by Buddha's passing or Confucius's solemnity. Born from Indian Mahāyāna passing through Chinese Daoist and Confucian soil around Kumārajīva's translation bureau in 414 CE, Zen took shape through Kumārajīva's bold pupil (who argued from the *Nirvāṇa Sūtra* that even rocks and heretics hold Buddha-nature and awaken instantaneously when 'the bottom falls out of the bucket').

Following Bodhidharma, ex-general Huike (Eka), and Third Patriarch Sōsan (author of *Xinxin Ming*), Sixth Patriarch Huineng (Enō, remembered in the *Platform Sūtra*) warning against turning *dhyāna/Ch'an/Zen* into wooden quietism: blanking the mind makes a 'stone Buddha,' not a living one. Master Tanka dramatized this vividly by chopping up a cold temple's wooden Buddha statue for firewood and dryly mocking the furious priest by poking ashes for cremation jewels (*śarīra*). Similarly, when asked 'What is Buddha?', a Tang master famously snapped 'A dried turd!'—not to shock for shock's sake, but to redefine mental purity as effortless non-stickiness: birds staining zero sky, geese reflections leaving zero mark on still lakes, and swimmers ceasing to fight the swift Dao stream.

## Stories, Analogies & Anecdotes Offered

- **Sengai's Painting of Hotei (Budai)**: Zen painting of the fat wandering tramp stretching awake, unconcerned with Maitreya's delay or Confucian propriety.
- **Kumārajīva & the Nirvāṇa Sūtra (414 CE)**: A pupil branded a heretic for preaching universal Buddha-nature and sudden awakening until the freshly translated *Nirvāṇa Sūtra* vindicated him.
- **Tanka Burning the Wooden Buddha**: On a freezing night, Master Tanka chops a wooden temple statue for kindling—then pokes ashes for non-existent *śarīra* jewels, asking to burn the other statue too.
- **Swimming With the Stream**: Contrasting futile exhaustion fighting the torrent of the Dao with gliding along using the river's full momentum.
