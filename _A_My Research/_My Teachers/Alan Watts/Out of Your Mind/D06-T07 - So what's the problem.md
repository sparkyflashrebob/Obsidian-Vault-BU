### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Cutting to the bedrock question—'What does it feel like inside when you imagine everything dissolving like foam into nothing?'—Watts notes how priests and governments weaponize fear of unknown post-mortem punishment to rule people, which explains why rulers deeply distrust mystics who no longer dread dying. Yet the common nightmare of floating in cold darkness forever rests on a logical confusion, as illuminated by Watts' conversation with a congenitally blind seminar student: darkness requires contrast with sight. Like writing ink across an empty sheet—or noticing the unimaginable blankness behind one's own visual field—form can only appear because luminous emptiness stands invisibly behind it.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts asks listeners to stop debating theories and feel directly in their bellies what happens when contemplating total dissolution—the universe dissolving like smoke or water bubbles into sheer nothingness. Why do societies shudder so? Across history, rulers and priests have populated the blank void past death with vague threats so subjects stay docile ('do as I say, or die'); thus no authoritarian power trusts mystics, for anyone who knows 'it takes nothing to have something' cannot easily be bullied.

Crucially, Watts dismantles humanity's eerie fantasy of going to sleep and never waking up—imagined secretly as being buried alive in chilly blackness forever. Recalling a discussion with a seminar participant born blind, he notes she had no concept whatever of 'darkness,' since black exists strictly alongside light. Look directly behind your own eyes right now: there is neither light-pool nor black wall, only unthinkable zero—and out of that very zero looks every bright sight you behold. Like legible ink requiring blank paper underneath (write endlessly without blank background and everything turns illegible black smudge), figure always implies unseen background: emptiness itself is the quiet mother of form.
