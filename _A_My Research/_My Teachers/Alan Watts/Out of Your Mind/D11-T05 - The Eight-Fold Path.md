### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Approaching the Fourth Noble Truth (*Mārga*, the Eightfold Path), Watts tells a beloved story of D. T. Suzuki amusing students in Hawaii by solemnly announcing *shōken* ('right view') as step one, pausing at step two, and quipping 'Oh, I forget second step—look it up in book!' Rather than reciting rigid formulas, Watts highlights the Path's three functional divisions—understanding, conduct, and meditation—and critiques the standard English rendering of *samyak* (Pāli *sammā*) as 'right.' Related etymologically to 'sum' (total, complete, all-embracing) and centered equilibrium, *samyak* signifies a 'middle-wayed' fulcrum of balance across view, speech, livelihood, and action.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Turning to the Fourth Noble Truth (*Mārga*, 'path'), Watts playfully mirrors D. T. Suzuki's famous lecture at the University of Hawaii: after dramatically unveiling step one (*shōken*, Right View), the scholar suddenly shrugged and told students to look up step two in a textbook! What truly matters, Watts stresses, is not memorizing numbered headings mechanically, but grasping the Path's three organic pillars: wisdom/understanding, ethical conduct, and meditative absorption.

Crucially, Watts challenges how Western translations flatten every step's prefix *samyak* (*sammā* in Pāli) into stiff, moralistic 'Right' ('Right View,' 'Right Livelihood'). Pointing to its root kinship with English 'sum'—meaning total, integrated, all-encompassing—Watts offers a richer translation: *middle-wayed*, functioning like a delicately poised fulcrum balancing every aspect of life. Seen this way, Buddhism presents no external checklist of righteous rules, but an invitation toward whole, centered harmony across thought, relationship, work, and stillness.

This always reminds me of a story about Dr. Suzuki, who is a very, very great Buddhist scholar. And many years ago, he was giving a fundamental lecture on Buddhism at the University of Hawaii.

[00:40] And he got to, he'd been going through these four truths, and he said, ah, fourth Noble Truth is called a Noble Eightfold Path. First step of a Noble Eightfold Path called a shōken. Shōken Japanese mean right view. For Buddhism, fundamentally is right view, right way of viewing this world. Second step of Noble Eightfold Path is, oh, I forget second step, you look it up in the book.

[01:21] Well, I'm going to do rather the same thing. What is important is this, the eightfold path is really got three divisions in it. The first are concerned with understanding. The second division is concerned with conduct, and the third division is concerned with meditation. And every step in the path is preceded by the Sanskrit word Samyak, in which some is the keyword in Pali Sammā.

[02:14] And so the first step, Samyag Dṛṣṭi, which means, Dṛṣṭi means a view, a way of looking at things, a vision, an attitude, something like that. But this word Samyak is in ordinary texts on Buddhism, almost invariably translated right. This is a very bad translation. It does, of course, the word is used in certain contexts in Sanskrit to mean right, correct.

[02:57] But it has other and wider meanings. Some means, like our word some, which is derived from it, complete, total, all embracing. It also has the meaning of middle weighed, representing as it were the fulcrum, the center, the point of balance in a totality. Middle weighed way of looking at things. Middle weighed way of understanding, the dharma. Middle weighed way of speech, of conduct, of livelihood, and so on. Now this is particularly cogent when it comes to Buddhist ideas of behavior.
