### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Turning to the 17th-century democratization of Japanese culture, Watts pairs haiku pioneer Matsuo Bashō (contrasting simple 17-syllable directness against over-allusive poetry like T. S. Eliot's *Four Quartets* quoting Julian of Norwich and the *Bhagavad Gītā*) with Zen egalitarian Bankei Yōtaku. Teaching ordinary farmers around the 'Unborn' (*Fushō*), Bankei offered immediate proof right in the lecture hall: without straining, listeners effortlessly distinguish a distant crow's caw from a twittering sparrow while attending to his voice. Rejecting Hakuin's rigid sitting rules, Bankei abolished *keisaku* shoulder beatings—insisting 'even a sleeping man is still a Buddha'—and likened trying to scrub thoughts clean to washing off blood with blood or polishing a brick into a mirror. Appropriately, Hakuin produced many institutional successors while elusive Bankei left none.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Closing Session 8 (*The World as Just So*), Watts moves to Japan's vibrant seventeenth century, when culture spilled outside elite aristocratic salons onto common streets. Just as Bashō distilled complex *Zenrin* insights (*'Wind drops, flowers keep falling; bird calls, the mountain grows more mysterious'*) into 17-syllable haiku freed from heavy literary footnotes akin to T. S. Eliot's learned allusions in *Four Quartets*, master Bankei carried Zen directly out of intimidating monasteries to ordinary farmers.

Bankei's radical alternative to Hakuin's punishing koan treadmill rested entirely on *Fushō*—the 'Unborn' (birthless, immaculate Buddha-mind received from one's parents). To skeptical priests, Bankei gave an instant, undeniable demonstration: while sitting calmly listening to me, if a crow caws and a sparrow twitters behind your back, you hear both crystal clearly without deciding to listen! That natural, effortless awareness neither needs advancing nor can recede; letting a thousand shallow thoughts flicker across its expanse leaves zero stain. Serving as abbot of Myōshin-ji (spoken here as Noshinji), Bankei banned the disciplinary *keisaku* stick because 'even a sleeping man is still a Buddha,' dismissed frantic striving as rubbing bricks or washing blood with blood, and walked gently out of history with zero institutional lineage—which many consider his finest accomplishment.

[04:22] Nobody here can deny this. All of you are living Buddhas because the birthless mind which each possesses is the beginning and the basis of all. [04:53] Indeed, I suppose, like most people, you've memorized this phrase while being ignorant of the fact of birthlessness.
