### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Revisiting the two pillars of ego—fictitious self-image plus muscular strain—Watts moves to the next illusion: believing our conditioned scale of 'good,' 'bad,' 'important,' and 'trivial' belongs to reality itself. Zen trains students to act immediately without hesitant chewing, breaking the inertia of creatures of habit like the bowler-hatted Londoner who panics if offered smoked salmon instead of his daily beef sandwich. Beneath arbitrary contrasts (loud/soft, red/blue), consciousness turns out to resemble a single bright point zipping across a television tube—meaning an ordinary sound like a metal twang (*boing*) or candle flame reveals the entire cosmos once freed from adult utility.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Once a person sees directly that no solid controller sits inside the skin, meditation or contemplation opens naturally: happenings simply witness themselves. Yet one stubborn spell remains—the cultural value map labeling some vibrations 'wonderful' and others 'wretched.' Because people assume deeply etched habits take heroic years to shift, Zen training demands immediate unhesitating response on cue, starving the mind of time to complain 'I have always done it another way!'—breaking the rigidity exemplified by a London clerk in a bowler hat whose day collapses if his regular bartender swaps a beef sandwich for smoked salmon.

Watts strips down sensory perception to pure vibration. Color red has no isolated existence apart from yellow and violet; loud exists only alongside soft; even a television picture dissolves on closer timing into a single hyper-fast dot of light fooling a retentive retina. Music makes ideal contemplative training precisely because pure notes 'mean' nothing outside themselves. Reclaiming a child's delight in staring into candle flames, wood grain, or listening to a metal spring twang *boing* opens the universe inside the humblest syllable.
