### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Comparing split human consciousness to dinosaurs sporting one brain in their skull and another in their rump, Watts reassures listeners that present mental jitters mark an evolutionary transition rather than a curse—remembering 'an oak is an acorn's way of becoming other acorns,' and in spring's landscape 'no flowering branch is superior or inferior.' Moving beyond childish good-guy/bad-guy binaries (cops vs. robbers, capitalists vs. communists), Watts acknowledges a paradox: as lecturer, his 'in-group' teacher role relies totally upon students showing up and lending him language. Sharing his own childhood trauma of piano scales under a pencil-rapping teacher (leaving a lifetime echo of 'bombinating papa' and 'screaming mama'), he explains why liberation cures education's scars—and why Zen masters roar like ferocious dragons in purple robes at first, only to laugh like affectionate brothers once students break through.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts opens with playful biological wit: humanity suffers from evolutionary jitters—resembling huge dinosaurs trying to coordinate one small brain in the head with a second brain down in the rump. Yet rather than despising the jitters or grading maturity along egoic ranks, remember nature's circularity ('a chicken is one egg's way of becoming others') and the *Zenrin* verse: *'In the landscape of spring, there is nothing superior and nothing inferior; the flowering branches grow naturally, some short, some long.'* Moralistic sermons from pulpits and judicial benches keep treating polarity struggles like a cartoon duel between heroes and villains.

Candidly facing the irony of teaching non-duality from a stage, Watts notes that teacher and listener arise completely together—without students and shared English, he would have nobody to talk to! He traces hesitant adulthood straight back to anxious schooling, recalling his childhood piano teacher hovering with a sharp pencil to whack wrong notes—teaching lifelong hesitation lest the pencil drop again. While children must learn rules to join society's game, grown-ups require deliberate *de-education*—which is why Zen masters deliberately stage a scary dragon-act behind formal gold robes: keeping conventional authorities calm while teasing bold candidates until teacher and student trade jokes like affectionate brothers.
