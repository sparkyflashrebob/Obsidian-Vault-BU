### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Entering daily *sanzen* interviews—gong strikes, full prostrations, and merciless instant dismissal with a single ring of the master's bell—reveals Zen's deliberate psychological vise: commanding complete sincerity before an imposing tiger-like father figure. Rejecting copied 'Zen antics'—illustrated by Watts' Kyoto friend smuggling a tame garden bullfrog up his kimono sleeve, only for the *Rōshi* to shake his head: 'Mm-hmm, *too intellectual*' (too contrived!)—the training intensifies into brutal five-day *sesshin* (*observing heart-mind* on four hours' sleep), pushing seekers toward 'great doubt.' Concluding with two friends—one five-day British *satori* report and a Fulbright scholar breaking through on his final night upon seeing *there was nothing to realize*—Watts cites Linji (Rinzai): Zen holds out a closed 'empty fist' or a golden autumn leaf solely to soothe a crying child until it opens its hand cleanly.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Watts lifts the curtain on *sanzen*—Zen's fiercest crucible—where a candidate strikes the corridor bell three times, kneels bowing forehead to floor, states his koan, and risks summary dismissal at a single brisk tinkle of the *Rōshi*'s handbell. Psychologically, the ritual springs an intense double-bind: commanding spontaneous sincerity before an authority figure far stricter than one's own father! Desperate novices imitate stories out of books—like Watts' acquaintance in Kyoto who grabbed a tame bullfrog from the garden pond inside his kimono sleeve and slammed it dramatically onto the interview mat, only to hear the calm *Rōshi* murmur, 'Too intellectual' (meaning stiffly theatrical and copycat).

When rejection builds the 'great doubt' to fever pitch, monasteries launch five-to-six-day *sesshin* ('examining the *shin* / heart-mind'): four hours' sleep, relentless sitting, twice-daily *sanzen*, and do-or-die intensity (comparable to modern rapid 'five-day *satori*' programs). Watts recalls an American Fulbright scholar agonizing until the very eve of flying home before breaking through simply upon realizing *there was nothing deficient to fix and nothing special to attain!* That climax fulfills Rinzai's tender parable: Zen dangles an intriguing closed 'empty fist' or hands a crying toddler a bright yellow autumn leaf called 'gold'—solely to captivate curiosity until the fist opens and laughs at empty air.
