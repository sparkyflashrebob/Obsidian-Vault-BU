### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Moving past both 'organic' and 'mechanical' to sheer *tathatā* ('suchness'—playfully sounded as *da-da-da*), Watts resolves the classic lover's terror: why profound joy always carries secret dread that tragedy could steal the beloved away. Keeping a quiet reservation at the back of the mind—what German calls a *Hintergedanke*, whispering 'this is the game'—removes primordial dread, liberating a Bodhisattva to love lavishly without sterile detachment. Quoting R. H. Blyth's letter announcing he 'abandoned *satori* to become deeply attached to as many people and things as possible,' Watts concludes that meditative silence transforms monotonous chronic burdens (bedsores, dishwashing) into timeless 'wasting of time.'

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Transcending the split between warm organic flowers and cold plastic gears brings perception to sheer *tathatā* ('thatness' or 'suchness'), which Watts translates playfully into pure drum syllables: *da-da-da*. Normally, whenever human beings stake everything on something 'really amounting to something'—falling madly in love and scheming until affection is returned—they immediately pay the shadow price of terror: *what if something kills them tomorrow?* The antidote is not cold armored indifference, but carrying a delicate German *Hintergedanke* (an unspoken background awareness): *this is the game*. Paradoxically, knowing the dance is illusory makes it safe to fling oneself wildly into color, passion, and tenderness.

Watts gently skewers puritanical spiritual seekers who expect an enlightened sage to look scrubbed bare like a barren rock free of ornaments, beauty, or love. Citing R. H. Blyth's delightfully irreverent postcard—*'As for me, I have abandoned satori altogether, and I'm trying to become as deeply attached as I can to as many people and things as possible'*—Watts shows true awakening blossoms as fearless involvement. Furthermore, resting periodically in meditation—'the perfect waste of time'—grants resilience for life's hardest trials: not dramatic heroics, where ecstasy sometimes sparks spontaneously, but grinding, monotonous daily patience like lying in traction with bedsores, balancing accounts, or washing endless dishes.
