### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Comparing initial koan struggle to 'a mosquito biting an iron bull' or 'swallowing a ball of molten lead,' Watts clarifies why passing the first koan (seeing no safety exists, giving up attempts to 'beat the game,' entering *śūnyatā*) merely opens the 'frontier gate.' Masters immediately assign further koans across five progressive tiers: the opening *Hīnayāna* koan (achieving personal nirvana), followed by four *Mahāyāna* levels returning that freedom into the world—from playful 'miracle' koans (snapping out a candle in Timbuktu, pulling Tokyo out of one's sleeve) all the way through refined social conduct and monastic precepts. Citing American concert pianist Walter Nowick's decade-long training soon culminating back on a New England farm, Watts shows graduates fanning out smoothly as laypeople, village priests, or future *Rōshis*.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

When existential struggle breaks open and the student walks into *sanzen* no longer caring a damn—seeing his desperate urge to 'outsmart life' was purely self-invented, like a mosquito chewing an iron bull or coughing up molten lead—a wise teacher greets breakthrough calmly: *'Good! You've stepped across the threshold, yet you must redouble effort.'* Far from finishing Zen, solving 'one hand' or *Mu* passes only tier one of five koan levels.

Watts distinguishes the first *Hīnayāna* phase—attaining fearless nirvana where holding onto safety collapses because there is literally nothing solid to grasp—from the four subsequent *Mahāyāna* tiers bringing awakening straight back into human action. These escalate through playful paradoxical 'miracle' riddles (halting distant bells, pinching candles in Timbuktu) into subtle nuances of ethical sensitivity and daily monastic etiquette—training that routinely takes ten years or more. Watts spotlights American classical pianist Walter Nowick nearing graduation to launch the West's first accredited *Sōdō* back home on a farm, before graduates disperse joyfully with cheers as ordinary layfolk, married temple priests, or future *Rōshis*.
