### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Turning from physics to aesthetics, Watts argues that poets intuit a secret philosophers miss: transience itself is breathtaking once clinging ceases. Reading Shelley's 'dome of many-colored glass' and Macbeth's 'tomorrow, and tomorrow,' he introduces the untranslatable Japanese aesthetic of *yūgen* ('deep mystery of the valley')—felt watching sails slip behind distant islands, wild geese vanish into clouds, or looking across Mount Tamalpais without rushing to verify the unseen side. Blending Chinese verse on windless falling petals, childhood memories of lone Pyrenean cowbells ringing out mountain stillness, and T. S. Eliot's *Four Quartets*, Watts punctures immortalist fantasies: frozen at the exact same age forever, humans would become grotesque Madame Tussaud's waxworks.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Moving cleanly from impermanence as suffering (*dukkha* when clutched) to impermanence as enchantment, Watts unpacks why Western poetry glows brightest precisely around fading life. In Shelley's *Adonais*—'Life, like a dome of many-coloured glass, stains the white radiance of eternity until death shatters it'—or Shakespeare's mournful 'tomorrow, and tomorrow,' the poet's secret thrill lives inside the shattering transience itself.

To name this wonder, Watts teaches the Japanese aesthetic *yūgen*—literally rooted in characters evoking deep valley shadows—never destroyed by anxious prying. We taste *yūgen* spotting distant ships slip behind an offshore island, watching wild geese vanish into mist, or gazing at Mount Tamalpais's ridge satisfied not to drive behind it to inspect the view. Illustrating *yūgen* with a Chinese couplet ('The wind drops, yet petals keep falling; a bird cries, and the mountain grows deeper in stillness') and his own childhood memory of clanking cowbells magnifying Pyrenean silence, Watts ends with T. S. Eliot and a comic thought-experiment: magically frozen forever by 'zit!' immortality, everyone present would petrify into horrifying Madame Tussaud's wax figures.
