### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Opening with the English dinner-party joke about Sir Roderick confident of heaven yet scandalized by 'depressing' death-talk, Watts diagnoses Western society's awkward squeamishness around terminal illness—comparing embarrassed hospital politeness toward dying patients to nervous awkwardness around a loud drunk. Just as Dr. Grantly Dick-Read demystified childbirth pain as culturally trained tension rather than inevitable curse, Watts proposes re-imagining death as healthy release. Praising wise priests who speak bluntly to the dying—encouraging them to give possessions away and clear unresolved honesty like Adlai Stevenson did—and quoting Ananda Coomaraswamy's prayer ('that death will not find me still unannihilated'), Watts imagines joyful 'Institutions for Creative Dying' stocked with champagne, sacred liturgy, psychedelics, and music.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

With sharp satire balanced by tenderness, Watts confronts Western death-denial head-on. Recounting a dinner-party host polling guests on the afterlife—ending with devout churchwarden Sir Roderick booming that he is bound straight for everlasting bliss while begging everyone to drop such a 'depressing conversation'—he exposes how morticians and hospital etiquette collaborate in polite deception. Because physicians are socially scripted strictly as healers, terminal patients suffer sterile distance and false cheer ('back home for a picnic soon!') precisely when aching for candid presence.

Drawing a bold parallel to mid-century obstetrics—when Dr. Grantly Dick-Read revealed that much labor agony stemmed from fearful muscular tension rather than natural necessity—Watts urges a parallel cultural shift toward dying. Instead of lingering lonely amid plastic tubes pretending tragedy hasn't struck, a mature community gathers honestly around a dying friend to celebrate freedom from bills, possessions, and lingering regrets (recalling Adlai Stevenson clearing political compromises off his chest shortly before dying). Citing Ananda Coomaraswamy's haunting prayer—*'I pray that death will not come and find me still unannihilate'*—Watts envisions bright 'hospitals for delightful dying' uniting champagne celebration, glorious sacred ritual, psychedelics, and music to go out with a joyful bang rather than a whimper.
