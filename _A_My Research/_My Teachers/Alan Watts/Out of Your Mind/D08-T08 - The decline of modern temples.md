### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Stepping back critically, Watts classifies heavy koan pressure as 'homeopathic' medicine—echoing William Blake's proverb ('the fool who persists in his folly will become wise') and risky psychiatric shock treatments that push over-eaters or alcoholics to extremity. Because such extreme strain demands rare mentors and often breaks modern minds, great Kyoto temples stand nearly hollow: Myōshin-ji holds 80 priests where 600 once trained, mostly reluctant sons inheriting family shrines. Worse, since 18th-century reformer Hakuin, answers grew codified alongside mandatory verse-matching from the *Zenrin Kushū*—prompting a mischievous monk to threaten publishing an answer key! Advocating timely modern koans ('What's the first word in the Bible?' or asking why Schweppes bearded pitchman Commander Whitehead has no beard!), Watts points toward living immediacy outside archaic idioms.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Summing up Rinzai's demanding monastery machine, Watts names its secret principle: homeopathic cure ('hair of the dog that bit you'). Since talking cannot convince anyone their cherished ego is a post-hypnotic hallucination, Zen borrows William Blake's gambit—*the fool who persists in his folly will become wise*—comparable to bold clinicians forcing binge-eaters or drinkers face-first into excess. Effective under brilliant supervision, this severe hothouse remains precarious and plainly alien to modern youth: Kyoto's colossal Myōshin-ji complex, built for 600 monks, now houses barely 80—too many merely clergy heirs fulfilling family duty without burning inner hunger.

Compounding obsolescence, master Hakuin's eighteenth-century system fossilized koans into fixed approved responses paired with mandatory classical poetry couplets from the *Zenrin Kushū*—inviting recent satire when a young rebel monk threatened to print the whole answer book! Even classical sayings like 'sound of one hand' presupposed an everyday Chinese proverb (*'one hand won't clap'*), meaning stale medieval riddles lose punch today. Freshly minted teachers invent crisp live exchanges instead ('No, too late!' or teasing modern commercials like asking why bearded Schweppes icon Commander Whitehead has no beard), restoring electric unpredictability.
