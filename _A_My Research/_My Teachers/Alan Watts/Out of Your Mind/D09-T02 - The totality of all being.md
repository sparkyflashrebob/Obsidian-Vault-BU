### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Grounded in the poetic *Upaniṣads*, Watts introduces *Ātman*—Self in the cosmic sense ('the totality of all being')—which necessarily evades logic because every logical category requires an external opposite outside its pigeonhole box. Borrowing Alfred Korzybski's phrase 'the unspeakable world' (both beyond words and mildly taboo!), Watts likens *Ātman* to a radio speaker's vibrating diaphragm: zero songs mention the thin metal cone, yet every chord and melody rides *on* it ('this one's on me!'). Rejecting lonely solipsism (joking about a 'Congress of solipsists' arguing over who exists), he compares human beings to sensitive nerve-ends and eyes sprouting from one shared central Self—captured in the Japanese haiku, *'A hundred gourds from the mind of one vine.'*

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Turning from Zen back into India's foundational *Upaniṣads*, Watts unpacks *Ātman* not as a puffed-up personal ego inside your ribcage, but as the shared basis of all existence. Strictly logical philosophers dismiss *Ātman* as meaningless precisely because logic operates via contrast—sorting everything into inside/outside pigeonholes ('animal, vegetable, mineral'). Yet ultimate reality includes stars *and* interstellar emptiness alike, sitting outside every conceivable box alongside Alfred Korzybski's 'unspeakable world' (both unutterable and socially hush-hush).

How then can one imagine one Self underlying billions of distinct voices? Listen to music streaming through a radio loudspeaker: none of the songs sing about the metal diaphragm, yet every trumpet blast and flute trill is literally a vibration *of* that diaphragm—what Hindus express by saying the entire universe rests 'on the Self.' Far from ridiculous solipsism (whose followers could hardly convene a conference without arguing who is the sole real dreamer!), every living person, animal, and rock acts like a delicate nerve-ending—an eye peeking outward on behalf of one vine, echoing the haiku: *'A hundred gourds from the mind of one vine.'*
