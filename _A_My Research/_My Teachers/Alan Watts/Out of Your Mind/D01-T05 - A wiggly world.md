### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Using the Big Bang ink-splat image, Watts shows how we identify solely with one complex curlicue at the fringe of the explosion while forgetting we belong to the whole splash. Inspired by a bright Italian high-schooler who realized 'a thing is a noun,' Watts argues nature contains no separate things—everything is wiggly. To grab a slippery fish we cast a numbered net, which becomes calculus and human naming; yet cutting up a chicken doesn't mean chickens hatch pre-bitted. Because we mistake verbal cutouts for separate objects, we live terrified that our individual 'wave' will vanish at death.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Opening with the Big Bang compared to flinging a bottle of ink against a wall—dense at the center, branching into intricate droplets at the edges—Watts illustrates why ordinary consciousness feels trivial: we shrink our identity to one isolated curlicue out on the fringe and forget we are the original bang still banging. To dissolve this alienation, he tackles the deepest superstition of everyday speech: the belief in discrete 'things' and separate 'events.'

Recalling a visit to a high school class where an Italian student astutely answered that 'a thing is a noun,' Watts drives the insight home: there are no nouns in nature, only dynamic verbs and continuous 'wiggles'—clouds, mountains, trees, and human bodies alike. When humans want to control a slippery wiggly fish, they throw a net over it, count the squares up and across, and invent calculus and language—marking off 'bits' of the wiggle. Yet nature does not come pre-sliced any more than an egg hatches into a pre-cut fryer! You and I are as continuous with the cosmos as a wave is with the ocean (*'the ocean waves and the universe peoples'*). Trapped by social hypnosis into believing we exist only behind our skins, people live terrified between 'the maternity ward and the crematorium,' preaching church creeds they don't genuinely feel because their gut common sense remains trapped in the fully automatic nightmare.

## Stories, Analogies & Anecdotes Offered

- **Bottle of Ink Thrown at a Wall**: Watts pictures the Big Bang as ink splattering outward into ever finer curlicues on the perimeter, showing we are continuous with the initial splash rather than detached dust.
- **Italian High-Schooler: 'A Thing Is a Noun'**: When Watts asked teenagers to define a 'thing,' most offered synonyms like 'object' until a perceptive Italian girl answered, 'a thing is a noun'—exposing 'thinghood' as grammar rather than physics.
- **Catching a Wiggly Fish with a Net**: To hold a slippery fish, humans weave a numbered grid—originating coordinates, calculus, and named events—mistaking the grid's squares for reality's joints, like expecting a cut-up fryer straight out of an egg.
- **Father Maskell & Jehovah's Witnesses**: Watts cites Father Maskell's grim summary (*'between the maternity ward and the crematorium'*) and notes even earnest door-knocking fundamentalists reveal how polite disbelief replaces genuine faith.
