### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Once admitted, the student meets the piercing, direct *Rōshi*, who declares 'We teach nothing here!' (knowing *śūnyatā*, the great void, underlies everything) and swiftly halts pious talk of escaping *saṃsāra* by asking, 'Who is it that wants to be emancipated?' Or in a classic ambush, after cozy small talk about hometowns and college, the master flips out a palm: 'Why is my hand so much like the Buddha's hand?' Explaining that *koan* (*kung-an*) literally means a legal 'case precedent,' Watts describes training inside the *Sōdō* under the *Jikijitsu*: cross-legged posture on thick *zafu* cushions (as thick as a San Francisco phone directory!), incense-stick half-hours broken by brisk walking, firm shoulder slaps with the flat *keisaku* stick (not merely to fight sleep, Watts winks from investigation!), and open-eyed listening to distant traffic, hammering, and sweet rain on roof tiles before tackling classic koans like 'one hand' or Jōshū's *Mu*.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Stepping at last inside the interview quarters, the novice confronts a fierce yet uncanny *Rōshi* whose gaze cuts right past polite masks. Telling the newcomer flatly that Zen teaches zero curriculum—which any sharp student recognizes as *śūnyatā*—the teacher cuts open pious excuses: 'I want liberation from birth and death!' 'Who wants liberation?' Or pausing mid-chat about family and schooling, the *Rōshi* flashes a hand: 'Why is my hand so much like the Buddha's hand? Who are you underneath your name?' That shocking wedge embodies a *koan* (*kung-an*), literally a legal precedent case distilled from ancient Tang exchanges.

Sent back to the *Sōdō* under the strict hall leader (*Jikijitsu*), the monk learns communal rhythm: sitting cross-legged atop cushions stout as a San Francisco phone book while incense sticks mark 30-minute spells broken by swift walking circuits and brisk *keisaku* shoulder blows (which Watts wryly insists double as British-school discipline minus the neuroses). Unlike tight-shut eyes elsewhere, Zen sits with soft open eyes and unobstructed ears—letting distant cars, dogs, carpentry hammers, and pattering roof-rain flow unhindered—first settling thought via counted breaths up to ten, then plunging into core koans: 'sound of one hand,' 'original face before parents conceived,' and Jōshū's crisp 'No! (*Mu!*)' on whether dogs have Buddha-nature.
