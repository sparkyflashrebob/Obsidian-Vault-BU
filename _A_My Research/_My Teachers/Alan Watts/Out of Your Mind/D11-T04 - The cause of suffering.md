### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Examining the Second Noble Truth (*tṛṣṇā*—thirst, clinging, or psychological 'being stuck' where a Buddha flows unimpeded like rising river water over any dam), Watts shows why Buddhism's 'Middle Way' operates far more subtly than avoiding asceticism and hedonism. When an eager student tries to eliminate desire, he catches himself desiring *not* to desire—and the teacher gently calibrates: give up only as much desire as you actually can! Moving to the Third Noble Truth (*Nirvāṇa*), Watts re-translates it from grim 'blowing out' into vital 'breathing out': holding one's breath out of anxiety (like slaving over suburban lawns and bills to maintain a 'standard of living') ruins life itself, whereas releasing the grip brings *Nirvāṇa* right here and now.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Moving to the Second Noble Truth, Watts unpacks *tṛṣṇā* (Pāli *taṇhā*, literally 'thirst'—cognate with English thirst) as clinging, fixation, or psychological blockage. An attached person hesitates and stalls in life; an awakened Buddha, by contrast, resembles a river whose waters quietly mount behind any dam until overflowing smoothly forward. Yet telling students 'don't cling' triggers an immediate comic bind inside the Buddhist dialogue: students aggressively *crave* to stop craving! True 'Middle Way' skillfulness answers that trap subtly—neither bingeing on desire nor aggressively grasping after desirelessness, nudging extremists gently back toward natural balance.

Turning to the Third Noble Truth, *Nirvāṇa* (*Nibbāna*), Watts counters Western stereotypes of gloomy annihilation. Reversing the usual 'blowing out the candle' cliché into 'breathing out' (*prāṇa* released), he draws a vivid everyday parallel: holding your breath turns stifling within seconds, because breath returns only when let go. Modern suburbanites slave away maintaining lawns, mortgages, and paperwork simply to 'keep holding onto life,' never actually living; releasing that anxious clench restores presence to *this* very moment.
