### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Watts defines the guru not as a taskmaster conferring enlightenment, but as an Awakener who looks past our mask and says, 'Come off it, I know who you are!' Citing Sri Ramana Maharshi's sparkling eyes and counter-question ('Who is asking?'), he shows how guilt—like a Good Friday card declaring 'This have I done for thee'—acts as a self-imposed veil delaying awakening until we feel we've suffered enough to deserve it. Choosing to identify with your whole involuntary-and-voluntary organism replaces victimhood with courageous ownership.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Stating his metaphysics plainly—one central Self playing all roles in universal hide-and-seek—Watts turns to what happens when a seeker starts wanting out of the game and approaches a guru. Rather than dispensing secret tricks, a true master mirrors Sri Ramana Maharshi: when disciples ask about past lives, Maharshi smiles and asks, *'Who is asking the question?'* Looking at his photograph by Watts' front door, one sees eyes shimmering with playful recognition: *'Come off it, Shiva—what a funny way God has come on today!'*

Why then do spiritual traditions invent tortuous initiation hurdles and 'put you through the mill'? Because, Watts argues, seekers themselves demand a price tag before admitting the truth. Religious guilt—illustrated by his childhood Good Friday postcard showing a bleeding Christ accusing every ordinary meal—convincing people their very existence is an offense serves as a self-guarded sanctuary veil. Just as someone visiting a psychiatrist tacitly defines themselves as needing their head examined, approaching a Zen master earns '30 blows with a stick' for inventing a nonexistent problem. By reclaiming both voluntary acts and involuntary mastery (beating your heart, digesting, breathing), we step out of the posture of helpless sinner into fearless authorship of our life.
