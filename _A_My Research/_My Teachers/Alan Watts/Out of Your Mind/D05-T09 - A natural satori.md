### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Watts recounts meeting German diplomat and Black Forest Zen teacher Karlfried von Dürckheim in Zurich (1958), who observed 'natural *satori*' emerging when people in impossible wartime extremes completely accepted death: a soldier beneath a whistling bomb (later proving a dud), a hopeless camp prisoner, and a displaced refugee who lost every vestige of identity. Far from hallucinating, these were rare moments they truly woke up—proving dying offers life's deepest chance to see clearly if approached without embalmed denial or tube-fed panic.

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

Drawing on his 1958 Zurich conversation with former diplomat and Black Forest retreat founder Karlfried von Dürckheim, Watts presents vivid evidence that total surrender unlocks extraordinary lucidity. In three wartime archetypes—listening to a whistling bomb directly overhead, enduring endless anonymous confinement in a concentration camp, or standing as a stripped refugee without family, home, or social identity—the moment a person stops fighting reality and accepts utter helplessness (becoming 'the mosquito biting the iron bull'), terror flips into crystal clarity: not a grain of dust stands out of place, and freedom feels light as air.

When survivors tell relatives about these awakenings, families dismiss them as stress hallucinations—whereas Dürckheim showed they were the rare hours those people finally woke up from ordinary trance. Watts applies this directly to how modern culture treats death. Because existence needs no 'quantitative continuity'—one lucid moment outweighs endless anxious years—dying invites conscious surrender. Instead, following Unamuno's observation that humans alone hoard their dead, our culture embalms corpses against worms, turns birth and death alike into cold clinical pathologies, and traps elders in frantic tube-fed medical prolongation.
