### Condensed Version of the Teachings

Tracing Zen's genealogy after Enō's death in 715 CE across the Five Houses down to the two surviving schools—'gutsy' koan-focused Rinzai and serene Sōtō—Watts dates China's Golden Age between 715 and 1000 CE, before alchemy and pursuit of occult *siddhi* powers sidetracked mainland Zen. Fortunately, Eisai (c. 1130s/late 12th c.) and Dōgen (founder of lush Eiheiji) transplanted vibrant Ch'an to Japan. Recalling how Tang monks hiked miles from monastery to monastery practicing 'walking Zen,' Watts unpacks the Chinese character for *wu*/*satori*: 'heart-mind' (*xin*) fused with 'five mouths/senses' (*wu*), portraying awakening as one's heart leaping into all five senses at once—discovering you are the entire works ('it').

### Expanded Version of the Teachings

From Sixth Patriarch Enō's five great successors sprang Zen's Five Houses, surviving primarily in Rinzai's confrontational koan intensity and Sōtō's calm 'just sitting.' Watts identifies 715–1000 CE as Chinese Zen's incomparable Golden Age—after which later dynasties diluted Ch'an with Daoist longevity alchemy and quests for supernormal *siddhis*, which classical masters dismissed as irrelevant distractions. The vital spark migrated intact to Japan through Eisai and Dōgen, whose atmospheric mountain monastery at Eiheiji endures.

Crucially, Golden Age monks trained primarily through 'walking Zen' rather than stationary marathons—hiking dusty roads between masters hoping an unexpected spark would trigger *wu* (*satori*). Analyzing the Chinese ideogram for awakening—combining *xin* (heart-mind) beside *wu* (I / five mouths)—Watts translates it poetically as 'my heart came into all five senses!' Discovering you were never a fragile ego trapped in a bag of skin but the seamless whole ('it') feels logically nonsensical (since 'everything' lacks any external contrast) yet strikes perception with electric vividness.
