
# Mindfulness and RAIN

## Meditation and Mindfulness Practices


Drawing from the provided sources and our conversation history, several methods and practices for meditation and mindfulness are detailed.

At its core, meditation is described as a deliberate training or directing of attention that awakens us beyond the conditioned mind and habitual thinking, revealing the nature of reality1.... It opens us to perspectives larger than ordinary consciousness or the small sense of self3. It's emphasized that "meditation" isn't one single thing, but rather there are many forms or skillful means to quiet the mind and open the heart1.... Mindfulness is one of these forms6....

Mindfulness is defined as awareness cultivated by paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally6.... It is also described as loving attention or loving awareness5.... Mindfulness involves being a spacious, kind, nonjudging awareness of the present9. It also includes an appropriate response to the present situation, indicating it is not merely passive9. Mindfulness serves awakening and freedom9.... Bringing awareness to experience deepens presence and freedom15....

Mindfulness is traditionally taught as focusing on four different dimensions or foundations of experience: the body, feelings, mind (thoughts), and the process of life itself or mind states1....

Here are some specific methods and practices mentioned:

•

Mindfulness of Breath: A basic mindfulness practice often used to stabilize attention and serve as an anchor to the present moment21.... It's not about controlling the breath but allowing the body to breathe itself33. You can notice where you feel it most easily or experience the whole body breathing33. Details include noticing if the breath is long or short, pausing, and becoming sensitive to the body's life33. One practice involves bringing in qualities like calming or ease with each breath33.... Using a half smile can support this28.... Gently naming the breath, like "in breath" and "out breath," can be helpful, but the focus should be 95% on feeling the direct experience33. Counting breaths can also engage the mind slightly, but the numbers are secondary (5%) to the feeling33. Resting with the breathing and relaxing with it are also mentioned34....

•

Mindfulness of the Body: This involves cultivating awareness in and through the body10.... It can include scanning the body to notice and soften tension27.... Mindful movement practices, like those adapted from Taijiquan focusing on the "Five Fields of the body" (feet, legs, hips/pelvis, torso/arms, neck/head), can help establish mindfulness in the body, provide inner focus, and release tension38.... Paying attention to interoception (what's happening inside the body) and exteroception (what's happening in the space) is part of embodied mindfulness37. The body can also serve as an anchor for presence24....

•

Mindfulness of Feelings/Emotions: This involves paying mindful loving awareness to feelings as they arise10.... Working with difficult emotions or "hard stuff" is an important aspect, involving opening to them with self-compassion, presence, and mindfulness52. Bringing awareness to feelings is described as profoundly liberating21. When working with strong or intense emotions, particularly with trauma, it can be necessary to step back and use strategies to find stability and groundedness53....

•

Mindfulness of Thoughts: This practice is not about getting rid of thoughts but about recognizing when thinking is happening so you are not lost in it10.... It allows you to witness the activity of mind rather than being that activity60. You can observe thoughts with mindful loving awareness61.... Techniques include gently naming thoughts according to their quality (e.g., planning, judging, worrying)61.... The practice helps you step out of the stories thoughts tell and see common patterns without being caught in them, resting instead in mindful loving awareness64.... Thoughts are seen as ephemeral, dissolving like clouds61....

•

Heart Practices: These include practices cultivating qualities like lovingkindness, compassion, joy, equanimity, gratitude, forgiveness, and generosity1.... Self-compassion is specifically mentioned as adding kindness and common humanity to mindfulness52.... Practices can involve offering phrases of care to oneself70. These are seen as aspects of the awakened heart74....

•

RAIN (Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Nurture): Listed as a practice that students learn and are expected to understand, practice, and lead others in18.... It is also a theme for Dharma talks72....

•

Concentration Meditation: This is a major category of meditation aimed at developing the ability to attend fully, focus, and potentially unify with a particular experience1.... It can use various anchors like the breath, body, sound, visual objects, mantras, or koans22.... It often involves repetition69 and is used to settle and focus the mind76.

•

Insight Meditation (Vipassana): Described as being central to the teachings of awakening82.... Its purpose is to strengthen the capacity to experience reality directly ("things as they are")82.... It involves bringing natural and clear attention to whatever arises in the present moment82.... It is also referred to as awareness or mindfulness practice9.

•

Contemplation: A form of meditation involving reflecting on sacred passages, prayers, words, or themes to raise spiritual questions and reflect deeply69.

•

Devotional Practice: Focuses attention on inspiration and love, opening practitioners to prayer, worship, or connecting to something bigger3.... It often involves surrender and can use practices like prayer, song, or visualization69.

•

Pause, Relax, Open: A three-step practice particularly useful for staying present to difficult experiences84.... It involves consciously pausing, relaxing the nervous system (which can include softening the eyes and a slight smile, referred to as "smile yoga"84...), relaxing the throat and belly84..., noticing body pressure84..., and opening to presence84....

•

Grounding: A strategy for managing strong fear or trauma53.... It involves becoming aware of physical contact points, such as feet on the ground, body on the seat, or hands resting53.... It can also involve slowing the breath and feeling the sense of gravity53.... Using the senses to acknowledge "I am here in this moment" is part of it53..., as is touching a pleasant or neutral object53.... Resourcing is also mentioned as a strategy for groundedness with trauma55....

•

Mindfulness in Daily Life: Applying mindfulness to everyday activities24.... This can include using the pause, conscious breathing, the body as an anchor, or engaging mindfully in activities like walking, eating, washing dishes, folding laundry, or drinking tea24....

•

Choiceless Awareness: Mindfulness of whatever is predominant without trying to direct or control it, simply noticing and allowing24.... This can be cultivated as an open-focused awareness87....

•

Mindful Dialogue: A practice for interaction that involves creating a safe, caring container, allowing pauses between speaking, and speaking from one's own experience and heart89....

•

Setting Posture: Many practices begin with establishing a comfortable yet alert posture, often described as spine erect but not stiff27.... Releasing obvious physical tension is also a common starting point27.... Practitioners are often given choices regarding posture (sitting, standing, lying down) and whether to close or open their eyes92....

•

Invitational Language: When guiding others, using language like "I invite you to," "perhaps," or "if it feels comfortable/accessible for you" is suggested to offer choices and acknowledge that experiences can vary96....


## Working with Difficult Emotions

Several methods are detailed for dealing with difficult emotions. A fundamental approach involves meeting difficult emotions with mindfulness and compassion1.... This means being mindfully present with the emotions and holding them within a space of loving awareness and kindness3....

One structured method discussed is the RAIN acronym10...:

•

R - Recognize: Notice what is arising, such as fear, hurt, anger, grief, or judgment22.... It can be helpful to name the emotion gently to yourself, like "sad, sad" or "fear, fear"3.... This helps to acknowledge what is present without being swept away by it4....

•

A - Allow: Agree to "be with" the emotion or experience, allowing it to be present just as it is, without judging or trying to fix it22.... This is the willingness to pause and stay present even when the experience is painful30.... Phrases like "This too," "Yes," or "I can hold this" can support this process30.... Allowing requires gentleness and tenderness30....

•

I - Investigate: This involves inquiring into the felt sense of the experience, primarily focusing on physical sensations in the body rather than just cognitive analysis10.... You can ask yourself questions like "What is happening?", "Where am I feeling this in my body?", "What most wants attention?", "What am I believing?", "What does this vulnerable place want or need from me?"23.... Investigate with curiosity and care, approaching your experience nonjudgmentally32.... You can also notice the interplay between thoughts and emotions50....

•

N - Nurture: This step involves regarding your experience with self-compassion and kindness23. It means offering care to the vulnerable part of you that is having a hard time53.... You can sense your own heart's care56... and send a sincere message of self-compassion directly to the places of fear, unmet needs, or pain59.... This might involve mentally whispering phrases such as "I see how I've caused myself suffering, and I forgive myself now" or simply "Forgiven, forgiven"59.... Other messages of care include "This is hard and I am right here," "May I feel at ease," or "May I remember love right now"62.... Nurturing can also involve calling on a wise and compassionate aspect of yourself or an ally like a friend, pet, teacher, or spiritual figure54.... Placing a hand on your heart can help establish this connection53....

Another important principle is to normalize difficult emotions3.... These emotions are described as natural body-mind energies experienced by all humans36.... Normalizing helps reduce the "second arrow," which is the layer of self-judgment or feeling bad about ourselves for having the difficult emotion in the first place68.... Recognizing that emotions are not personal or a fault helps to release this second layer of suffering68.... Emotions are seen as energies or weather systems that arise and pass14....

Embodied presence is key to working with emotions4.... Locating the emotion in the body allows mindfulness to get closer to the experience, allowing it with loving awareness and feeling it on multiple dimensions4. Breathing with the sensations or relaxing around the tension can also be helpful37....

When emotions or sensations are very strong or overwhelming, particularly with trauma, it is considered skillful and compassionate to step back and resource10.... Resourcing involves shifting attention away from the intense experience to something that brings a sense of balance, safety, or ease64.... This could be a pleasant or neutral area in the body, an external object, an image, message, or memory that evokes comfort or love78.... Engaging in soothing activities like having tea, taking a walk, or connecting with a friend can also serve as resourcing75.... The priority is to evoke love and safety before attempting to bring direct awareness to strong fear or trauma80....

Related to managing intense experiences is titrating and pendulating85.... Titrating means contacting only a little bit of the intense physical or emotional pain at a time, allowing for mindful integration without feeling overwhelmed85.... Pendulating involves moving attention between the difficult sensation and a resource anchor to maintain balance and presence85....

The sources also suggest honoring the intelligence of emotions90.... Emotions are not inherently bad; they are part of our design and can be seen as "nectar" or carrying wisdom90. Even anger has a function and can be a message about an unmet need or a threat to well-being92.... Working with difficult emotions can involve recognizing the message they carry and what needs attention93.

Allowing emotions that have been held inside, such as grief, fear, or rage, to be felt and expressed is also part of the healing process20.... Grief, for example, is described as part of the process of healing the heart and opening to understanding and compassion95....

Finally, exploring the underlying vulnerability beneath strong emotions is mentioned, particularly with anger99. Underneath anger, there is often something tender, a "soft spot" related to what we care about, and sensing this vulnerability can include feeling fear or hurt99. Holding this underlying vulnerability with compassion is a key aspect of self-forgiveness53....

## A Closer Look at Rain

Based on the sources, RAIN is an acronym representing a practice used for navigating difficult emotions and experiences1.... It is described as an easy-to-remember tool for practicing mindfulness and compassion1..., an applied meditation7, and a living pathway of practice that continues to evolve8. RAIN is rooted in the basic teachings of waking up, mindfulness, and heartfulness, applying these to areas of difficulty or "tangles"9. It is also presented as a valuable guideline and tool for teachers to use10....

Here's a breakdown of why RAIN is needed and how it works:

Why We Need the RAIN Practice:

• To relate skillfully with difficult emotions1....

• To address areas where we feel stuck14.

• To de-condition habitual reactive patterns, such as resisting, lashing out, shutting down, or obsessive thinking, which cause suffering15....

• To respond to challenging situations from natural intelligence and compassion15....

• To awaken the two wings of awareness (mindfulness) and compassion (heartfulness)18....

• To empower individuals and deepen their trust in their capacity to wake up through challenges9.

• To help us find our own way home when emotionally stuck, rather than solely depending on external help like therapists22....

• To undo identity and stuckness, revealing who we are beyond the identification with a limited sense of self18....

• To provide a pathway to self-healing when faced with difficulty28.

•To work with specific difficult experiences such as grief, anger related to chronic pain29, fear30, judgment and blame31..., interpersonal conflict34..., and discomfort around race-related issues41....

•It is needed when reactive patterns like anger, defensiveness, fear, or hurt persist.

• It helps us turn inward to find the unmet need beneath challenging emotions33....

•As a general principle, "When in doubt, let it RAIN".

How the RAIN Practice Works:

RAIN involves applying mindfulness and compassion to difficult tangles.... It is typically presented as a four-step process, though these steps can interpenetrate and may unfold spontaneously or over extended periods24....

The standard steps are:

1. R - Recognize what is happening: This involves noticing and acknowledging the difficult emotions, thoughts, or sensations that are arising in the moment1.... It is the mindful noticing wing47. You become aware of the situation and the inner experience it triggers35.... It's important to recognize when you are stuck14....

2. A - Allow life to be just as it is: This is the practice of letting the experience be there without judgment or trying to fix it1.... It involves pausing and making space for whatever is present51.... Allowing requires gentleness and a willingness to stay present with the experience, even if it's painful52.... Phrases like "This too," "Yes," or "I can hold this" can support the allowing52.... Allowing creates the space between stimulus and response, which is where freedom lies54.

3. I - Investigate with kindness or gentleness: This step involves inquiring into the experience with interested and kind attention1.... Crucially, investigation in RAIN is not primarily a cognitive or analytical process52.... It is about directing attention to the somatic, embodied experience – how the difficulty feels in your body52.... You might ask questions like, "How does this feel in my body?"56... or inquire directly with the felt sense, asking, "What does this place most need?"58..., "How does it want me to be with it?"62, or "What are you believing (about yourself or others)?"63.... Investigation contacts reality by unwinding the emotional pain and beliefs and getting in touch with the underlying vulnerability21....

4. N - Nourish with self-compassion (current version) or Non-Identification (older version):

◦ The earlier version used "Non-Identification"1.... This was understood as the gift or freedom that arises after the other steps, not a separate doing.... Without incorporating compassion earlier, this could sometimes feel like a "dry emptiness". 

Generally "dry emptiness" refers to having a purely conceptual or intellectual grasp of what "emptiness" refers to,  which is a way of describing the experience as being empty of any properties you can pin down, or, more likely in this case, the experience of emptyness lacking any compassion.  

◦ The current version uses "Nourish" or "Nurture".... This step involves actively offering kindness, acceptance, forgiveness, compassion, or protection to the vulnerable part of yourself that was contacted during investigation.... This can be done through gentle inner phrases ("I love you," "I'm listening," "It's OK"), touch (like a hand on the heart)..., or imagery.... Getting in touch with vulnerability naturally leads to this tenderness.... Nurturing, or nourishing with self-compassion, helps complete the process of shifting from being identified with the difficult feelings to resting in a more spacious, tender presence....

• After the RAIN: After moving through the steps, there is a phase of resting in the presence and heartspace that has emerged25.... This is where the shift in identity naturally occurs – you realize you are the presence, the tenderness, the loving awareness, rather than the small self caught in difficult emotions25.... Resting here helps you become familiar with this larger sense of being27....

RAIN directly challenges the resistance or reactivity we have towards difficult experiences15.... By intentionally bringing attention (Recognize and Investigate) and kindness/space (Allow and Nurture) to our inner experience, especially the felt sense in the body, we begin to loosen the identification with the difficult emotions and access our natural intelligence and compassion9....

Variations and Considerations:

• A "light RAIN" involves moving gently through the steps, perhaps for less emotionally charged situations19....

• RAIN can be practiced with a partner (RAIN Partners protocol) for mutual support, accountability, intimacy, and shared awakening4....

• RAIN can be applied specifically to conflict, sometimes called "RAIN on Blame," to develop understanding and empathy for oneself and others....

• RAIN may not be the best initial practice for individuals experiencing trauma or great fear; safety and resourcing might be needed first, sometimes for months....

• For depression, activities like exercise or social engagement might be needed before engaging in RAIN to avoid being swamped.

• RAIN might feel "forced" because it interrupts habitual reactions, which is normal. If it feels "artificial," it might mean the practice isn't embodied, and attention needs to be directed to the physical sensations....
