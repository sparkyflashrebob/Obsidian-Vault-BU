### Outline
### Introduction to Mindful of Race and Its Relevance Today

- Current social issues including the criminalization of Black bodies, immigration, Native American rights, domestic terrorism, and misogyny are all framed as belonging issues rooted in race and racism.

- These issues are described as a reflection of a divided heart and mind, representing conditioned patterns of consciousness that demand collective attention.

- Meditation teachers are identified as holding a seat of power, as inviting students to close their eyes and slow down is fundamentally an act of asking for trust and creating a sense of acceptance and belonging.

- Understanding the responsibility of the teacher's seat requires attuning to the invisible racial and social dynamics that participants bring into the meditation space.

- Racial awareness is presented as both timely and timeless, requiring teachers to know their own conditioning, understand their impact, and respond with sensitivity and wisdom.

### The Racial Awareness Rubric and Its First Pair

- A framework called the Racial Awareness Rubric is introduced, consisting of three pairs of experience designed to help teachers examine racial dynamics within themselves and their communities.

- Racial awareness is defined as a life practice and a leadership competency, meaning there is no fixed destination but rather a continuous process of learning, forgiving, and waking up to habits of harm.

- The first pair of the rubric establishes that all people are simultaneously good individuals shaped by personal histories and members of racial group identities shaped by collective conditioning and societal projections.

- Focusing solely on individual identity makes it difficult to recognize the patterns of harm and well-being that operate at the level of racial group membership.

- Gross language such as "white folks" and "people of color" is acknowledged as limited but used intentionally to name existing separations and bring awareness to them rather than to create new divisions.

### Dominant and Subordinated Racial Group Identities

- The second pair of the rubric establishes that not all racial group identities are created equal, as some groups occupy dominant positions while others are subordinated within the current social structure.

- White people as a collective are identified as a dominant racial group, meaning they primarily define social norms, make and influence rules, control resources, and operate from a presumed position of superiority.

- Members of dominant racial groups tend to identify primarily as good individuals rather than as members of a racial group, which results in a reduced sensitivity to the experiences of other racial groups.

- People of color as a collective are identified as a subordinated racial group, meaning they must navigate, assimilate into, or resist dominant structures while fighting for resources and recognition.

- Subordinated group members tend to have extensive knowledge of dominant group members because their survival has historically depended on it, and their mindset ranges from determination to despair depending on the degree of harm experienced.

- Viewing these dynamics through an individual lens makes the patterns invisible, whereas a group identity lens reveals the constellations of dominance and subordination that permeate society.

### Six Hindrances to Racial Harmony

- Six observable behavioral hindrances to racial harmony are introduced as dynamics that emerge from the dance of dominance and subordination and can be recognized both internally and within groups.

- The first hindrance discussed is the "good white individual," which describes the tendency of white people to identify with personal goodness rather than with membership in a white racial group identity.

- A common expression of this hindrance is the statement "I don't see color," which, despite good intentions, effectively erases the historical and collective racial identity of people of color and is experienced as harmful.

- When white people approach conversations from a purely individual lens and people of color approach them from a collective historical lens, the two groups miss each other, resulting in pain and disconnection.

- Addressing this hindrance requires white people to invest in understanding whiteness as a racial group identity by examining what has been collectively conditioned around it, rather than leaving that work undone.

- The second hindrance discussed is described as "stars and constellations," referring to the difference between seeing a single incident in isolation versus recognizing it as part of a broader pattern of harm.

- White individuals often perceive racial incidents as singular events, while people of color tend to perceive them as part of a recurring constellation of systemic harm rooted in historical conditioning.

- Movements such as Black Lives Matter are described as constellation-level responses to systemic patterns, whereas responses like "all lives matter" reflect a star-level, individual view that misses the structural inequality being named.

- Perceptions around race are shaped by deeply conditioned beliefs, and slowing down to examine those perceptions before acting on them is presented as essential to reducing harm.

### Racism at the Institutional Level

- Racism is located primarily at the institutional level, where policies, practices, norms, and assumptions determine who holds power, who is seen, and who defines belonging.

- At the individual level, bias exists in all people, and at the group level, collective discrimination can be observed, but it is at the institutional level that these dynamics become systemic and self-perpetuating.

- Institutions led predominantly by white individuals tend to reflect an unexamined white racial group identity, which becomes normalized as the dominant cultural standard without being consciously acknowledged.

- White privilege proliferates institutionally through a collusive dynamic of blindness, sameness, and silence, where stepping outside the norm of whiteness requires conscious interruption of that collusion.

- Teachers and leaders are encouraged to examine the blind assumptions they carry about their own racial group identity and to question what "out of the norm" actually means in the contexts they inhabit.

### Mindfulness Meditation as a Tool for Racial Awareness

- Mindfulness meditation is identified as one of two structural supports for deconstructing conditioned racial impulses, perceptions, and habits of mind.

- A key power of mindfulness is the shift from being the activity of the mind to witnessing the activity of the mind, which creates space for examining rather than automatically acting on perceptions.

- A perceptual cycle is described in which a perception immediately generates thoughts and emotions, which form a view or belief, which in turn reinforces the original perception in a self-perpetuating loop.

- Racial harm is often driven by unexamined perceptions, as illustrated by the example of a woman calling the police on a nine-year-old Black boy based on a misperception that was later disproven by video evidence.

- Mindfulness practice supports the nervous system in settling enough to allow a different relationship with habitual racial stimulation, creating the conditions for questioning rather than reacting.

### Racial Affinity Groups as a Structural Practice

- Racial affinity groups are introduced as the second structural support, designed to cultivate racial group awareness in a way that complements the individual-level work of mindfulness meditation.

- These groups bring together people of the same race to examine their conditioning, unpack inherited stories, and sit with impulses, numbness, and rage without rushing to fix or resolve them.

- White people in racial affinity groups are encouraged to explore why it feels difficult to engage with whiteness as a group identity, as that difficulty itself is described as the work of whiteness.

- When white people do this internal work within their own groups, people of color are relieved of the burden of educating or managing white discomfort, which contributes to greater collective equanimity.

- People of color are encouraged to gather separately to address internalized oppression, to explore their identity outside of whiteness as a dominant topic, and to examine dynamics of dominance that can arise within communities of color.

- A recommended structure for these groups includes no more than five participants, a commitment to meet monthly for one year, and specific questions and guidelines designed to keep the process safe, internal, and loving.

### Equanimity as a Practice During Times of Urgency

- Equanimity is described not as a permanent state but as a practice cultivated through intention, presence, and a consistent return to the body and breath.

- Developing equanimity requires building a relationship with calm and ease by consciously pausing to notice moments throughout the day when the nervous system is not gripped by urgency or distress.

- Attention to the exhale is offered as a practical tool for supporting the nervous system in recalibrating toward stability and even-handedness.

### Starting Points for White Teachers Addressing Race in Predominantly White Communities

- Forming a racial affinity group is recommended as the primary starting point for white teachers who want to bring conversations about privilege and race into their communities.

- Acknowledging that the work feels scary is validated as appropriate, since engaging with racial group identity requires stepping out of the comfort of individual identity and into vulnerability.

- Scariness is described as a comma rather than a period, meaning it is a signal to pause and care for oneself rather than a reason to stop.

- Giving oneself ten "get out of jail free cards" is suggested as a self-compassion practice, where one side of each card records a misstep and the other side records what was learned from it.

- This work is framed as a long-distance commitment rather than a sprint, requiring sustained engagement, self-care, and the understanding that one is joining a broader generational movement.

### Repairing Harm After a Racial Misstep

- Repairing harm begins with an internal acknowledgment that a rupture has occurred, which is itself an important first step before any external action is taken.

- A common mistake is moving too quickly toward external repair in order to relieve the discomfort of the misstep, which can cut short the deeper internal transformation that the moment is inviting.

- Taking time to care for oneself, understand what happened, and deeply know one's own role in the rupture is described as essential preparation before attempting to repair the relationship externally.

- The distinction between reacting and responding is emphasized, with responding requiring that the nervous system be sufficiently settled to act from clarity rather than from urgency or guilt.

### Building Trust as a Meditation Teacher

- Trust between teacher and student is described as dynamic and influenced by the causes and conditions present in a student's life at any given time.

- One of the most effective ways to build trust is to teach only from lived experience, as authenticity creates a transmission between teacher and student that shaped or performed presentations cannot replicate.

- When teachers speak honestly from their own experience with the teachings they offer, students are supported not only in trusting the teacher but in trusting their own capacity for direct experience.

### A Vision for a Culture of Care

- A culture of care is described as a world where race exists without racism and where all forms of social isms have been dissolved.

- Such a culture would be characterized by authentic self-expression, spontaneity, and the freedom to be one's own authority without the inhibitions imposed by systemic conditioning.

- Greater listening to children and elders, along with an intimate and protective relationship with nature, are named as hallmarks of a healed collective consciousness.

- This vision is framed as a reflection of a well mind, meaning that healing at the individual and collective level would naturally manifest as a more just and caring society.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to form or join racial affinity groups using the guidelines outlined in the book and available on Ruth King's website, committing to a sustained monthly practice over the course of a year.

- Continued engagement with mindfulness meditation is recommended as an ongoing individual practice for examining racial conditioning and interrupting habitual perceptions.

- Teachers are encouraged to deepen their understanding of whiteness and racial group identity as a leadership competency, treating racial awareness as a lifelong learning practice rather than a destination.

- Self-compassion practices such as the "get out of jail free" card exercise are recommended as tools to sustain engagement with this work over the long term.

- Further exploration of the topics covered is directed toward Ruth King's book "Mindful of Race Transforming Racism from the Inside Out" for detailed guidance on racial affinity group structures and how to talk about what disturbs.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Seat of the Meditation Teacher**
- Meditation teachers hold a seat of power by inviting students to slow down, close their eyes, and trust
- When students enter this sacred space, there is a psychological hope for acceptance, understanding, and being held
- Teachers are responsible for attuning to what is in the room, including invisible concerns around race and diversity
- Circles are not always racially diverse, and teachers must understand the dynamics present even when diversity is not visible
**The Racial Awareness Rubric**
- Racial awareness is framed as a life practice and a leadership competency
- It is a learning and awareness competency with no fixed destination
- The rubric has six sides representing three pairs of experience
**First Pair: Individual Identity and Racial Group Identity**
- All people are good individuals shaped by family, loss, trauma, and membership dynamics
- All people are also part of racial group identities conditioned by personal experience and societal projections
- Dominant group members tend to identify primarily as individuals and do not always see themselves as part of a racial group
- Subordinated group members are more likely to be aware of their racial group identity because their lives depend on understanding dominant group members
**Second Pair: Dominant and Subordinated Racial Groups**
- All racial group identities are not created equal
- White people collectively occupy a dominant racial group identity: they define reality and social norms, make and influence rules, control resources, and carry a presumed superiority
- People of color collectively occupy a subordinated racial group identity: they must follow, assimilate, or resist dominant structures, fight for resources, and carry a presumed social norm of inferiority
- The dominant group mindset is one of privilege; the subordinated group mindset ranges from determination to despair
- These dynamics are best seen through a group identity lens rather than an individual lens
**Six Hindrances to Racial Harmony**
- There are at least six observable behavioral hindrances that reflect the dance of dominance and subordination
- Two are discussed in detail:
  - The Good White Individual: White people often identify as good individuals without recognizing their membership in a white racial group identity. This leads to statements such as "I don't see color," which can feel whitewashing to people of color. The work here is for white people to invest in understanding whiteness as a collective racial group identity.
  - Stars and Constellations: White individuals tend to see singular incidents of racial harm (the star), while people of color tend to see patterns of repeated harm (the constellation). This difference in perception creates a disconnect in conversations about race. Black Lives Matter is described as a constellation; All Lives Matter is described as a star.
**Institutional Racism**
- At the individual level, all people carry biases
- At the racial group level, collective discrimination occurs
- At the institutional level, racism lives through policies, practices, norms, assumptions, and power structures
- Unexamined white racial group identity in leadership becomes the proliferated norm in institutions and culture
- White privilege perpetuates itself through a collusive dynamic of blindness, sameness, and silence
**The Perception-Thought-View Cycle**
- When a perception occurs, it immediately generates thoughts and emotions
- Repeated reinforcement forms a view or belief
- That view reinforces the next perception, creating a self-reinforcing cycle
- This cycle operates whether or not one is conscious of it
- Mindfulness meditation allows practitioners to slow down and examine perceptions before acting on them
**Third Pair: Two Structures for Deconstructing Conditioning**
- Mindfulness Meditation: Shifts practitioners from being the activity of the mind to witnessing the activity of the mind. Supports examination of perceptions, slowing of habitual stimulation, and settling of the nervous system.
- Racial Affinity Groups: Small groups of people of the same race who meet regularly to examine their conditioning. Groups of no more than five people, meeting once a month for a year, using specific questions and guidelines. White people use these groups to understand whiteness and step out of the individual mindset into racial group membership. People of color use these groups to examine internalized oppression, define identity outside of whiteness as a dominant topic, and address dominance hierarchies within communities of color.
**Repairing Harm**
- Acknowledging that a rupture has occurred is itself an important repair
- The first step is an internal inquiry to deeply understand the rupture within oneself
- Moving toward external action too quickly can cut short important internal transformation
- Self-care and fortification are necessary before engaging externally
- A chapter in the book Mindful of Race addresses how to talk about what disturbs, with detailed guidance
**Building Trust as a Teacher**
- Teaching only from lived experience builds trust
- Honesty and transmission of lived truth support attunement between teacher and student
- When teachers speak their truth, students are better able to trust themselves and their own experience
**Equanimity as Practice**
- Equanimity is a practice rooted in intention, not a permanent state
- It involves staying present, keeping attention on the body and breath, and attending to the exhale
- Practitioners are encouraged to notice moments of ease throughout the day and allow the nervous system to recalibrate
- Equanimity can often be recognized in retrospect as a cultivation that has been occurring

### Stories
**The Wristband on the Plane**
Ruth King shares that she wears a wristband that reads "Mindful of Race, Not There Yet." On a plane, a flight attendant saw the wristband and her bald head and perceived that Ruth had cancer, offering encouragement about her health. Ruth used this as an illustration of the perception-thought-view cycle: the attendant perceived the wristband and bald head, formed a thought and emotion based on prior memory, developed a view that Ruth had cancer, and acted on it without checking the perception.
**Barack Avenue in Charlottesville**
Ruth King shares that after conducting a Mindful of Race training in Charlottesville, she was driven to the airport and saw a street sign reading "Barack Avenue." She immediately perceived it as a street named after Barack Obama, felt pride, and began forming an elaborate story about Charlottesville being a progressive city. Her driver clarified that locals call it "Barracks Avenue." Ruth used this as a humorous and direct illustration of the perception-thought-view cycle operating rapidly and without examination.
**Michael Brown and the Ferguson Discussion Group**
Ruth King describes a gathering in Charlotte shortly after the killing of 18-year-old Michael Brown by a 28-year-old white police officer in Ferguson, Missouri. A white man in the group, approximately 44 years old, was visibly shaken and spoke about the singular incident as something that should never have happened. Ruth, also shaken, spoke about a constellation of repeated killings of unarmed Black men by police officers. This story illustrates the stars-versus-constellations hindrance and how differently dominant and subordinated group members perceive and narrate the same event.
**The Brooklyn Deli Incident**
Ruth King references a widely reported incident in which a 53-year-old white woman called 911 outside a Brooklyn deli, accusing a nine-year-old Black boy of rubbing her behind. Video footage showed it was the child's backpack that had brushed against her. She apologized to the child but maintained righteous indignation toward the mother. Ruth used this as an example of unchecked perception leading to harmful action and the need to slow down and question racial conditioning.
**The Dalai Lama and the Outraged White Man**
Ruth King shares a story in which a white man at a Dalai Lama talk stood up and expressed outrage about what was happening in Tibet, saying he wanted to come and help. The Dalai Lama responded by telling him to take care of his own heart first, then come. Ruth offered this as a model for how to approach the impulse to repair racial harm externally before doing the necessary internal work.

- Ruth King acknowledges that her own practice is changing significantly given the current times
- She describes moments of feeling challenged to love everything that is happening
- She describes her heart quaking to the point where she sometimes distrusts the practice and doubts whether compassion is all it is said to be
- She describes returning to memories of being deeply loved as a way to reground when doubt arises
- She describes feeling incredibly vulnerable and doubtful at times
- She returns repeatedly to the practice of recognizing aspects of herself in others, especially when gripped, as a humbling and stabilizing practice
- Equanimity is noted as difficult when the nervous system is highly activated, with some practitioners not trusting moments of peace because the system is so wired
- The nervous system is described as needing recalibration through intentional attention to moments of ease throughout the day
