### Outline
### Introduction to East Bay Meditation Center

- Founded ten years prior, the East Bay Meditation Center (EBMC) is located in downtown Oakland and is celebrating its tenth anniversary.

- Hosting approximately 150 to 200 people, the center operates seven weekly practice groups and around 80 classes and retreats per year.

- All programming runs on an all-dana gift economics model, meaning no fees are charged for classes.

- An estimated 17,000 individual visits are recorded at the center each year, reflecting consistent and growing community engagement.

### Lessons Learned from the Spanish-Language Sangha Initiative

- An early attempt to launch a Spanish-language sangha illustrated the risks of committing to programming without adequate foundational support.

- A highly committed native Spanish-speaking teacher, with an immigrant family background, proposed and began leading a regular sangha that met twice monthly with approximately twelve consistent attendees.

- Expansion into half-day and full-day retreats revealed critical gaps, including the absence of fully bilingual volunteers for greeting, bilingual signage, and outreach capacity into the Spanish-speaking Oakland community.

- Inability to meet those operational needs led to the teacher relocating the group elsewhere, which was acknowledged as a fair and instructive outcome.

- Key lessons drawn from this experience included the understanding that a single champion, regardless of commitment, cannot sustain a program without broader structural support.

- Building programming without sufficient scoping, resources, and staffing was identified as attempting to construct a house on an unstable foundation.

### The Concept of Beloved Community

- A guiding framework for EBMC's approach to diversity is drawn from bell hooks, who defined beloved community as formed not by the eradication of difference but by its affirmation.

- Claiming all identities and cultural legacies that shape how individuals live in the world is central to this vision.

- Rather than focusing solely on diversity and inclusion, the center's goal is to affirm with positive joy the full spectrum of who people are.

- Structural and foundational intentionality is emphasized as the means by which a genuinely welcoming community can be built.

### Guided Visualization of an Inclusive Center

- Participants were invited into an imaginative exercise to envision what entering a truly welcoming center would feel, look, smell, and sound like.

- Visual elements in the imagined space included posters featuring a diverse range of teachers, classes offered at varied times including weekends, and an altar with different statues and personal photographs.

- Physical accessibility was represented through wheelchair-accessible pathways, gender-neutral bathroom signage, and kitchen facilities designed at waist level for ease of reach.

- Sensory inclusivity was reflected in the availability of clean, filtered air for those with asthma or chemical sensitivities, as well as the absence of overwhelming fragrances.

- Emotional and social welcome was conveyed through a smiling greeter, the sounds of laughter, the smell of food from a potluck, and a low-light compassion room for those needing quieter space.

- Christine identified this visualization as closely reflecting her own first experience entering EBMC, offered as an example of what intentional welcome can produce in practice.

### Core Principles for Building an Inclusive Organization

- Intentionality is identified as a foundational requirement, with the understanding that diversity work must be structural rather than cosmetic.

- Cultural humility and active listening are described as ongoing practices at the center, with an emphasis on asking first rather than assuming community needs.

- Honest assessment of organizational capacity before making commitments is presented as essential to avoiding the failures illustrated by the Spanish-language sangha story.

- Gift economics is framed not merely as a dana practice but as a critical mechanism for economic access and radical inclusivity.

- Diversity and inclusion work is described as an ongoing practice and way of living rather than a destination to be reached.

### Leadership Demographics and Teacher Diversity Policies

- Ensuring that the demographic composition of leadership visibly reflects the communities an organization seeks to serve is presented as a foundational strategy.

- Having more than 50 percent of leadership composed of people of color is recommended when that community is a primary priority, acknowledging that this takes time and patience.

- EBMC maintains a teacher sangha of nearly 100 teachers and works to ensure that group is demographically reflective across race, ethnicity, gender identification, sexual orientation, physical ability, and age.

- A policy requires that white teachers co-teach day-longs, retreats, and class series alongside a teacher of color, with exceptions made only for six core teachers who have demonstrated sustained commitment as allies.

- Board diversity is supported through the provision of stipends for board members, counteracting the class bias inherent in unpaid volunteer governance roles.

### Programming Design for Inclusive Community Building

- EBMC's first practice group was intentionally a people of color sangha, followed by an LGBTQI and two-spirit community group known as the Alphabet Sangha, reflecting a deliberate sequencing to create safe entry points.

- Beginning meditation series specifically for people of color were among the first classes offered, designed to address underrepresentation in Western convert Buddhist spaces.

- Current weekly groups include Everybody Every Mind for people living with disability and chronic illness, a White and Awakening Sangha for those exploring allyship through a dharma lens, a social justice lunchtime sangha, a multiracial deep refuge group, and Alphabet Brothers of Color.

- Groups are created in direct response to community input about what spaces are needed for members to feel whole within the sangha.

- A registration policy sets aside 40 percent of class spots for people of color across all programming, acknowledged as logistically demanding but prioritized as a values-driven commitment.

- Curriculum development integrates social justice themes, including a program exploring the Eightfold Path through a social justice lens and a bodhisattva path curriculum designed to meet community members' desire to serve.

### Operational Practices Supporting Accessibility and Inclusion

- Operations are described as the primary way people actually experience a space, making them central to the mission rather than peripheral.

- A strict fragrance-free policy is enforced to protect community members with asthma and multiple chemical sensitivities, with volunteers and event coordinators trained to compassionately address violations.

- Physical accessibility is treated as non-negotiable, illustrated by the access committee's refusal to accept a second-story space during a period of crisis relocation, ultimately leading to the discovery of a fully accessible venue.

- All teachers and speakers are required to use a microphone regardless of room size, and all written materials are produced in a minimum of 14-point font.

- Multicultural interaction agreements are displayed prominently on full walls in every room, serving as a visible reference point for teachers and community members during conflict or discomfort.

- Trauma-informed language is identified as a growing edge for the center, with community members actively contributing to its development.

### Gift Economics as a Foundation for Access

- EBMC operates entirely on a no-fee, all-dana model with the exception of one to two fundraising events per year, grounding economic access in the center's core values.

- Dana is defined as a Pali word meaning the generosity that arises naturally from deep gratitude, and is understood as essential to spiritual liberation in Buddhist teaching.

- Contributions to the center extend beyond financial donations to include skills and time, with volunteers managing temple keeping, technology, website development, and database administration.

- Community contribution through the gift economics model is seen as strengthening the sangha by ensuring that all members feel they are participating in sustaining it.

### Trauma-Informed and Trauma-Sensitive Language in Teaching

- Concrete language is recommended as a way to maintain focus and reduce the risk of re-triggering participants, replacing evocative or metaphorical instructions with clear, grounded directions such as "press down evenly with both feet" rather than "root toward the center of the earth."

- Positive language is emphasized because the body and brain respond to negatively framed instructions with an automatic barrier, making it harder to learn; replacing "don't scrunch your fingers" with "spread your fingers wide" is offered as an example.

- Invitational language is presented as essential for participants who have experienced trauma and may be triggered by directive instructions, using phrases such as "if it feels comfortable" or "if you have access to this" to allow individuals to proceed at their own pace.

- Encouraging language focuses on affirming exploration rather than foreshadowing difficulty, replacing phrases like "this might be hard for some of you" with invitations to "explore your edges."

- Meaningful affirmations are described as having lasting impact on practitioners, particularly beginners, when they are specific and grounded in observed behavior rather than generic praise.

- Exploratory language supports curiosity and self-awareness by leaving space for individual experience, such as replacing "your right arm should feel light" with "notice how your right arm feels."

- Neutral language removes embedded judgment from instructions, with the example of replacing "you can just keep your knees down" with "you can keep your knees down or raise them off the mat, it's your choice."

- Individual words and tones, not only full phrases, can serve as triggers for people with PTSD, making careful word selection a meaningful act of care in teaching environments.

### Managing Conflict and Cultural Conditioning in Diverse Communities

- Diversity of thought is acknowledged as a source of friction within communities, and developing the capacity to make conflict productive is identified as essential to organizational growth.

- Investing in a skilled neutral facilitator is recommended as a high-value use of resources during periods of growth, rough patches, or cultural transition.

- Building personal capacity to tolerate discomfort when engaging across cultural differences is described as a practice that makes diversity work sustainable, joyful, and productive.

- Avoiding the tendency to take cultural conditioning personally is offered as a key disposition for those engaged in long-term inclusion work.

### Next Steps

- EBMC intends to continue developing its trauma-informed and trauma-sensitive language practices, with ongoing input from community members.

- The center plans to maintain and deepen its structural commitments to demographic representation in leadership, teacher diversity policies, and accessible programming.

- Continued growth of the gift economics model and community contribution structures is planned as a means of sustaining both financial viability and inclusive access.

- Participants are encouraged to explore EBMC's website for further resources on the five principles of gift economics and other foundational practices shared during the presentation.

**Trauma-Informed and Trauma-Sensitive Language**
- Christine drew on her personal experience as a survivor of PTSD, rape, and assault, and from a year-long program called Practice in Transformative Action led by Mushim Ikeda at EBMC
- Practices were sourced from Naropa Institute's mindful movement work
- Six language principles were presented:
  - Concrete language: Use clear, direct physical cues rather than abstract imagery to avoid re-triggering
  - Positive language: Frame instructions in the affirmative rather than beginning with "don't" or "no," as the body learns on "yes"
  - Invitational language: Use phrases like "if it feels comfortable" or "if you have access to this" to allow participants to proceed at their own pace
  - Encouraging language: Replace foreshadowing of failure with exploratory affirmations such as "explore your edges"
  - Meaningful affirmations: Offer specific, grounded affirmations rather than surface-level compliments
  - Neutral language: Avoid embedded judgment in word choice; present options without implying a correct or preferred choice
**Conflict, Facilitation, and Cultural Conditioning**

- Budgeting for a skilled neutral facilitator is recommended for navigating rough patches
- Building capacity to tolerate discomfort when engaging with different cultures is emphasized as an ongoing practice
