### Outline
### Opening Guided Meditation Practice

- Participants were invited to find a comfortable position in their bodies, whether sitting, standing, or lying down.

- Eyes could remain open or closed based on personal preference, with an instruction to allow the gaze to soften rather than focus sharply.

- Deep breaths were taken through the nose and released, allowing the body to relax without forcing away any tension or pain.

- Awareness was directed toward the breath, with thoughts and feelings acknowledged but released rather than engaged with.

- A sense of openness and relaxed awareness was cultivated and extended toward the community gathered for the session.

### The Cultural Roots of Meditation and Its Transformation in the West

- Meditation and mindfulness practices originated in cultural contexts such as India, Japan, Thailand, and indigenous communities, where they were deeply embedded in communal life and ritual.

- These practices were not performed in isolation but were supported by larger communities and carried a deeply ethical foundation.

- As these traditions migrated to the West, a significant metamorphosis occurred in which the communal and ethical dimensions were gradually stripped away.

- A notable conceptual shift took place from the Eastern notion of no-self-realization, rooted in the Buddhist understanding of impermanence, to a Western reframing of self-realization centered on the individual.

- The dominant cultural expression that absorbed these practices in the West was individualism, which became the invisible framework through which mindfulness was interpreted and practiced.

- This shift mirrored the Protestant Reformation's move away from communal religious practice toward a private, direct relationship with the divine, which elevated privacy as a spiritual and cultural value.

### Radical Individualism as the Ideology of Whiteness

- Radical individualism, particularly as articulated by political philosopher Thomas Hobbes, is identified as the ideological foundation of whiteness as a social construct.

- This ideology is not defined by phenotype but by a set of beliefs centered on separation, superiority, and control, and can be adopted by people of any racial background.

- Multiple forms of separation underpin this ideology, including the separation of humans from the divine through secularism, humans from nature through the doctrine of dominion, one people from another through colonialism, and the mind from the body through Cartesian philosophy.

- Collective orientations among Native Americans, African communities, and women were historically used as justifications for denying them full legal personhood and rights in the United States.

- Popular cultural figures such as John Wayne and the Borg collective in Star Trek illustrate how radical individualism is celebrated and collectivism is portrayed as threatening or inferior.

- Fear and the drive to dominate are presented as consequences of this ideology of separation, manifesting in the need to control anything perceived as mysterious, uncontrollable, or other.

- The Enlightenment's promise to eliminate all unknowing through reason is described as a form of hubris that sought to colonize the world and bend it to human will.

### Othering, Inclusion, and the Concept of Belonging

- Othering is defined as the process of marking certain people as less than fully human based on characteristics such as religion, sexuality, culture, or ethnicity, leading to their exclusion, control, or elimination.

- Inclusion is distinguished from belonging in that inclusion typically involves being invited into an existing structure under conditions set by those already in power, placing the burden of adaptation entirely on the newcomer.

- Belonging, by contrast, is predicated on the idea that people are welcomed with their full selves, not a version of themselves that conforms to a dominant norm.

- A key principle of belonging is co-creation, meaning that the presence of each person actually changes and shapes the institution or community they are part of.

- Co-creation requires agency, a sense of self, power, capacity, love, and a shared sense of responsibility for the collective enterprise.

- The concept of saming, or asserting that everyone is the same and differences should be ignored, is rejected as a false solution to othering because it erases meaningful human differences and projects a dominant norm onto all people.

- James Baldwin's experience of being excluded from a literary society unless he concealed his Black social circle and his homosexuality illustrates the conditional and costly nature of inclusion, captured in his phrase "the price of the ticket."

- Baldwin's statement that he felt he belonged nowhere, in contrast to Margaret Mead's sense of belonging everywhere, highlights the structural and racialized nature of belonging rather than it being merely a personal feeling.

### The Limitations of Individualism in Mindfulness Practice

- Much of how mindfulness has been practiced in the United States has been repurposed around isolated individualism, with the focus placed on inner feelings as the primary source of truth and transformation.

- Leonard Cohen's lyric "I don't trust my inner feelings, inner feelings come and go" is cited to challenge the assumption that inner experience is a stable or sufficient foundation for practice.

- Political philosopher John Rawls argued that individual wants are derivative of culture and therefore cannot serve as the foundation for justice, pointing instead to institutional arrangements as the proper lens for evaluating a just society.

- Economist Amartya Sen similarly emphasized the importance of structural conditions over individual preferences in understanding fairness and human flourishing.

- Systems thinking is invoked to challenge the reductionist assumption that society can be understood by examining only its individual parts, since the whole of any system has its own dynamic that cannot be reduced to its constituents.

- Buddhist teacher David Loy is cited as acknowledging that Buddhism, including the Buddha's own teachings, never sufficiently addressed institutions, a gap that American individualism has exploited at an unconscious level.

- Structural and institutional blindness is identified as a significant limitation of individualist approaches to mindfulness, preventing practitioners from seeing how systems shape experience and perpetuate inequality.

### Belonging in the Context of Multiple Pandemics and Social Disconnection

- The convergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, racial injustice, and climate change is described as a moment of multiple simultaneous crises requiring collective rather than individual responses.

- Disparate COVID-19 infection and death rates among Black, Latino, and Native American communities are presented as evidence that people are not similarly situated and that sameness-based responses cause harm.

- The term "social distancing" is critiqued as a misnomer that reinforced individualism, with the preferred framing being "physical distancing and social solidarity" to better reflect the need for creative communal connection.

- Young people in particular were harmed by the framing of isolation as a purely individual problem to solve, rather than a collective challenge requiring institutional creativity and support.

- The example of East African communities, particularly in Tanzania, with low COVID-19 spread despite poor sanitation and dense living conditions, is raised as a potential source of learning about communal resilience.

- Darwin's concept of survival of the fittest is reinterpreted not as survival of the strongest but as survival of those who best fit their environment, suggesting that human adaptability must now include learning to live collectively with each other and the planet.

- The European Union is offered as an imperfect but instructive example of formerly warring nations choosing to construct a shared identity while retaining distinct ones, resulting in over 75 years without major war in Western Europe.

### The Role of Engagement, Love, and Structural Change

- Engagement rather than happiness or health is proposed as the highest aspiration one could wish for, because deep engagement inherently places people in relationship with each other and with the world.

- The Bodhisattva ideal is invoked to argue that individual salvation is neither possible nor legitimate while others suffer, framing social justice work as inseparable from spiritual practice.

- Love is affirmed as essential but insufficient on its own, since power dynamics embedded in patriarchal and racialized structures can seep into even loving relationships if those structures are not addressed.

- Correct relation, not merely relation, is identified as the goal, requiring both personal transformation and structural change to redistribute power equitably.

- New selves are described as emerging through the process of creating new structures and possibilities, suggesting that individual and collective transformation are mutually constitutive.

- The tension within progressive spiritual communities around race and identity is addressed by cautioning against the trap of essentializing either the individual or the collective, and against using transcendence as a way to bypass engagement with structural realities.

- Freedom as understood in the United States is described as already racialized, shaped by the history of slavery and white ideology, meaning that spiritual concepts of freedom cannot be treated as neutral or universal.

### Next Steps

- Mindfulness teachers are encouraged to seek out communities different from their own, including Black, Native American, and other marginalized communities, and to approach those communities as students rather than only as teachers.

- Practitioners are invited to face structural and systemic realities rather than retreat into individualism, and to develop literacy around how institutions shape experience and perpetuate inequality.

- Communities and organizations are encouraged to find creative and safe ways to come together physically, treating the harm of prolonged isolation as a real injury that requires active collective redress.

- The broader project proposed is one of co-creating new institutions, identities, and ways of living together that ensure no person or life form is left outside the circle of human concern.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Transformation of Eastern Spiritual Practices in the West**
- Dr. Powell observed that practices such as yoga, meditation, and other contemplative traditions were deeply embedded in community, ritual, and ethical frameworks in their cultures of origin — India, Japan, Thailand, Africa, and Indigenous communities
- As these practices migrated to the West, they underwent a metamorphosis, becoming detached from communal and cultural roots
- The shift moved from a Buddhist emphasis on "no-self" or non-self-realization to a Western emphasis on "self-realization"
- Western practice became embedded in the cultural expression of individualism, which Powell identifies as so pervasive it is nearly invisible
**Radical Individualism and the Ideology of Whiteness**
- Powell traces radical individualism to the political philosophy of Thomas Hobbes
- He identifies this radical individualism — the total separation from all others — as the essence of the ideology of whiteness
- This ideology is not about phenotype; it is an ideology that can be embraced by anyone regardless of race
- The ideology is characterized by multiple separations: humans from the divine (secularism), humans from nature (dominion/domination), one people from another (colonialism), and mind from body (Cartesian dualism)
- These separations produce both fear of and the need to dominate what is "other" — nature, non-Western peoples, women, the body
- Historical examples cited: land taken from Native Americans partly because they held land collectively; women denied full citizenship because they could not stand as full individuals; the Dred Scott case denying Black Americans the right to belong
**Othering**
- Othering is the process of marking a person or group as less than fully human, as scary, as needing to be controlled, excluded, or even eliminated
- Reasons for othering include: manner of worship, sexual orientation, culture, food, ethnicity, gender
- The Dred Scott Supreme Court decision is cited as a legal codification of othering — ruling that Black people could never belong in the United States regardless of their freedom status
**Inclusion vs. Saming vs. Belonging**
- Inclusion: being invited into someone else's club under their conditions; all the work of fitting in falls on the person being included; guests must follow the host's rules or leave
- Saming: the liberal response to othering — asserting "we are all the same" — which Powell rejects as inadequate and inaccurate; it erases real differences and projects one group's circumstances onto all others
- Belonging: people are welcomed with their full selves, not a version of someone else; the institution or community is co-created by all members; presence changes the community; requires agency, power, capacity, love, and responsibility
**The Price of the Ticket — James Baldwin**
- Baldwin's experience of being invited into a prestigious literary society but told to keep his Black friends away and never mention his homosexuality illustrates the cost of conditional inclusion
- Baldwin left for France, heartbroken; his book "The Price of the Ticket" names this cost
- Baldwin's exchange with Margaret Mead illustrates the asymmetry of belonging — Mead felt she belonged everywhere; Baldwin felt he belonged nowhere
**Institutions, Systems, and the Limits of Individualism**
- Much mindfulness practice in the United States ignores institutions and systems, or reduces them to collections of individuals
- Systems thinkers assert the whole is greater than the sum of its parts and cannot be understood by examining only constituent parts
- John Rawls argued that individual wants are derivative of culture and cannot be the foundation of justice; institutional arrangements must be examined
- Amartya Sen similarly emphasizes structural and institutional dimensions of justice
- David Loy, a Zen Buddhist teacher and friend of Powell's, notes that the Buddha never sufficiently addressed institutions
- American individualism operates at an unconscious level and produces institutional blindness
**Inner Feelings as Insufficient Foundation**
- Powell challenges the mindfulness assumption that deep inner feelings are universal or foundational
- He cites Leonard Cohen: "I don't trust my inner feelings / Inner feelings come and go"
- Rawls: wants are derivative of culture, not foundational
- The claim that "we all suffer" at a deep level is questioned as a basis for justice or social transformation
**The Multiple Pandemics and Disproportionate Impact**
- COVID-19 death and infection rates were several times higher in Black, Latino, and Native American communities
- Native communities were told to wash hands frequently despite lacking access to water
- Climate change is literally burning communities in California and Colorado
- The nation-state is not designed to address globalization or climate change
- Pollution and environmental harm do not respect national borders
**Turning Toward Each Other**
- Powell invokes Jack Kornfield's framing: turning toward each other rather than on each other
- Acknowledging that people are not similarly situated is a prerequisite for genuine solidarity
- The concept of "no one outside the circle of human concern" — extending to all humans and all life
- The European Union experiment: former warring nations creating a shared European identity over 75 years without major war in Western Europe
- Einstein's warning about World War III and IV cited as context for the urgency of cooperation
**Social Distancing vs. Physical Distancing**
- Powell argues the term "social distancing" was a mistake; it should have been called "physical distancing with social solidarity"
- Young people are at the height of their socializing process; telling them to socially distance is like telling people not to drink water
- Not being with each other is described as an injury and a horror
- Creative, safe ways to come together physically and socially need to be developed collectively, not left to individual devices
**Engagement as the Highest Aspiration**
- Powell states that if he could wish one thing for his children, it would not be happiness or health but engagement
- Deep engagement is inherently relational — one cannot be deeply engaged alone
- The Bodhisattva ideal: one cannot save oneself while the world burns; individual salvation is not a legitimate call
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**On the Nature of Self and Other**
- Judith Butler's "The Force of Nonviolence" is cited: the assertion "I have a right to protect myself against the other" contains no examination of who the self or the other actually is
- Much spiritual practice acknowledges profound interconnection, making the self/other binary complicated — yet Western society resolves this complication quickly and without examination
- The Buddhist concept of no permanent self was transformed in the West into self-realization, reflecting the cultural dominance of individualism
**On Race as Ideology, Not Phenotype**
- James Baldwin: "There's no hope for them or us as long as they think they're white" — referring to the ideology of superiority, separation, and control, not skin color
- The ideology of whiteness can be practiced by anyone; not every phenotypically white person fully embraces it
- People of color must also be careful not to essentialize race or gender as fixed identities, even while acknowledging their social construction
**On Freedom and Its Racialization**
- Orlando Patterson's work is cited: the concept of freedom in the United States is already racialized, already embedded in white ideology
- The way freedom is conceived in American culture is a reaction to slavery and carries that history
**On Spiritual Practice and Social Justice**
- Powell has maintained both a spiritual/meditation practice since the mid-1960s and a lifelong commitment to social justice work
- His book "Racing to Justice" includes a chapter called "Lessons from Suffering," noting that many spiritual practices are animated by concern for suffering
- Spiritual practice helped Powell channel anger that was otherwise self-injuring
- The invitation to mindfulness teachers: go be with other communities and be their student — East Oakland, Native American reservations — and partake of other people's gifts
**On Correct Relationship**
- Love alone is not enough; we must be correctly related
- Even loving relationships are subject to power dynamics — e.g., patriarchal norms seeping into marriages, with 80% of childcare during COVID falling on women
- Structural and cultural forces shape relationships regardless of individual intentions
**On Uncertainty and Not-Knowing**
- We are in a time of deep not-knowing — institutions are changing, nation-states are inadequate, the future is unclear
- The Enlightenment's promise to dispel all darkness and know everything is identified as hubris
- Mystery is no longer afforded in a culture that seeks to colonize and control everything unknown
- Powell references his own uncertainty about why he was invited to speak, and frames uncertainty as something to sit with
**On Learning from Other Communities**
- The example of Tanzania and East Africa: despite poverty, poor sanitation, and lack of computers, COVID spread remained low — Powell suggests there may be something to learn from how those communities live together
- Darwin's "survival of the fittest" reframed as survival of those who fit best with their environment — relevant to humanity's relationship with a changing planet

### Stories
**The Ohio State University Car Rental Policy**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell about his own experience. When teaching at Ohio State, a university rule required all staff to rent subcompact cars regardless of physical stature. Powell, over six feet tall, objected. The administration's response was that everyone is treated the same. Powell noted the person who made the rule was likely under six feet. The matter escalated to the chancellor, who granted Powell special dispensation to rent a full-size car — which then led colleagues to remark that he thought he was special. Used to illustrate the failure of "saming" as a response to real human difference.
**James Baldwin and the Literary Society**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell about James Baldwin. At the height of his literary career, Baldwin was invited to a prestigious literary society — something he deeply wanted. Upon acceptance, he was told his Black friends were not welcome at the facilities and that his homosexuality should never be mentioned. Baldwin left the United States for France, heartbroken. His book "The Price of the Ticket" names the cost of conditional belonging.
**James Baldwin and Margaret Mead**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell. In a conversation between Baldwin and Margaret Mead, Mead stated she belonged everywhere — she was British, educated, rich, and famous. When she asked Baldwin the same question, he replied: "Nowhere." Used to illustrate the asymmetry of belonging and the invisibility of exclusion to those who are included.
**Harry Belafonte and "Ibid"**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell about Harry Belafonte. Belafonte did not learn to read until he was in the army. Upon becoming a reader, he encountered the footnote citation "Ibid" repeatedly and assumed it referred to a prolific and important author. After leaving the military, he went to a library and asked a white female librarian for all books by "Ibbott." She said she didn't know the author. Belafonte accused her of lying, calling her a white racist. He later discovered "Ibid" was not a person but a Latin citation abbreviation. He sought out the woman to apologize, recognizing he had projected his own anger and injured someone in the process. Used to illustrate the harm of misdirected anger and the importance of accountability.
**John Powell's Father's Words During a Moment of Despondency**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell about his own experience. While feeling exhausted and overwhelmed by his work, Powell's father — a Christian minister — asked what was wrong. Powell said the work was too much to do alone. His father responded: "You never are by yourself. That's the illusion." Used to address the feeling of overwhelm among new teachers and practitioners.
**Powell's Response to Early COVID Mortality Projections**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell about his own experience. When early COVID projections suggested older Black men with pre-existing conditions were at very high risk of death, someone implied Powell should prepare for the worst. Powell said he was at peace with his own death given the life he had lived, but was not at peace with his granddaughter not having a future. Used to illustrate the shift from self-concern to collective concern as a source of purpose and ease.
**The European Union as an Experiment in Shared Identity**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell as a historical example. Europe experienced three major wars in sixty years — the Bismarck era, World War I, and World War II — each more deadly than the last. Einstein warned that he did not know what weapons would be used in World War III but that World War IV would be fought with sticks and stones. European nations, after centuries of conflict, chose to create a new shared identity — European — while retaining separate national identities. For 75 years there has been no major war in Western Europe. Used to illustrate the possibility of creating new collective identities across historical division.
**John Wayne as Symbol of Radical Individualism**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell as a cultural observation. Powell notes that in old John Wayne films, Wayne never kissed a baby, never had a romantic relationship, rode into town alone, shot up the place, and left alone. Used to illustrate how radical individualism is embedded in American popular culture and smuggled into mindfulness practice.
**Star Trek and the Borgs**
Shared by Dr. John A. Powell as a cultural observation. In Star Trek, collectivism is represented by the Borgs — portrayed as threatening and alien — while Captain Kirk embodies radical individualism. Powell notes he has nine siblings and is sixth of nine, humorously connecting himself to the Borg designation "Six of Nine." Used to illustrate how American popular culture frames collectivism as dangerous and individualism as heroic.
