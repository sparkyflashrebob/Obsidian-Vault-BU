### Key Points, Subjects and Themes
### Outline
### Opening Meditation and Intention Setting

- Participants are guided to close their eyes, take full breaths, and bring attention to the heart area to collect presence and stillness.

- Awareness of the breath moving in and out of the heart is used as an anchor to sense the current quality of inner presence.

- Inward listening is directed toward identifying personal intention or aspiration as participants enter the shared retreat space.

- A collective aspiration toward awakening heart-mind and observing that awakening in others is invited and widened to encompass the whole group.

- The chanting of OM three times is introduced as a sound kernel of connectedness, linking individual bodies, hearts, and minds to each other and all beings.

### Reading from the Poet Donna Falls on Presence and Impermanence

- A poetic reading emphasizes living each moment fully rather than allowing time to slip away unnoticed.

- Time is described as precious and disappearing like water into sand unless deliberate attention is chosen.

- The reader is urged to treat each day as if it were the last, bringing compassion to oneself and openness and love to others.

- All activities, including writing, relating, eating, and ordinary tasks, are encouraged to be experienced as if for the first time, with nothing dismissed as unimportant.

- Living in this way is described as leading to richness beyond measure, gratitude beyond words, and arriving at death without regrets.

### The Role of Ritual in Teaching and Practice

- The facilitator describes beginning every Wednesday night class, regardless of its secular or interfaith audience, with a reflection on heart intention and the chanting of Aum with palms together.

- Participants are told that many students report the chant as surprisingly welcoming rather than exotic, finding it liberating and engaging rather than unfamiliar.

- Chanting is identified as a practice that moves participants out of thought patterns and egoic mind states, creating receptivity to something more mysterious, transcendent, and full of possibility.

- Attendees are invited to reflect on whether they incorporate a chant like Aum or a formal ritual in their own teaching or personal practice.

- A humorous cartoon anecdote is shared in which two cows observe a meditating holy man and suggest that the sound should begin with "moo," illustrating the lighthearted side of ritual practice.

### Developmental Framework from Egoic Self to Larger Beingness

- A developmental frame is presented in which human beings are understood to be emerging from an egoic self defined by incessant inner dialogue, self-centeredness, and the sense of being the agent of all events.

- This unfolding is described as an opening into a larger sense of beingness that includes but is not limited by the ego's thoughts and behaviors.

- Development in this framework does not transcend or eliminate earlier stages but rather integrates them, just as the brain retains its reptilian and limbic structures while developing higher capacities.

- Signs of regression into a smaller identity include tightening, comparing mind, judging, and falling into the trance of feeling not okay or not enough.

- A story from Anthony de Mello is shared about a father waking his 45-year-old son who is also the school headmaster, illustrating how easily one can slip from a position of authority into the trance of resistance and self-doubt.

### Two Pathways of Awakening and the Practice of Taking Refuge

- Two complementary pathways of waking up are identified as recognizing and allowing old conditioning by shining light on craving, judgment, and resistance, and intentionally resourcing by turning toward what reconnects and reminds.

- Taking refuge is introduced as the ultimate form of resourcing, drawing on the traditional Buddhist language of refuge in Buddha as awareness, Dharma as the path and practices of presence, and Sangha as the relational field.

- The archetypal myth of the Buddha sitting through the night under the tree of awakening is presented as the foundational illustration of both practices, holding all that arises with compassion until it transforms.

- Mara's final challenge of doubt and comparing mind is described as the last fetter, representing the deep core belief that something is wrong or that one should be different.

- Siddhartha's act of reaching out to touch the ground and calling on the earth goddess is interpreted as an act of taking refuge in Buddha nature, resourcing himself by turning toward something larger in the moment of greatest doubt.

- This gesture of touching the ground is connected to Rumi's teaching that turning toward what you love saves you, affirming that both radical presence and intentional resourcing are essential practices.

### The Myth of Shedding Skin and Taking the Exquisite Risk

- A Polynesian myth is recounted in which a mother regularly sheds her skin at the river to renew herself, but one day retrieves her old skin to comfort her frightened teenage daughter.

- According to the myth, this act of returning to the old skin is the moment humans lost their ability to be immortal and fully alive in the timeless present.

- Holding onto old skin is described as a way of soothing the inner child, feeling protected, and meeting others' expectations of predictability.

- For practitioners, shedding skin is understood as a continuous process of shining light on what is happening, becoming more undefended, and exposing oneself to growth rather than a single transformative event.

- Mark Nepo's phrase "taking the exquisite risk" is introduced to describe this process, with "exquisite" connoting beauty, sensitivity, and excellence, and "risk" acknowledging the ego's perception of danger and loss.

- A passage from Dana Falds' poem "Trusting Prana" is read, urging the listener to trust the energy that courses through them, surrender deeply, follow each sensation to its source, and dare to be their own illumination.

- Specific examples of taking the exquisite risk within the training are named, including sharing authentically in dyads, holding eye contact, leading practices outside one's old identity, and stretching into realness in many forms.

### Guided Practice on Resourcing and Taking Refuge as Teachers

- A guided practice is introduced to help participants sense, as emerging teachers, where doubt and old skin are still held, and how to take refuge in the most awake parts of themselves to become freer.

- Rick Hanson's resourcing process is referenced as a helpful framework involving turning toward what one loves, fully letting in the associated feelings, and then sensing what one loves about it.

- Possible objects of resourcing are offered, including images of loved ones, children, animals, or friends, as well as any experience that evokes a sense of love or safety.

- A passage from Rilke is read in which God speaks to each person, urging them to go to the limits of their longing, embody the divine, let everything happen including beauty and terror, and trust that no feeling is final.

- The facilitator shares a personal resourcing practice of envisioning a field of luminous loving presence that is close and real, receiving its blessing, and using physical gestures such as bringing palms together or bowing the head to soften the egoic self and increase receptivity.

- Words spoken in a prayerful or grateful tone such as "please love me" or "thank you" are described as ways of softening the outer layers of the ego and creating greater openness to receiving.

- The final phase of taking refuge is described as a resting that becomes a becoming, in which longing transforms into belonging.

- Flexibility in language is encouraged, noting that the same resourcing process can be framed in psychological, poetic, or tradition-specific spiritual terms depending on the audience.

### The Motivating Force Behind Spiritual Unfolding

- The deep question of what motivates the unfolding toward awakening is raised, pointing beyond conditioning and contraction toward something that pulls from within.

- Early conditioning is described as creating a contracted dream state, analogous to the story of not wanting to go to school, that resists growth and emergence.

- A calling from one's future self or awake evolved beingness is proposed as the force that urges turning toward what one loves and continuing the journey of unfolding.

- These two pulls, the contraction of old conditioning and the calling of emerging beingness, are named as the two poles that shape the ongoing practice of awakening.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to continue experimenting with personal resourcing rituals and to identify the specific images, gestures, words, or practices that most effectively reconnect them with a sense of love, safety, and larger beingness.

- Developing flexibility in framing resourcing and refuge practices across psychological, poetic, and spiritual languages is identified as an ongoing priority for participants as they grow in their roles as teachers.

- The integration of opening rituals such as chanting, intention setting, and heart reflection into teaching contexts is affirmed as a practice to carry forward into participants' own groups and classes.

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Nature of Awakening and the Egoic Self**
- Awakening is not about eliminating the ego but integrating it into a larger sense of beingness
- The egoic self perpetuates a trance of separation, comparison, and unworthiness that must be recognized and held with compassion
- Doubt is identified as the last and most persistent fetter on the path to awakening
**Ritual as a Gateway to Presence**
- Ritual — such as chanting, bowing, or sitting in stillness — serves as a reliable technology for shifting out of habitual mind states
- These practices create a container of receptivity for something beyond ordinary thinking
**Resourcing as a Spiritual Act**
- Turning toward what one loves is not escapism but a spiritually intelligent act, mirroring the Buddha's own gesture of touching the earth
- Rumi's teaching — "turning towards what you love saves you" — is cited as a universal principle
- The act of calling on the beloved, whether framed as God, loving awareness, or Buddha nature, softens the egoic defenses and opens the practitioner to grace
**Impermanence and the Preciousness of Time**
- The reading from Donna Falls emphasizes treating each moment as if it were the last, pushing nothing aside as unimportant
- Time is described as precious and disappearing; full awareness is the antidote to letting life slip by
**The Myth of Immortality and Shedding Skin**
- The Polynesian myth suggests that humans lost immortality — the ability to be fully alive in the timeless present — by clinging to old identity out of fear and to comfort others
- Spiritual growth requires the willingness to become unrecognizable to those accustomed to our old selves
**The Call of the Future Self**
- There is described as a pull or calling from one's future, awake self toward greater beingness
- This calling is what motivates the turn toward what we love and the willingness to take the exquisite risk

- Shared by @Tara Brach, attributed to Anthony de Mello
- A father knocks on his son Jamie's door to wake him for school; Jamie refuses, citing three reasons: school is dull, kids tease him, and he hates school. The father gives three reasons he must go: it is his duty, he is 45 years old, and he is the headmaster

**The Buddha's Night Under the Tree of Awakening**
- Shared by @Tara Brach
- The Buddha sat through the night under the Bodhi tree, holding with compassion everything that arose — all the challenges of Mara (the shadow side) — which were transformed into flower petals by morning
- The final challenge was Mara's shadow of doubt; Siddhartha responded by touching the ground and calling on the Earth Goddess to bear witness to his intrinsic goodness, which caused Mara to back off and enabled full awakening
- Used to illustrate the two practices of direct presence and resourcing
**The Polynesian Myth of Shedding Skin**
- Shared by @Tara Brach, sourced from Polynesian tradition
- A mother of the tribe regularly went to the river to shed her skin; one time her old skin caught on a branch and she returned to the village in her new skin. Her teenage daughter was frightened and distressed by the change. To comfort her daughter, the mother returned to the river, found her old skin, and put it back on
- The Polynesians say that from that day on, humans lost their ability to be immortal — to be fully alive in the timeless present moment
- Used to illustrate how we hold onto old identity to soothe others and ourselves, and the cost of doing so
