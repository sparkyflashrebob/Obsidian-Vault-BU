# MMTCP Podcasts — Q&A Transcripts & Question Index

This document provides a comprehensive, cataloged archive and index of the Questions & Answers (Q&A), dialogues, and pedagogical inquiries from the **Mindfulness Meditation Teacher Certification Program (MMTCP)** podcast series.

Each session features founding teachers **Jack Kornfield** and **Tara Brach**, along with guest master teachers, addressing the core dilemmas of meditation practice, teacher-student dynamics, emotional healing, trauma sensitivity, and embodied presence.

#### Question & Answer Catalog

##### Q1 [06:50] — Student Inquiry: In a daylong training, teaching mindfulness of thought using Tara Brach's phrase 'real but not true' caused co...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> In a daylong training, teaching mindfulness of thought using Tara Brach's phrase 'real but not true' caused confusion, especially for devout Christians. How can we help students notice healthy versus unhealthy thoughts without getting caught in theological or philosophical debates over what is 'real'?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Thoughts are present, but they are not necessarily true. Keeping the language simple prevents students from getting lost in metaphysical arguments. We can invite them to examine which thoughts serve their best interests and values, and which are destructive or lead them away from their hearts.

##### Q2 [08:18] — Student Inquiry: I have been asked to teach an individual person, but I worry about acting more as a counselor or crossing prof...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> I have been asked to teach an individual person, but I worry about acting more as a counselor or crossing professional lines. How should we navigate 1-on-1 teaching?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Trust your intuitive sense. If it feels like the wrong setting, it is completely fine to explain that you primarily teach groups. If you do teach individuals, maintain transparent boundaries: state clearly that you are guiding them in mindfulness and lovingkindness practices, not functioning as a psychotherapist or counselor.

##### Q3 [09:16] — Student Inquiry (Psychiatric Nurse): Many of us are healthcare professionals or psychotherapists trained in an illness model. How can we offer medi...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> Many of us are healthcare professionals or psychotherapists trained in an illness model. How can we offer meditation skills without violating professional boundaries or falling into diagnostic traps?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Transparency is the greatest protection. Step consciously into the role of a meditation guide rather than a clinician. Name the shift explicitly. In this role, we look for what is whole, healthy, and luminous in each human being rather than diagnosing pathology.

##### Q4 [10:32] — Student Inquiry: How do we work with student projections—both positive idealization and negative blame—without personal reactiv...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> How do we work with student projections—both positive idealization and negative blame—without personal reactivity?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Quiet the heart and remember that praise and blame are the ancient worldly winds. Even the Buddha and Jesus faced praise and blame. Take a breath and remember our role is not to seek praise or evade blame, but to offer the heartfelt practices that work. Receive praise with a bow and let it go; meet criticism with curious listening.

##### Q5 [11:52] — Student Inquiry: As I prepare to lead a silent retreat day, how should we structure the schedule and skillfully break the silen...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> As I prepare to lead a silent retreat day, how should we structure the schedule and skillfully break the silence at the conclusion?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Follow the classic sequence: sitting, walking, body awareness, emotions, thoughts, and lovingkindness. Trust the structure. To break silence gently, invite students to notice the transition within themselves first, share in pairs, then small groups of four, before opening to the full room.

##### Q6 [13:03] — Student Inquiry: In my first practicum, is my role simply to share wisdom and trust that seeds will germinate, or should I lovi...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> In my first practicum, is my role simply to share wisdom and trust that seeds will germinate, or should I lovingly nudge students to commit to a daily practice?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Both are true. We plant seeds with deep patience and trust, and at times we offer a gentle, encouraging nudge toward consistent practice.

##### Q7 [13:40] — Student Inquiry: What lineage or type of meditation should we tell students we teach? Is it Insight meditation, MBSR, or Dharma...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> What lineage or type of meditation should we tell students we teach? Is it Insight meditation, MBSR, or Dharma teaching?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

The simplest and most authentic response is that we teach mindfulness meditation, compassion, and lovingkindness. MBSR itself was developed from these very mindfulness retreat traditions.

##### Q8 [14:38] — Student Inquiry: What about the imposter syndrome feeling that we need to know everything? How do we provide a great student ex...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> What about the imposter syndrome feeling that we need to know everything? How do we provide a great student experience without omniscience?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

No one knows everything. Even great masters like the Dalai Lama openly say, 'I don't know.' It is deeply liberating and humanizing to say, 'I don't know the answer to that; let's explore it together.' We teach from what we have lived and known.

##### Q9 [15:40] — Student Inquiry: What guidelines should we follow regarding teacher-student ethical boundaries, especially when student adorati...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> What guidelines should we follow regarding teacher-student ethical boundaries, especially when student adoration inflates ego?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Maintain clear ethical boundaries. Beyond physical and financial ethics, pay close attention when adoration arises. Ground yourself in what is true and avoid feeding on praise.

##### Q10 [17:00] — Student Inquiry: How should we work with trauma in meditation settings as outlined in Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness?...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> How should we work with trauma in meditation settings as outlined in Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Remember that we are meditation teachers, not trauma therapists. When trauma arises, help students ground in physical resources—feeling their feet on the floor, opening their eyes, softening the belly—and remind them they have full agency to pause practice.

##### Q11 [17:33] — Student Inquiry: Students ask whether metta is just positive thinking or an affirmation. What is the difference?...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> Students ask whether metta is just positive thinking or an affirmation. What is the difference?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Affirmations often assert 'I am calm' or 'I am loving,' which can feel forced or untrue. Metta phrases are intentions and prayers of the heart ('May you be safe, may you be held in compassion'). They orient the heart in a wholesome direction without denying present difficulty.

##### Q12 [18:12] — Student Inquiry: Self-doubt is my biggest challenge: 'Who am I to be teaching this?' How do we hold feelings of inadequacy?...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> Self-doubt is my biggest challenge: 'Who am I to be teaching this?' How do we hold feelings of inadequacy?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Hold self-doubt with real tenderness. Recall the Zen poet Ryokan: 'Last year a foolish monk, this year no change.' The path is not about perfecting an ego; it is about embodying compassionate presence.

##### Q13 [18:45] — Student Inquiry: I want to affirm basic goodness, but I cannot authentically say I feel it for everyone yet. How do we navigate...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> I want to affirm basic goodness, but I cannot authentically say I feel it for everyone yet. How do we navigate this?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Affirm what you can authentically touch right now. Even 80% authenticity is wonderful. We grow into our capacities gradually, just like tending a garden.

##### Q14 [19:14] — Student Inquiry: How do we work with the desire for recognition, praise, or fame on the spiritual path?...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> How do we work with the desire for recognition, praise, or fame on the spiritual path?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Notice desire with mindful awareness rather than self-condemnation. Beneath the longing for recognition is often a human yearning to be valued. When we inhabit our own inherent worth, the craving for external praise naturally subsides.

##### Q15 [19:42] — Student Inquiry: What are the recommendations for teaching groups composed of our own therapy patients or executive coaching cl...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> What are the recommendations for teaching groups composed of our own therapy patients or executive coaching clients?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Make an explicit shift in frame. Announce that during this period, you are stepping out of the therapist/coach role into the role of a mindfulness guide.

##### Q16 [20:30] — Student Inquiry: How do we handle painful silence or reluctance to share in practicum groups?...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> How do we handle painful silence or reluctance to share in practicum groups?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Silence is not a failure. Sit with the quiet for a minute or two, and gently name it: 'We are sharing a moment of stillness together.' Acknowledging the silence creates safety, and students often open up naturally afterward.

##### Q17 [21:20] — Student Inquiry: Despite encouragement, very few students develop a daily formal practice. How can we support them?...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> Despite encouragement, very few students develop a daily formal practice. How can we support them?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Establish peer practice buddies. Having students text or check in with each other provides mutual accountability. Micro-practices (10-15 minutes daily) are far more accessible than daunting hour-long sits.

##### Q18 [22:30] — Student Inquiry: I led an exercise imagining having only one month to live, and students became visibly overwhelmed. Did I cros...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> I led an exercise imagining having only one month to live, and students became visibly overwhelmed. Did I cross a line in a workplace intro class?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Trust your intuition. Intensive reflections on death and mortality can be too confronting for introductory corporate settings. Ground introductory students in physical anchors and emotional resourcing before introducing deeper existential inquiries.

##### Q19 [25:12] — Student Inquiry: How do we manage student requests for lunch, coffee, or individual availability outside of class hours?...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> How do we manage student requests for lunch, coffee, or individual availability outside of class hours?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Set warm, transparent boundaries. Explain that while you are happy to answer questions for a few minutes after class, other life and family commitments prevent private socializing.

##### Q20 [25:47] — Student Inquiry: Students expect teachers to have perfect lives. How and when should we disclose our own struggles or imperfect...

> **Question / Inquiry:**
> Students expect teachers to have perfect lives. How and when should we disclose our own struggles or imperfections?

**Teacher Response (Jack Kornfield):**

Share personal difficulty only after you have integrated it and gained some perspective. Sharing active, unresolved drama burdens students. Sharing how we met difficulty with mindfulness and self-compassion models real-world practice.
