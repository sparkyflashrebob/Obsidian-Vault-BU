# Mod 23-1 True Nature Awake Awareness - Oct, 20

### Mod 23-1 True Nature Awake Awareness
📅 October 20, 2026
👥 Tara Brach (Main Teacher), Jack Kornfield (Co-Teacher)
Tags: True Nature, Awareness, Non-Duality, Mindfulness, Self-Inquiry, Enlightenment, Spiritual Practice, Meditation, Identity, Trance, Compassion, Teaching Methods, Contemplative Practice
### Outline
### The Central Inquiry of True Nature and the Question "Who Am I?"

- Explored in this session is the investigation of essential nature and the fundamental question of what awareness is.

- A metaphor is introduced describing the process of awakening as jumping from a plane without a parachute, realizing there is no ground to hit because everything is changing, and ultimately recognizing that there was no one who jumped.

- This sequence illustrates the progressive dissolution of the illusion of control, permanence, and a separate self.

### Approaches to Teaching True Nature and Awareness

- A wide range of teaching approaches exists, from allowing mindfulness practices to reveal true nature implicitly, to pointing directly at awareness without naming it explicitly, to openly describing and naming the nature of awareness.

- Some teachers feel that explicitly discussing true nature does not match their temperament or their understanding of how change occurs, preferring instead to let practice speak for itself.

- Others find value in practices that orient students toward awareness without using explicit language, while still others believe that direct pointing out instructions can awaken an innate knowing that already exists within the student.

- Nisargadatta Maharaj is cited as an example of someone who trusted his teacher's direct statement that he was pure awareness, pondered it for years, and eventually realized it as true, illustrating how trust in the possibility of awakening can itself deepen the inquiry.

- Consideration is given to the appropriateness of these teachings depending on the setting, with quieter and more settled environments being more conducive to inviting the investigation of awareness.

### Key Elements Included When Teaching True Nature

- When incorporating teachings on true nature, Tara Brach outlines several core components she typically includes, particularly later in retreats or periodically in weekly classes.

- One foundational element is conveying that true nature is always already present and that realizing it is a genuine human potential, not a distant or impossible achievement.

- Another element involves acknowledging the strong conditioning that keeps people in a state of psychological trance or dream, removed from the recognition of their essential nature.

- Significant time is devoted to illuminating the nature of trance itself, as shining light on the dream is understood to be a primary gateway to recognizing what lies beyond it.

- Different practices for seeing past the trance and for sensing and embodying true nature are also explored as part of this teaching framework.

### The Musk Deer Legend and the Delusion of Seeking Elsewhere

- An ancient Indian legend is shared in which a musk deer spends its entire life searching the world for the source of a heavenly fragrance, only to discover at the moment of death that the scent had been emanating from within itself all along.

- This story is presented as a metaphor for the deepest and most commonly forgotten truth that what human beings most long for is not located elsewhere in time or space but is present right here and now.

- Living according to a mental map of progress toward some future attainment is described as a fundamental block to the radical presence of what is already here.

- The perennial philosophy and the teachings of the Buddha are cited as converging on this same reminder that what is sought is already present, even in moments of greatest confusion, helplessness, or hopelessness.

### The Wave and Ocean Metaphor and the Process of Awakening

- The process of enlightenment is described as waking up from the story of a separate self and recognizing, with increasing familiarity, the loving awareness that is always present.

- A central metaphor used throughout the teaching is that of waves and the ocean, illustrating that individual experience is never truly separate from the underlying ground of awareness, just as a wave is never separate from the ocean.

- Flexibility in language is emphasized as an important teaching skill, with terms such as beingness, ultimate reality, God consciousness, and the mystery all pointing to the same ground of being.

### The Role of the Teacher's Own Trust and Confidence

- The most powerful foundation for teaching true nature is identified as the teacher's own trust and confidence in what is present now and always.

- This inner confidence is described as contagious, meaning that when a teacher is genuinely awake in spirit, students naturally trust more deeply.

- Personal experience is shared of being asked on retreat whether one trusts being an awakening Buddha, with the honest answer being "sometimes," particularly in moments of stillness, spaciousness, or connection with nature.

### Shining Light on Trance and the Inquiry "Who Am I Taking Myself to Be?"

- The inquiry "Who am I taking myself to be?" is introduced as a powerful tool for beginning to illuminate the trance state, which is described as occupying large portions of both teachers' and students' lives.

- Trance is characterized as a mentally constructed world in which the protagonist is a separate self engaged in constant self-referencing and moving through time toward some imagined destination.

- Aldous Huxley's concept of a reducing valve of awareness is referenced, describing how stress and fear cause awareness to narrow and fixate on objects as a survival strategy, leading people to increasingly identify with a contracted band of reality.

- Nisargadatta Maharaj's teaching is again invoked, describing how the real world lies beyond the net of thoughts and desires and that this net, being full of holes, can be stepped through.

- The phenomenon of selfing is introduced as the habitual tendency of the mind to organize around a sense of self, which is acknowledged as a natural feature of human brain architecture rather than a flaw to be condemned.

- Making selfing into a problem is noted as counterproductive, as the path involves shining a compassionate light on self-referential awareness rather than judging it.

### Pain as a Gateway to Awakening and the Story of the Architect

- Pain is identified as a common catalyst for waking up from identification with a contracted sense of self, as the discomfort of living in a cocoon that is too small motivates the search for a larger sense of being.

- A story is shared of a woman whose father, a celebrated architect, had been emotionally distant throughout her life due to his focus on his professional accomplishments.

- When asked near the end of his life what he was most proud of, the father replied with tears that it was his daughter, revealing how decades of fixation on work had obscured what mattered most.

- This story illustrates how impermanence can serve as a powerful wake-up call, revealing the way the reducing valve of awareness causes people to fixate on what is ultimately less important.

- The concept of the big squeeze is introduced to describe the daily oscillation between moments of smallness and self-concern and moments of touching something larger, such as the heart's awareness and a sense of interconnection.

### Two Pathways for Guiding Students Toward True Nature

- Two primary pathways are described for guiding students toward the recognition of true nature, one moving through form and one moving directly toward formless awareness.

- The first pathway involves bringing mindfulness and heartfulness to changing forms and phenomena, with the understanding that deepening attention to the waves of experience naturally reveals their ocean-like nature.

- The second pathway, more accessible in quieter settings such as retreats, involves turning attention directly toward awareness itself, which then naturally includes and encompasses all changing forms.

- The Heart Sutra's teaching that form is emptiness and emptiness is form is cited as the philosophical ground for understanding that these two pathways are ultimately not separate.

- Students are guided to notice the background of awareness that is present when there is some quietness and less reactivity, as a way of beginning to sense the formless dimension of experience.

### The Story of Sherry Maples and Realization Through Loss

- The life of Sherry Maples, a Dharma teacher who died following a serious bicycle accident, is offered as an example of realization through sustained engagement with form and loss.

- Prior to the accident, she had experienced a devastating end to a long-term relationship, which led to a deep depression and withdrawal from teaching and daily life.

- Her recovery involved repeatedly feeling the weightiness, fear, and loneliness of that loss and bringing self-compassion to it, which gradually opened her to a sense of oceanic tenderness beneath the waves of painful experience.

- Through this repeated process, she came to recognize that the tender presence she was relaxing into was more truly who she was than any story of a rejected or lonely self.

- When visited after the accident, she described herself as having already died through the earlier loss, and said that the space opened by that prior work was large enough to hold the new one.

- Her capacity to see the light and goodness in everyone who visited her in her final year was understood as a direct expression of the fearless heart she had cultivated through this process.

### Guided Practice for Sensing Awareness Through Loving Presence

- A brief guided meditation is offered as a direct experiential demonstration of how mindfulness practice reveals emptiness and true nature.

- Participants are invited to close their eyes, arrive in the present moment, and allow awareness to fill the body, feeling aliveness and softening into physical sensation.

- Bringing to mind a being toward whom love arises easily, participants are guided to sense how that being sees goodness in them and to feel the goodness in return.

- As the image of the person fades, attention is directed toward the felt sense of loving itself, its warmth, spaciousness, and edgelessness.

- The quality of tender presence that arises in this loving is described as more true than any story, pointing to the ground of being as the Radiant Sutras passage is read, describing the heart as a place of vastness where everything meets and where one can know "I belong here."

### The Backward Step and Direct Investigation of Awareness

- The practice of taking a backward step is described as a way of relaxing back repeatedly until one is inhabiting awareness itself, with nowhere further to go.

- Awareness cannot be perceived through the senses in the ordinary way, as it is that which is looking rather than an object that can be seen, analogous to the eye being unable to see itself.

- A Tibetan teacher's illustration is shared in which a V drawn on white paper is described not as a bird but as sky with a bird flying through it, demonstrating the shift from fixating on objects to recognizing the formless space in which they appear.

- A simple classroom exercise is described in which participants are asked to try not to be aware for ten seconds, after which the ever-present nature of awareness becomes immediately self-evident.

- Awareness is characterized as having qualities of openness without edge or boundary, inherent wakefulness, a knowing quality, and a natural tenderness and responsiveness, described through the image of a sunlit sky in which openness and illumination are inseparable.

### Jack Kornfield's Contributions on Identity Shift and Everyday Nirvana

- Jack Kornfield frames the purpose of all contemplative teachings, whether mindfulness, compassion, loving kindness, or forgiveness, as fundamentally a shift of identity away from identification with particular stories, feelings, or experiences.

- An approach used with students is described in which, rather than being drawn into the drama a student presents, the teacher responds with gentle curiosity, saying something like "How interesting, you're really into that one," which helps the student begin to step back from their identification.

- Another practice described involves gazing into someone's eyes and seeing their secret beauty, recognizing who they really are beyond the content of their experience, without necessarily needing to say anything explicit.

- The mirror exercise is offered as a teaching tool, inviting people to notice that while the body visibly ages, there is a sense of inner presence that does not feel older, pointing to the fact that awareness itself does not exist in time.

- The Big Sky or Big Mind Meditation is referenced as another accessible gateway, beginning with listening to sound and expanding the sense of mind to be as vast as the sky, with sounds, thoughts, and sensations appearing like clouds or bubbles within it.

- The concept of everyday nirvana is introduced, describing the ordinary moments in daily life when one is neither grasping nor resisting and a natural peacefulness and presence is available, suggesting these moments deserve recognition and celebration rather than being overlooked.

- A vast silence is described as surrounding everything, accessible whenever one quiets even briefly, encompassing the turning of galaxies, the movement of planets, and the changing of seasons, pointing to a dimension of being larger than personal life drama.

### Two Essential Elements for Resting in Awareness

- Two key elements are identified as necessary for the practice of turning toward and resting in awareness, particularly when a student is not caught in strong emotional reactivity.

- The first element is non-doing, described as the capacity to rest completely and allow everything to be as it is, without directing attention, focusing on an object, or trying to produce a particular experience.

- Any form of contriving or effortful directing of attention is understood to create a subtle contraction that prevents full recognition of awareness itself.

- A recommendation is made to reserve a portion of each sitting for stopping all meditating entirely, on the basis that the deepest goal of meditation is to realize who one is beyond the meditator.

- The second element is inquiry, described as a subtle questioning that brings the interest of the mind to what is present, followed immediately by releasing and resting rather than pursuing the question analytically.

- The practice is described visually as a movement of looking and seeing, asking "What is here, who am I," and then letting go and being free, alternating between engaged inquiry and open resting.

### Next Steps

- Continued development of flexibility in language for pointing to true nature is recognized as an important area of ongoing growth for teachers.

- Integrating both pathways, through form and directly toward awareness, into teaching practice is affirmed as a valuable direction depending on the readiness and settledness of students.

- Reserving time within meditation sittings for non-doing and the cessation of all effortful practice is identified as a key element to incorporate into personal and instructional practice.

- A final group meditation is planned to close the session, with Jack Kornfield invited to contribute to that closing.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Question "Who Am I?" and the Investigation of True Nature**
- The session centers on the inquiry "Who am I?" as a gateway to understanding essential nature and awareness
- Teachers are encouraged to consider whether and how to address true nature explicitly with students
- Some teachers allow mindfulness practices to reveal true nature without naming it; others point directly to awareness; others use explicit descriptions
**The Parachute Metaphor**
- True nature is revealed through three recognitions: there is no parachute (no control), there is no ground to hit (everything is changing), and there was no one who jumped
**Conditioning and the Dream of a Separate Self**
- Strong conditioning keeps people in a dream or trance, removed from realization of true nature
- The trance is described as a mentally constructed world in which the protagonist is the self, with constant self-referencing
- Aldous Huxley's concept of the "reducing valve of awareness" — stress and fear narrow the band of reality, causing fixation on objects as a survival strategy
- The more stressed a person is, the more tight and narrow their perceived reality becomes
**Selfing**
- "Selfing" is the habitual organizing of experience around a sense of self
- It is part of the brain's architecture and not inherently wrong
- The path involves shining a light on self-reflexive awareness so one is not identified with it
**Two Pathways to Realizing True Nature**
- Pathway one: paying attention to forms (waves), deepening attention until the ocean-ness is discovered; compassion and mindfulness applied to changing experience reveal the formless
- Pathway two: when there is quietness, turning attention directly to awareness itself; awareness then naturally includes changing forms
- These two pathways correspond to the Heart Sutra teaching that form is emptiness and emptiness is form
**The Backward Step**
- Resting in awareness is described as "taking a backward step" — relaxing back repeatedly until inhabiting awareness itself
- Awareness cannot be seen through the senses; it is what is looking
- The Tibetan teacher's poster illustration: a V shape is not just a bird but "the sky with a bird flying through" — shifting attention from object to formless space
**Non-Doing and Inquiry**
- Non-doing is essential to resting in awareness; any contriving or directing of attention creates contraction
- Tara Brach recommends reserving a portion of each sitting to stop all meditating, since the goal is to realize who we are beyond the meditator
- Inquiry involves dropping in a question ("What is here? Who am I?") and then resting — look and see, then let go and be free
**Shift of Identity**
- Jack Kornfield describes all contemplative teachings as fundamentally a shift of identity — releasing identification with a particular story, feeling, or experience
- Seeing the "secret beauty" in another person — their true nature — without needing many words
- Looking in the mirror and noticing aging of the body while not feeling older as a gateway: awareness itself does not exist in time
**Big Sky / Big Mind Meditation**
- Beginning with sound, experiencing the mind as vast like the sky, with sounds, thoughts, and sensations appearing like clouds or bubbles
- Invites a shift of identity in a simple and accessible way
**Everyday Nirvana**
- Moments in daily life when there is neither grasping nor resisting and a sense of presence — described as everyday nirvana
- A vast silence surrounds everything; the galaxies, stars, and seasons point to a vastness beyond personal life drama
**Teaching Considerations**
- For students who need stabilization, self-compassion, and grounding, inviting inquiry into awareness itself could be spiritual bypass
- For students with quietness and settledness, inquiry into awareness is a powerful and appropriate invitation
- Flexibility in language is important: beingness, ultimate reality, God consciousness, the mystery, loving awareness, ground of being
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Perennial Teaching Across Traditions**
- All spiritual and contemplative paths, including the teachings of the Buddha, share the reminder that what we seek is already here
- The "map of progress" — living as though realization is down the road — blocks radical presence to what is right here now
**The Deepest Delusion of the Separate Self**
- The deepest delusion is that what we long for is anywhere other than this present moment
- This delusion manifests as believing realization belongs to wise beings of the past, or is achievable only through long effort
**Pain as a Gateway**
- Pain and impermanence are described as the great wake-up calls that reveal how attention has been fixated on what is less important
- The story of the dying architect illustrates how decades can pass in fixation before recognizing what truly matters
**The Big Squeeze in Daily Life**
- Every day, stress causes people to become small and self-concerned
- Moments of touching something larger — the heart, awareness, interconnectedness — punctuate this smallness
- The path involves the ongoing cycle of remembering and forgetting
**Trust and Possibility as Catalysts**
- When a student trusts that awakening is possible, that very sense of possibility causes them to look more deeply
- Being in the presence of someone who is genuinely awake increases trust in others
- The contagious quality of genuine awakening is itself a teaching tool
**Awareness as Timeless and Boundless**
- Awareness has no edge or boundary; it is the space in which everything happens
- It has an inherent wakefulness and knowing quality, described as a sunlit sky — open, illuminated, interwoven knowing and openness
- Awareness is naturally tender and responsive when engaged with experience
**The Wave and Ocean Metaphor**
- We are never separated from awakened heart-mind any more than a wave is separate from the ocean
- The process of enlightenment is waking up from the story of a separate self and recognizing the loving awareness that is always here

### Stories
**The Parachute and No Ground**
Shared by Tara Brach. A metaphor describing the revelation of true nature: jumping from a plane with no parachute, then realizing there is no ground to hit because everything is changing, then realizing there was no one who jumped.
**The Musk Deer**
Shared by Tara Brach. A legend from ancient India. A musk deer detects a heavenly fragrance and spends its life searching the world — climbing icy mountains, trekking through jungles and deserts — only to collapse at the end of its life. As it falls, its horn pierces its own belly and the fragrance fills the air. The deer realizes the scent had been emanating from within all along.
**Jamie and the Headmaster**
Shared by Tara Brach, attributed to Anthony de Mello. A father tries to wake his son Jamie for school. Jamie refuses, citing three reasons: school is dull, kids tease him, and he hates school. The father gives three reasons Jamie must go: it is his duty, he is 45 years old, and he is the headmaster. Used to illustrate the nature of trance — being asleep to one's own true situation.
**The Tibetan Teacher and the V Shape**
Shared by Tara Brach. A Tibetan teacher draws a V on a large white poster and asks what it is. Most people say "a bird." The teacher responds it is "the sky with a bird flying through." Used to illustrate the shift from fixating on the object to perceiving the formless space.
**The Meditating Cat**
Shared by Tara Brach, attributed to poet Piccarum. A poet meditates with their cat, who suddenly cries out asking what happened. The cat describes having been mingled with the sky, comets, suns, and galaxies, but now being landlocked in fur. The poet responds, "I know exactly what you mean." Used to illustrate the experience of being conditioned into a small biological self after knowing something larger.
**Sherry Maples — The Bike Accident and Prior Loss**
Shared by Tara Brach about her dear friend Sherry Maples, a Dharma teacher who passed away after a major bike accident. Sherry had previously experienced a devastating end to a long-term relationship, fell into deep depression, withdrew from teaching, and gradually found her way back through mindfulness and self-compassion. Each round of bringing compassion to the waves of pain deepened her realization of true nature. When Tara visited her after the accident, Sherry said, "I had already died," meaning the prior loss had already opened that space of awareness. In her final year, Sherry's transparency and spirit inspired all who visited her.
**The Dying Architect Father**
Shared by Tara Brach. A woman with a distant relationship to her father — a celebrated architect — spends time with him as he is dying. She asks what accomplishments he is most proud of. After a long pause, with tears in his eyes, he says, "Why you, of course." The story illustrates how decades can pass with attention fixated on work and achievement, with impermanence finally revealing what truly mattered.
**The Mirror and Aging**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. Looking in the mirror and noticing the body has aged, yet not feeling older inside. Used as a gateway to recognizing that awareness itself does not exist in time and is not the body.
**Tara Brach's Own Retreat Experience**
Shared by Tara Brach about herself. At an early retreat, a teacher asked, "Do you trust that you're an awakening Buddha?" Tara answered "sure" — meaning yes in moments of stillness, space, and nature, but also noticing the many moments of trance: fixation on food, concern about appearance, wanting to impress the teacher. This led her to the inquiry "Who am I taking myself to be?" as a way of shining light on trance.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Sherry Maples — Physical Injury and Loss of Mobility**
- Sherry Maples, a Dharma teacher and former professional athlete, was involved in a major bike accident
- She was never able to walk again following the accident and lived approximately one year before passing away from her injuries
- The loss of physical capacity was described as very compelling; her prior inner work with mindfulness and compassion was noted as foundational to how she held this experience
**Depression Following Relationship Loss**
- Sherry Maples experienced a major depression following the end of a long-term relationship
- She withdrew from teaching and from almost all activity
- Recovery involved many months of being "bottomed out" before gradually returning to present-moment awareness and self-compassion
**Stress and the Narrowing of Awareness**
- Stress and fear are described as causing the "reducing valve of awareness" to narrow, leading to fixation on objects as a survival strategy
- The more stressed a person feels, the more tight and narrow their perceived band of reality becomes, leading to over-identification with thoughts
**Spiritual Bypass as a Risk**
- For students who need stabilization, grounding, and self-compassion, inviting inquiry into awareness itself is identified as a risk of spiritual bypass
- Teachers are advised to assess the readiness and settledness of students before introducing direct awareness practices
**The "Big Squeeze" as a Daily Mental Challenge**
- Daily stress causes people to become small-minded and self-concerned, preoccupied with comfort, performance, and potential problems
- This is described as a regular feature of human experience that practitioners and teachers alike navigate
