### Gratitude and Acknowledgment of the Graduating Cohort

- Brightness and caring are highlighted as defining characteristics of this cohort, as observed through live sessions, emails, and mentor feedback.

- Participants are encouraged to pause and genuinely feel the truth of their own dedication before moving forward.

- An apology is extended to community members unable to attend due to the Chinese New Year, acknowledging the scheduling as an example of unconscious centering on the dominant culture calendar.

- Diversity within the cohort is affirmed as something to be honored and actively supported going forward.

### Qualities of an Inspiring and Effective Teacher

- A reflective exercise is introduced inviting participants to bring to mind teachers who have deeply touched them and to identify the qualities that made those teachers impactful.

- Participants share qualities via chat including depth, presence, authenticity, love, joy, generosity, humor, honesty, compassion, humility, courage, groundedness, and unconditional acceptance.

- Parker Palmer's research on teaching is referenced, highlighting that the common denominator among inspiring teachers is a passionate, inside-out love for what they are teaching.

- Believing in students, respecting them, and communicating that they have inherent goodness and capability is identified as a foundational quality shared by both early-life and spiritual teachers.

- A Tibetan teacher's principle of never giving up on anyone is presented as a powerful expression of trust in the Buddha nature present in every person.

- Mirroring goodness back to students, helping them trust their own awakening nature, is described as one of the most essential gifts a teacher can offer.

### The Role of Selfing in Teaching and How It Obstructs Presence

- Selfing, defined as habitual preoccupation with or insecurity about the self, is identified as a primary obstacle to compassionate, open-hearted teaching.

- Monitoring one's own performance during teaching, worrying about being liked, or comparing oneself to other teachers are offered as common and natural expressions of selfing.

- Worth and lovability becoming hitched to teaching performance is described as the root cause of vulnerability and self-doubt in teachers.

- Ram Dass is quoted to illustrate that the goal is not to eliminate judgment, craving, or impatience, but to develop a lighter, more good-humored relationship with these arising states.

- Selfing is reframed as changing currents within a larger sea of being, rather than as a fixed or defining characteristic of the teacher.

- Adding judgment to selfing is cautioned against, as doing so simply creates more selfing and deepens identification with it.

### Three Supports for Evolving as a Teacher

- Sharing selfing experiences openly with fellow teachers, including both inflation and deflation, is presented as the first support, reducing identification and the sense of isolation.

- Specialness and the tendency to position oneself as the sage on the stage rather than the guide on the side is named as a form of selfing that is often overlooked.

- Pausing before teaching to ask what really matters and reconnecting with sincere aspiration is offered as the second support, as this contact with genuine caring dissolves selfing and restores naturalness.

- Mary Oliver's idea that as long as you are dancing you can break the rules is cited to illustrate how sincerity of aspiration allows for greater freedom and spontaneity in teaching.

- Remembering one's place within a vast, multi-generational movement of spiritual awakening is described as the third support, shifting the sense of identity from isolated individual to a conduit for something much larger.

- Connecting to the lineages and thousands of years of dedicated practitioners allows awakening awareness to teach through the teacher rather than from the ego of the teacher.

### Guided Reflection on Growing Edges in Teaching

- Participants are guided into stillness and invited to scan for the beliefs, feelings, and patterns that most obstruct flow, presence, and heart-centered teaching.

- Self-doubt, fear of not being helpful, perfectionism, imposter syndrome, fear of judgment, and the need to be liked are among the growing edges shared by participants in the chat.

- Concerns about not knowing enough, not embodying the teachings sufficiently, and not being able to hold others' pain are also surfaced as common obstacles.

- Participants are encouraged to notice that these insecurities are widely shared across the cohort, reinforcing the understanding that such experiences are a universal part of the human condition.

### Guided Reflection on Messages from the Future Self

- A second guided reflection invites participants to journey imaginatively into the future and encounter a more awake, evolved version of themselves.

- Participants are encouraged to share their most challenging teaching obstacle with their future self and then remain open and receptive to whatever wisdom arises.

- Messages received and shared by participants include affirmations such as you are already enough, trust with a big T, you belong, do not hold anything back, and only love matters.

- Leonard Cohen's line that there is a crack in everything and that is how the light gets in is shared as a message embracing imperfection as a source of light.

- Participants are guided to sense how remembering these messages could bring greater naturalness, open-heartedness, and freedom as they move forward in service.

### Practical Guidance for Teaching with Presence and Compassion

- Finding something positive or important in every question asked by a student is recommended as a way to create a field of respect and mindful compassion from the very first response.

- Speaking personally and sharing one's own ongoing practice, including struggles, is encouraged as a way to help students relax and feel that they are genuinely in it together with the teacher.

- Aligning with one's highest or most loving intention before teaching, and invoking the lineage of teachings rather than the personal self, is described as a way to feel supported and reduce ego-driven teaching.

- Practicing loving-kindness before and during teaching, including visualizing students as young children to access their original innocence, is offered as a practical method for opening the heart.

- The role of the teacher is clarified as creating conditions within which students' own attention and compassionate awareness can do the healing, rather than fixing or changing students directly.

- Unconditional love is described, drawing on Ram Dass, as making contact with the place in students that is beyond changing conditions, which is pure loving awareness.

### Commitments to Ongoing Growth and Development as a Teacher

- Teaching is framed not as a completed achievement but as an ongoing practice requiring continued dedication, retreat attendance, and a beginner's mind.

- Additional training in trauma-informed approaches, somatic experiencing, EMDR, diversity and equity and inclusion, Insight Dialogue, internal family systems, and holotropic breathwork is recommended.

- Gathering stories, poems, and insights from a wide range of sources is encouraged as a way to enrich teaching and stay connected to the broader human adventure.

- Staying vulnerable, playing one's edge, and being willing to say I do not know are presented as marks of an honest and trustworthy teacher.

- Wendell Berry's poem is cited to reframe not knowing as the beginning of real work and genuine journey rather than as a failure of expertise.

### Reflective Journaling Exercise on Nurturing Teaching Capacity and Commitments

- Participants are guided to reflect on and journal responses to two intertwined questions about what will most nurture their capacity to teach well and what they are ready to commit to in the next season.

- Self-care, continued learning, community support, and maintaining the channel of presence and love are identified as dimensions to consider in the reflection.

- Participants are encouraged to make concrete, honest commitments that honor the depth of work and dedication invested throughout the two-year training.

### Next Steps

- Breakout room facilitators are in place to welcome participants, support group formation, and help manage time within the small group sessions.

### Stories
**Ms. Meissner, Third Grade Teacher**
Shared by Tara Brach. Tara described her third-grade teacher Ms. Meissner, who was young and soft-spoken, passionate and kind, teaching in a poor urban city near Newark, New Jersey. Even dedicated troublemakers gave her valentines. Tara later understood that Ms. Meissner genuinely liked all of her students, believed in them, and respected them.
**The Young Traumatized Woman**
Shared by Tara Brach. About 15 years before the session, Tara met a deeply traumatized young woman who was desperate for help, would stay after class trembling, had difficulty being around people, and viewed mindfulness as her last hope. What she needed was to be told she could get better. Tara believed in her, connected her with a trauma therapist, and stayed in touch over many years. The woman now teaches at a private high school, offering meditation to teens and faculty.
**The Monk Made Abbot by Ajahn Chah**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. A monk at Ajahn Chah's monastery who had been a kind of gangster — gruff, with a big scar across his cheek, and judgmental — was made abbot of a small rural temple. Jack questioned Ajahn Chah about this choice. Ajahn Chah responded with warm eyes: "You have no idea how far he's come." The story illustrates trust in people's capacity to grow and change.
**Ram Dass on Ongoing Inner Struggles**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, referencing Ram Dass. Ram Dass described that after 40 years of meditation, prayer, and practice, he was still filled with judgment, craving, anger, and impatience — but now these were like little schmooze that came and went and did not bother him. His openness caused people who heard him teach to have their whole being relax.
**Stanislav Grof and Holotropic Breathwork**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. Jack described working for many years with colleague Stanislav Grof, who originated holotropic breathwork. At retreats of two to three hundred people, participants would breathe intensely for hours, entering every kind of state — trauma, bliss, feeling like dying, singing. Facilitators were trained to trust that what needed to open would open, illustrating the principle of creating conditions rather than fixing or healing.
