### Outline
### Introduction to Mindful Movement Session

- Presented as the final weekend of a two-year program, the session is led by Jonathan Foust, who has guided mindful movement throughout the journey.

- Participants are encouraged to treat the session as a personal practice, ignoring any instructions that do not resonate with their body and doing what works best for them.

- Observing someone doing a completely different movement is welcomed as a sign that they feel safe enough to listen to their own body.

### Guidelines for Safe and Effective Movement

- Exploring movement at no more than 75 to 80 percent of one's capacity is strongly encouraged, as this allows the breath to remain accessible and relaxation to be maintained.

- Staying within this range helps avoid triggering the body's fear or self-protection mechanism, which is activated when pushing beyond comfortable limits.

- At 75 percent capacity, subtle sensations such as what could elongate or expand become more perceptible, making the practice more nuanced and rewarding.

- Gentleness in movement is presented not as a limitation but as a dynamic and supportive approach to practice.

### Breathwork Foundations

- Continuous breathing throughout the entire session is emphasized as essential, with participants reminded not to hold their breath at any point.

- Slowing the breath down is introduced as a particularly effective technique, with inhalations and exhalations matched in length to a count of four or five.

- This balanced breathing pattern is described as invoking pratyahara, a yogic concept referring to a sense of absorption and the shifting of awareness from the outer world to the inner world.

- Keeping the breath slow, deep, and smooth is explained as a way to heighten the feedback loop between awareness and sensation.

- Life force energy, referred to by various names including Prana, chi, Holy Spirit, and Oregon, is introduced as something participants can sense through attentive breathing.

### Warm-Up Sequence Through Shaking and Jogging

- A somewhat unconventional warm-up is introduced, described as the most efficient and effective way to get energy and circulation flowing throughout the body.

- Beginning with shaking out the left hand, the movement is progressively extended up through the elbow, shoulder, and into both arms simultaneously.

- Participants are reminded to deepen their breath as the shaking intensifies, as it is common to unconsciously stop breathing during this exercise.

- Shaking is then extended to the legs, alternating between left and right, before transitioning into jogging in place.

- Exhaling with an open-jaw sound is introduced as an optional invitation to release tension and deepen the energetic release.

- A final 15-second full-body shake is offered as an opportunity to let go of anything being held onto before coming into stillness.

- Following the shake, participants are guided to turn awareness inward, observing sensations and breath as they return to balance.

### Breath of Joy Practice

- A three-part inhalation technique known as the Breath of Joy is introduced as a way to build energy and heighten internal awareness.

- The breath is taken in three distinct segments on the inhale, followed by a full release on the exhalation.

- After completing the rounds, participants are guided to close their eyes and observe the breath as it naturally finds its way back to balance.

### Standing Meditation and Lateral Spinal Movement

- Standing meditation is introduced with feet placed hip-width apart, knees slightly bent, and the backs of the knees kept soft to avoid hyperextension.

- One hand is placed on the belly and the other on the sacrum, with a gentle downward drop of the sacrum encouraged to establish grounded alignment.

- Three slow breaths are taken in this position, with particular attention given to softening the belly and lower abdomen on each exhalation.

- Arms are described as heavy drapes hanging from the shoulders, and attention is directed to detecting pulse, tingling, or vibration in the palms and fingers.

- Softening the occipital ridge at the base of the skull, relaxing the tongue, and releasing the belly are offered as additional points of relaxation.

- Arms are swept out to the sides and raised overhead, with slow counted breathing re-engaged to support the movement.

- Lateral bending of the torso to the left and right is explored, with participants guided to find 75 percent of their capacity, remaining engaged without strain.

- On the inhalation, lengthening through the spine is encouraged, while the exhalation is used to soften and release into the stretch.

- A slow-motion descent of the arms is guided with eyes closed, tracking the movement of the hands as they flow through space.

### Seated Spinal Movements

- Transitioning to a seated position, the spinal twist is introduced as the next movement, beginning with the left hand placed on the outside of the right thigh.

- A long inhalation is used to lengthen the spine, while the exhalation initiates a gentle rotation of the chin toward the left shoulder.

- Pressing the hand gently against the outside of the thigh on the exhalation deepens the twist and provides an internal massage to the organs.

- The same sequence is repeated on the opposite side, maintaining the same breath pattern of lengthening on the inhale and relaxing into the twist on the exhale.

- A forward bend is then introduced as the fifth movement of the spine, with the torso folding over the tops of the thighs and the back of the neck relaxed.

- An optional deeper variation involves opening the knees wide and allowing the torso to come between the thighs for those who find it comfortable.

- This forward bend posture is described symbolically as placing the head beneath the heart, representing surrender of the mind into pure awareness.

- Opening the jaw wide and moving it side to side during the forward bend is suggested as a way to release tension held in the jaw.

- Coming out of the posture can be done either with a flat back to protect the lower back or by curling up vertebra by vertebra with the head rising last.

### Shoulder Blade Contraction and Upper Back Strengthening

- The sixth movement of the spine is introduced as a corrective practice for the chronic slumping that occurs during prolonged sitting.

- Arms are raised overhead, the navel is drawn in toward the spine, and elbows are brought down to shoulder height with palms facing inward.

- Shoulder blades are actively contracted together and drawn slightly downward, with this engagement held as the primary focus for approximately 90 seconds.

- On the inhalation, breath is directed up under the collarbones to fill the upper lobes of the lungs, while the exhalation draws the navel in to expel stale air fully.

- This practice is explained as taking the slack out of chronically lengthened muscles in the back while simultaneously lengthening the muscles across the chest.

- Lifting the chin slightly is added as an additional refinement to deepen the benefit of the posture.

- After three final breaths, arms are stretched overhead once more before the hands float down to the lap to rest.

### Transition to Seated Meditation and Deepening Relaxation

- A final arm stretch with alternating side reaches is offered as a transition, with the body invited to move freely in whatever way feels natural given its warmed-up state.

- Palms are brought to face height with eyes closed, and the breath is allowed to flow freely without restriction.

- Attention is directed to the space between the thumbs and fingers, with the micro-movements of the hands used as an anchor for meditative focus.

- An observer perspective is introduced, with participants imagining that someone watching them could barely perceive any movement.

- Progressive relaxation is guided through the forehead, the muscles around the eyes, the tongue, the root of the throat, the jaw, the arms, the elbows, the wrists, the palms, the lower back, the buttocks, the legs, and the soles of the feet.

- Three slow breaths are taken with attention to softening the belly and lower abdomen on each exhalation.

- Participants are invited to choose a personal anchor for their practice, such as breath, sound, vibration, or the sensation in the palms of the hands.

### Open Awareness and Witnessing Practice

- Deep relaxation is paired with alert wakefulness, with participants guided to remain simultaneously soft and present.

- Intimate contact with the chosen anchor is encouraged, with attention also given to the periphery or background of awareness.

- A non-preferential awareness is introduced, described as a heightened sense of oneself as the observer rather than the observed.

- Awareness is invited to widen and broaden in all directions, opening 360 degrees and noting everything that is changing.

- Participants are guided to explore what it means to let things be, resting in a quality of allowing rather than controlling.

- A shift from a practice of doing to a practice of being is offered, with all technique released in favor of simply noticing what is present in the absence of effort.

- Slow counted breathing is reintroduced gently, with the simple awareness of breathing in and breathing out as the final anchor.

### Next Steps

- A 15-minute break is announced before the next session begins, giving participants time to transition and integrate the movement and meditation practice.

- Participants are encouraged to carry the qualities of softness, presence, and breath awareness cultivated in the session into the rest of the day.

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Pratyahara — Withdrawal of the Senses**
- Derived from the yoga tradition, pratyahara describes the movement of awareness from the outer world to the inner world
- Jonathan noted that just three or four rounds of slow, matched breathing can begin to invoke this state
- This principle is foundational to deepening meditation and is accessible through breath regulation alone
**The Witness Consciousness**
- Jonathan guided participants toward taking the seat of the witness — an observing awareness that watches sensation, breath, and feeling without preference
- This non-preferential awareness was described as a lens through which all changing phenomena are observed
- The practice of widening awareness to 360 degrees was offered as a way to inhabit this witnessing perspective
**Surrender and the Forward Bend**
- The forward bend posture was described symbolically as placing the head beneath the heart
- Jonathan named it a posture of surrender — of the mind into pure awareness
- This framing connects physical posture to a spiritual orientation of releasing mental control
**From Doing to Being**
- A key teaching offered was the invitation to move from a practice of doing to a practice of being
- In the absence of effort and technique, participants were asked: what do you feel, what do you notice?
- This points to a contemplative principle found across traditions — that presence itself is the destination, not a method
**The Muddy Water Metaphor**
- Jonathan referenced a teaching from Vipassana and mindfulness meditation: muddy water left to sit will clear as the silt settles
- He added his own variation — shaking the water first (building energy through movement) and then letting it settle produces a heightened clarity
- This metaphor illustrates the value of active warm-up before stillness practice
**Universal Life Force**
- Jonathan acknowledged that the animating energy of the body is named differently across traditions — Prana in yoga, Chi in Chinese medicine, Holy Spirit in Christianity, Orgone in Reichian thought
- By naming multiple traditions, the teaching was offered as inclusive and cross-cultural

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Physical Considerations**
- Jonathan explicitly acknowledged that participants may have physical limitations and encouraged them to ignore any instruction that does not resonate with their body
- Participants were advised to protect the lower back when coming out of the forward bend, using a flat-back press-up if needed
- The shoulder blade contraction practice was framed as a corrective for the physical effects of prolonged sitting — chronically lengthened back muscles and shortened chest muscles
- Hyperextension of the knees was flagged as something to avoid in standing postures
**Mental and Psychological Considerations**
- Jonathan noted the tendency to associate pain with meaningful experience in movement practice, and actively challenged this assumption
- The instruction to stay at 75% capacity was partly aimed at preventing the activation of the body's fear or self-protection response
- Softening the belly was repeatedly cued as both a physical and a psychological release — referencing the image of a two-year-old's undefended belly as a model of relaxed presence
- The transition from doing to being was framed as a release of mental effort and technique
**Effects on Practice**
- Building energy through vigorous shaking and jogging before settling was described as heightening sensation and awareness during subsequent meditation
- Slow, matched breathing was identified as a tool to deepen the feedback loop between awareness and sensation, supporting concentration and absorption
- The slow hand movement anchor was offered as a way to maintain alert, focused awareness while in a state of deep relaxation
