### Outline
### Opening Guided Meditation and Reflective Practice

- Participants were invited to connect with their bodies and minds by noticing emotions, touch points, and physical sensations as a way of arriving in the present moment.

- Reflection was guided back to the beginning of the two-year program, inviting participants to recall the excitement, apprehension, and doubts they had about their ability to become teachers.

- Attention was then directed toward the practicum phase of the program, asking participants to reconnect with any fears or doubts they experienced as teaching became more imminent and real.

- Growth through the teaching process was highlighted, noting that participants who reviewed their own recordings and taught multiple times were able to witness their own development firsthand.

- Participants were invited to identify a particular strength that may have surprised them and to reflect on moments where they stepped into a sense of personal empowerment throughout the program.

- The closing reflection asked participants to notice any fears arising about moving forward independently, while drawing on the evidence of past growth as a source of confidence for what lies ahead.

### Review of the Business of Mindfulness Workshop Content

- Wise Livelihood was introduced as a foundational concept rooted in the traditional understanding of not causing harm and its connection to the eight path factors of Buddhist practice.

- Bringing a practice lens to one's work was emphasized, meaning that how a business or organization is operated is itself the practice rather than something separate from it.

- Practical applications of Wise Livelihood were outlined, including ethical speech in student interactions, honest presentation of qualifications, intentional relationship with money, and mindful creation of websites and marketing materials.

- Wise Intention was presented as the foundation of Wise Effort, with the understanding that beliefs and thoughts behind intentions drive behaviors whether consciously or unconsciously.

- Participants were encouraged to explicitly set and cultivate wise intentions for their businesses, using those intentions as a North Star to guide actions and attention.

- Identifying personal strengths was discussed through multiple lenses, including feedback from practicums and cohorts, self-assessment of facilitation and coaching abilities, and an emotional assessment of what activities generate energy and enthusiasm.

- A needs assessment was also recommended as a way to understand what the community requires and where a teacher's strengths align with those needs.

- Practical nuts and bolts of starting a business were covered, including creating a business plan, choosing a business structure, obtaining insurance, building a website, marketing and content creation, and using administrative tools such as Zoom.

- Ongoing self-resourcing was addressed as a critical component, encompassing finding a mentor, joining a sangha, attending retreats, maintaining a consistent personal practice, and connecting with professional organizations and colleague networks.

- Cloud Sangha was mentioned as offering monthly groups specifically for MMTCP graduates as well as weekly immersion groups to support ongoing practice and community belonging.

### Breakout Room Sharing of Strengths

- Participants were sent into breakout groups of three for approximately three to four minutes per person to share what arose during the guided meditation, particularly around strengths, skills they wished to cultivate, and intentions for their work in the world.

- Upon returning, participants were invited to claim and vocalize their strengths, with responses including connection to humanness, normalizing others' experiences, equanimity, authenticity, creating safe space, overcoming trauma, conviction, passion, vulnerability, and holding space.

- These qualities were affirmed as the core of what participants bring as teachers, coexisting with ongoing doubts and fears rather than replacing them.

- It was noted that doubts and fears do not fully disappear but evolve over time, and that areas perceived as weaknesses often reveal themselves as strengths through the act of teaching.

### Personal Journeys of Building a Mindfulness Business

- Genevieve Trigger shared that her business began with a clear sense of personal values and a formal training program on how to start a business, which covered both visioning work and concrete financial planning such as expenses and break-even points.

- Writing a business plan was described as a valuable first step even when some sections were uncertain, because the act of committing ideas to paper initiated forward movement.

- Starting small with limited resources was emphasized, including building a website independently as a way of both offering classes and clarifying the overall business vision.

- Genevieve described her business as currently mid-journey, having grown from early sangha offerings to online retreats and a contract with a county health department, none of which she could have anticipated at the outset.

- Transitioning from a sole proprietorship to an LLC was shared as a more recent and complex step that would have been overwhelming to consider at the beginning, reinforcing the importance of incremental progress.

- Sharon Paquette emphasized the importance of getting clear on personal intentions before taking action, including understanding one's desired lifestyle, preferred collaborators, and the degree of flexibility and travel involved.

- Removing the distinction between work and practice was described as a deliberate and defining decision, contrasting with a prior corporate environment where the two were entirely separate.

- Identifying personal strengths such as empathy, organization, communication, and technology expertise was presented as essential to understanding how to serve one's intended work in the world.

- Goal-setting through a whiteboard and quarterly planning was described as a practical tool for maintaining focus without becoming overwhelmed by the full scope of what is possible.

- The hindrances of doubt, anxiety about being seen, and clinging to a particular vision were reframed not as signs that something is wrong but as invitations into practice and as signals to continue rather than pause.

- Waiting for doubt to disappear before starting was cautioned against, with the recommendation to acknowledge the doubt and move forward alongside it rather than waiting for resolution.

- An ongoing practice around the relationship with money was acknowledged by both facilitators, with Sharon noting that exploring the stories and meanings attached to money has been more useful than trying to resolve the tension entirely.

- The concept of dana was introduced as a way of holding both paid offerings and generosity-based offerings together, with each supporting the other in a sustainable model.

### Participant Question on Managing Overwhelm and Focus

- A participant described feeling overwhelmed by the volume of tasks involved in running a mindfulness business and expressed a desire to do less while attracting more people and achieving greater financial sustainability.

- Genevieve responded by framing overwhelm as an edge of practice, suggesting that the experience of having too much is itself an inquiry into balance, self-care, and what one truly wants.

- Sharon identified a personal pattern of using activity as a buffer against a subtle resistance to rest, reframing the question of what to say no to as a practice of becoming more comfortable saying yes to space and stillness.

- Accepting that some genuinely good opportunities will need to be declined was presented as an unavoidable part of building a focused and sustainable practice, with the discomfort of that process being part of the work itself.

### Participant Question on Money and Heart-Centered Work

- A participant raised the challenge of asking for money in the context of deeply personal and spiritual work, particularly when the subject matter involves trauma and the work feels more like service than commerce.

- Exploring personal and cultural stories around money was recommended as a starting point for understanding what meanings have been inherited versus consciously chosen.

- The idea that financial ease and freedom enable greater service to others was offered as a reframe, with the observation that personal suffering around money does not create solidarity with those in need.

- Holding the tension between wanting to offer freely and needing financial sustainability was described as an ongoing practice rather than a problem to be solved, and one that becomes less paralyzing when approached with curiosity rather than the expectation of resolution.

### Participant Question on Teaching Mindfulness History and Serving Underserved Communities

- A participant working with Black and Brown youth in communities with limited mental health resources raised the question of how to maintain financial sustainability while serving populations who cannot afford to pay.

- Seeking grants and writing proposals to county, state, and other organizations was recommended as a concrete strategy for receiving income while offering services to those without financial means.

- Incorporating the history and origins of mindfulness into teaching was encouraged as a way of honoring the roots of the practice, including through informational slides or brief contextual sessions.

- The distinction between secular and sacred framing was raised, with the suggestion that even when teaching in a non-Buddhist context, historical and cultural background can be woven in meaningfully.

- Meeting students where they are rather than where the teacher wants them to be was emphasized as a guiding principle, ensuring that historical context is offered in service of reducing suffering rather than as a requirement.

- The adaptive nature of Buddhist teachings across cultures and geographies was cited as a precedent for responsive, context-sensitive teaching that honors origins while remaining accessible.

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Work as Practice**
- The distinction between spiritual practice and professional work was intentionally dissolved by Sharon Paquette, who came from a corporate background where the two were entirely separate
- The invitation offered is that how one operates a business, communicates, and relates to students is itself the practice
**Wise Livelihood and the Eightfold Path**
- Wise Livelihood is not merely an ethical checkbox but a living orientation that permeates every aspect of how a mindfulness teacher operates in the world
- It connects to Wise Speech, Wise Intention, and Wise Effort as an integrated whole
**Doubt as a Spiritual Companion**
- Both teachers emphasized that doubt does not disappear with experience or mastery
- Doubt is reframed not as an obstacle but as a bell of mindfulness — a signal to return to practice
- The teaching offered is to act alongside doubt rather than waiting for its resolution
**Dana and Commerce**
- The tension between offering heart-centered work and receiving money for it was raised as a widespread and ongoing spiritual inquiry
- Dana was presented as a complementary model — generosity-based offerings alongside paid work — rather than an either/or choice
- The teaching that one cannot be poor enough to feed others was cited as a reframe of the relationship between financial stability and service
**Cultural Integrity in Secular Mindfulness**
- A significant societal concern was raised about Western mindfulness programs charging high fees while stripping the practice of its Eastern origins
- The call was made for teachers to include the history and roots of mindfulness in their teaching
- The tension between accessibility, cultural honesty, and the pressures of capitalism was named as an active and unresolved challenge in the field
**Empowerment Through Action**
- The consistent teaching across the session was that stepping into one's strengths happens through doing, not through preparation or the resolution of fear
- The practicum experience was cited as evidence: teachers who doubted themselves grew visibly through the act of teaching
**Rest as Practice**
- Sharon Paquette identified a subtle resistance to rest and space as an underlying driver of overactivity
- Rest and the creation of space were reframed as active spiritual practices, not absences of practice
