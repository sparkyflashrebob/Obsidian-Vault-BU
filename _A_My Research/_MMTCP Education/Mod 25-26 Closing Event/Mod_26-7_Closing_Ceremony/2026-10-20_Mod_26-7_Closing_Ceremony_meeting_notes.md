# Mod 26-7 Closing Ceremony - Oct, 20

### Mod 26-7 Closing Ceremony
📅 October 20, 2026 — 2:04 PM
👥 Jack Kornfield, Tara Brach
Tags: Closing Ceremony, Bodhisattva Vow, Forgiveness, Compassion, Loving Awareness, Impermanence, Ritual, Teacher Training, Sangha, Gratitude, Sacred Intention, Buddhist Practice, Mindfulness
### Outline
### Honoring Loss and Those Who Have Passed

- Acknowledgment was made that over the two years of the program, many changes and losses occurred both within the community and in participants' personal lives.

- Losses from the pandemic were recognized as part of the collective grief held by the group.

- Specific mention was made of Janice Gates, a mentor in the program, as one who passed during this time and whose spirit remains woven into the community's ongoing work.

- A teaching from a Tibetan retreat master was shared, emphasizing that during long periods of practice, births and deaths occur among those close to practitioners, and one must become a place of compassion that holds it all.

- Participants were invited to hold those who have died in their hearts, understanding that carrying their spirits forward is a way of honoring them.

### Water Pouring Ritual for the Departed

- A traditional ritual of pouring water from one vessel to another was introduced as a symbolic act representing the movement of life from one form to another.

- The pouring of water was described as a way to send blessings, love, and strength to those who have departed or been lost.

- Participants were asked to pick up a bowl or cup of water and reflect on those they have lost or on changes that have occurred around them.

- As participants poured, a chant was offered to accompany the ritual, reinforcing the sense of impermanence and the continuity of loving awareness.

- The teaching offered was that all things are impermanent, and that practitioners can embody loving awareness as the great heart that holds all of life.

### Forgiveness Practice and Mutual Acknowledgment

- A ritual of forgiveness was introduced as one of the most powerful practices known during times of transition, aimed at clearing the way for more freely expressed love and connection.

- The purpose of the practice was explained as dissolving unconscious armoring that prevents a felt sense of unity and deep connection among practitioners.

- In the Buddhist tradition, it is customary at the end of a period of communal practice for teachers to bow and ask forgiveness from the community, stepping beyond their roles to meet heart to heart.

- Forgiveness was asked from participants for any pain caused, intentionally or unintentionally, through words, thoughts, or actions, and for any lack of sensitivity expressed during the program.

- Forgiveness was also extended to participants for any ways they may have caused pain, establishing a mutual field of a vast and forgiving heart.

- Participants were encouraged to also offer forgiveness to themselves for their own imperfections, allowing the armoring between self and others to dissolve.

### Setting Sacred Intentions and Bodhisattva Vows

- Participants were guided to reflect on what matters most as they move forward, with the understanding that remembering one's deepest values brings a sense of homecoming and essence.

- The Bodhisattva prayer was introduced as a framework for intention, with the aspiration that all actions, thoughts, and words spring forth from love and create more love in the world.

- Participants were invited to identify their own sacred intention in moving forward as trained teachers, keeping the language simple and resonant with their deepest commitments to heart and awareness.

- A few minutes of silence were offered for personal reflection, followed by an invitation to write down a simple remembrance of one's vow.

- The act of writing the vow was framed as a commitment to living from the essence of who one is, grounded in awareness and compassion.

### Blessing Cord Ritual and the Three Knots

- Participants were asked to bring out a cord, traditionally red, as a physical symbol of the thread that weaves through all life and connects practitioners to their commitments.

- The red cord was described as carrying the symbolism of a thread from the robe of a monastic, representing a life dedicated to compassion even while living in the world.

- Three knots were tied into the cord, each representing a distinct dimension of commitment to be carried forward.

- The first knot represented refuge or connection to one's deepest spiritual source, with the intention to remain connected to that source throughout daily life.

- The second knot represented compassion and the resolve to live with honesty, integrity, and care, actively working to alleviate suffering and protect the treasure of life.

- The third knot represented the personal vow or sacred intention reflected upon during the earlier journaling exercise, tied as a joyful commitment to love, remembrance, and awakening.

- The cords were described as protection cords, with the teaching that they protect practitioners primarily from themselves by keeping inspiration, intention, and compassion present.

- Participants were given the choice to wear the cord around their wrist or neck, or to keep it in whatever way felt meaningful as a personal ritual object.

### Small Group Sharing of Vows in Triads

- Participants were invited to break into small groups of three for approximately eight minutes to share the vows they had written during the earlier reflection.

- The sharing was framed as a tender and intimate act, drawing on the Sufi concept of sobat, the conversation of the heart, where one speaks and listens from the heart.

- Each participant was asked to read their sacred intention aloud within their triad, giving voice to their commitment as they go forth as teachers.

- Time was also made available within the triads for any additional connection or closing conversation before returning to the full group.

### Candle Lighting and Collective Recitation of Vows

- Upon returning from small groups, participants were invited to hold a candle, or to visualize one, as a symbol of the luminosity that is their true nature.

- The candle was described as connecting each participant to the more than two thousand others who have gone through the two-year training, all sharing in the same aspiration.

- A collective recitation of vows was led, with all participants giving voice together to intentions including being a lamp in the darkness, medicine for hearts and spirits, and a raft or bridge across troubled waters.

- The vows included a commitment to carry teachings of compassion, forgiveness, loving kindness, wisdom, and mindfulness into the world for the benefit of all beings.

- The act of reciting together was described as a felt experience of unity, with each person's light and learning being carried outward into the world.

### Closing Gratitude and Dedication of Merit

- Gratitude was expressed on behalf of the entire group toward the largely invisible holders of the program, including the staff at Sounds True, for their dedication, skill, and care throughout the training.

- Deep acknowledgment was offered to teacher mentors, guest teachers, the DEIA team including past and current members, and the new alumni association leaders.

- Mutual gratitude was expressed between the co-teachers Jack Kornfield and Tara Brach for their shared dedication and friendship throughout the program.

- Profound gratitude was extended to all participants for their dedication, goodness, great heart, and willingness to serve the world.

- A closing prayer was offered inviting participants to bring their palms together and feel the collective fruits of their learning, connecting, and serving together.

- The dedication of merit included aspirations for all beings everywhere to realize their essence as loving awareness, to be happy, to know natural joy and peace, and for growing justice and compassion on the planet.

- The closing prayer concluded with the aspiration that all beings everywhere may awaken and be free.

### Next Steps

- Graduates were recognized as trained teachers ready to carry their practice, vows, and teachings out into the world.

- The closing was framed not as an ending but as the beginning of a new chapter and ongoing shared adventure among all participants and teachers.

- Participants were encouraged to trust that the paths of all those in the community remain woven together beyond the formal conclusion of the program.

- The blessing cords created during the ceremony were intended to serve as ongoing physical reminders of each graduate's refuge, compassion, and sacred intention as they move forward in their teaching roles.

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Impermanence as a Foundation of Practice**
- The teaching that all things are impermanent was woven throughout the ceremony as a lived reality, not merely a concept
- The Tibetan retreat tradition illustrates that practitioners must develop the capacity to hold grief and loss inwardly, becoming a place of compassion rather than being destabilized by change
**Forgiveness as a Structural Practice in Community**
- Forgiveness was presented not as a personal emotional act but as a communal ritual that dissolves the residual armoring between people
- The tradition of teachers bowing to students and asking forgiveness models a non-hierarchical, heart-centered relationship between teacher and student
- Forgiveness of self was included as equally important, acknowledging that practitioners carry their own imperfections into the world
**The Bodhisattva Path as Active Engagement**
- The Bodhisattva vow was framed as the heart of the path — a conscious commitment to act from love and to create more love in the world
- Going forth as a trained teacher was described as carrying the perfume of the temple into the marketplace, meaning that spiritual commitment is expressed through everyday life and service
**Sacred Objects as Anchors for Intention**
- The blessing cord was presented as a physical reminder of inner commitments — refuge, compassion, and vow — that can be worn in daily life
- The candle was used as a symbol of the practitioner's own luminosity and true nature, making the inner light visible and shareable
**Interconnection and the Web of Life**
- The water-pouring ritual and the cord ritual both emphasized the continuity of connection — between the living and the departed, between teachers and students, and among all beings
- The closing dedication of merit expressed the understanding that individual practice has collective benefit, extending to all beings everywhere
**Gratitude as a Spiritual Practice**
- Tara Brach described gratitude as one of the sweetest experiences on the path
- The formal acknowledgment of invisible contributors — staff, mentors, DEIA team — modeled the practice of seeing and honoring those whose work sustains community

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to a friend who trained as a Tibetan Lama and completed a three-year, three-month, three-day retreat largely in silence
- The retreat master told participants at the outset that births and deaths would occur during the retreat, including among those close to them, and that they must become a place of compassion
- Jack Kornfield's friend experienced the death of a family member during the retreat but was unable to leave, and so had to hold the grief inwardly

**The Lama and the Protection Cord**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to a great Lama who was asked what protection cords truly protect one from
- The Lama's answer: "Why yourself, of course" — meaning the cords serve as reminders of one's own refuge, compassion, and intention, protecting against one's own misconduct and forgetfulness
