👥 **Main Teachers/Facilitators:** La Sarmiento (they/them) and Allison Cohen (she/her/hers), co-facilitating; both have been mentors in MMTCP for four years. **Others involved:** Steve Kaplan, Lindsay, Debbie Germino, India, Berta, Dawn, and approximately 190 participants total.

### Outline
### Session Overview and Purpose

- Participants were welcomed to a closing retreat breakout session titled "If You Want to Sing Out, Sing Out," co-facilitated by La Sarmiento and Allison Cohen as part of the MMTCP program.

- Approximately 190 participants joined the session, which was framed as an opportunity to explore the integration of music, song, and chant into personal practice, teaching practice, and life practice.

- The session was designed to strengthen the two wings of wisdom and compassion, supporting practitioners in inhabiting their lives with greater wisdom, compassion, and joy.

- Due to the breadth of content available on this topic, facilitators acknowledged that there was enough material for a full retreat, and encouraged participants to focus on identifying one key takeaway and one thing to experiment with after the session.

- A resource list including articles, tips, and all songs referenced during the session was promised to participants via a Google document link shared in the chat, with a request for MMTCP to email the link to all participants including those not present in real time.

### Grounding and Settling Practice

- Participants were invited to find a comfortable posture, whether sitting, standing, or lying down, and to fully arrive in the present moment together.

- Attention was directed to the weight of the body on the surface beneath it, with an invitation to imagine the unconditional support of the earth holding all beings, as a reminder of interconnectedness.

- Participants were guided to bring to mind loved ones, family, friends, pets, and communities that had supported them over the previous two years of the program.

- Acknowledgment was offered to wise and bright ancestors, both blood and cultural, known and unknown, upon whose shoulders practitioners stand.

- Honoring was extended to Asian ancestors from whom the teachings and practices of mindfulness and the Dharma have been preserved and passed down for centuries.

- Attention was then brought to the collective gathering of practitioners completing MMTCP, with appreciation directed toward peer groups, mentor groups, mentors, teachers Jack and Tara, and all Sounds True staff.

- The practice concluded by grounding participants back in the body and breath, with a sense of dignity in posture, before returning attention to the Zoom session.

### La Sarmiento's Personal Journey with Music, Song, and Dharma Teaching

- Music was described as a lifelong tool for expression, processing, and healing, with songs serving as a means of accessing emotions that thoughts and words alone cannot reach.

- La's entry into mindfulness practice came through a painful personal experience, with early practice rooted primarily in silent retreat, using the RAIN framework to recognize, allow, investigate, nurture, and non-identify with present experience.

- A personal practice of writing spoof songs was introduced as an accessible and creative alternative to formal Dharma talks, earning La the informal title of the "Weird Al Yankovic of the Dharma."

- An original spoof song written in honor of Tara Brach's book "True Refuge," set to the tune of "American Pie" by Don McLean, was performed and shared with participants as an example of this approach.

- Additional spoof songs were referenced, including one set to the tune of "I Want to Know What Love Is" reframed around the RAIN practice, and another set to "Breaking Up Is Hard to Do" reframed around the difficulty of freeing oneself from habitual thought patterns.

- Exposure to the tradition of Thich Nhat Hanh through a partner's sangha introduced La to the practice of singing songs based on Thich Nhat Hanh's poetry, which served as a reminder of his teachings, exemplified by a breathing meditation song performed during the session.

- Attendance at a retreat with teachers Thanissara and Kittisaro deepened La's engagement with Buddhist chanting, which was described as incorporating teachings within the chant itself while transmitting healing vibration and fostering collective voice within the Sangha.

- Chanting was presented as a highly portable practice that can be integrated into daily activities such as walking, cooking, and other routine tasks.

- The chant OM MANI PADME, translating to "The jewel is in the lotus," was shared as a practice that reconnects practitioners with their innate Buddha nature, goodness, wisdom, and compassion.

- During the pandemic, music and videos were incorporated into online retreat teaching to enliven the screen-based experience and to emphasize teaching points, with a metta-inspired video shared as an example of wishes extended to participants completing MMTCP.

- A Dharma songbook containing all of La's spoof songs was offered as a freely shareable resource, along with links to music and videos used at retreats, a recording of an early Wednesday night class featuring the songs in context, Thich Nhat Hanh's Dharma songs, and Buddhist chants from Thanissara and Kittisaro.

- Songs and chants were described as tools for remembering teachings that alleviate suffering and bring true happiness, given the human tendency to constantly forget and re-remember these teachings.

### Allison Cohen's Personal Journey with Music, Song, and Dharma Teaching

- Allison's path to practice began during a psychologically and emotionally difficult period in college, described as coming to the practice through the gateway of suffering, during which a seed was planted that another way of being in the world was possible.

- The seed began to blossom during a demanding period as a high school teacher in the Brooklyn Public Schools, when the cumulative weight of internal struggles, professional demands, and family responsibilities made the existing way of living feel unsustainable.

- On the advice of a colleague, Allison attended a young adult meditation retreat at Spirit Rock despite having minimal prior meditation experience, and found the week to be transformative.

- At the close of that retreat, after a full week of silence, participants were invited to walk around the room, look into each other's eyes, and bow while listening to a song, an experience that proved unexpectedly powerful.

- The song, "A Song for All Beings" by Jennifer Berezan, performed by a large ensemble, was searched for and found after the retreat, and was described as providing a deep sense of being seen and summarizing the kind of person Allison aspired to be.

- Music was identified as a particularly effective gateway to metta and loving kindness practice, with the experience of being bathed in song by a community of practitioners allowing access to the felt sense of loving kindness more readily than reciting phrases alone.

- Practical applications of music in teaching were described, including listening to or singing a song before formal sitting, walking, or standing practice, and playing music as participants arrive at a teaching session to support settling and inspiration.

- A teaching from Rabbi Bunim was shared, describing every human being as metaphorically wearing a coat with two pockets, one containing the note "I am but a speck of dust and to dust I will return," and the other containing the note "For my sake, the world was created."

- The teaching was framed as a practice of wisdom and compassion, with the challenge being to know which note is needed in any given moment and to cultivate the capacity to hold both truths simultaneously.

- A song by singer-songwriter Ali Halpert, discovered on Spotify, was shared as a musical rendering of this exact teaching, and was described as something Allison listens to frequently, often before meditation.

### Participant Questions and Facilitator Responses

- A question was raised about the appropriate way to share indigenous songs, chants, or language from cultures other than one's own, particularly in the context of wilderness and mindfulness teaching.

- Facilitators recommended acknowledging the cultural origin of any material being shared, learning as much as possible about it, and presenting it with explicit respect and honoring of the culture from which it comes.

- A concern was raised about the possibility of choosing a song that triggers a negative memory or emotional reaction in a participant, and whether facilitators worry about this when selecting music.

- Allison guided the questioner through a brief inquiry inviting reflection on what their most courageous and loving self would offer in response to that concern, which led the participant to articulate that clarity of intention, acknowledgment of the emotional weight songs can carry, and permission for all responses are sufficient foundations for sharing music.

- The distinction between intent and impact from the MMTCP multicultural interaction guidelines was invoked, with the reminder that integrity in responding to unintended impact is more important than avoiding all risk.

- A practical question was raised about the technological challenges of bringing sound into online teaching environments without compromising audio quality or causing discomfort for participants.

- Facilitators indicated that a compiled list of tips for sharing recorded or live music and videos through Zoom would be included in the resource document being distributed to all participants.

- A question was raised about how to find sanghas and communities that integrate music, singing, and chanting alongside mindfulness practice, as existing searches had not yielded clear results.

- The Sacred Mountain Sangha of teachers Thanissara and Kittisaro was recommended as a starting point, with their website noted as containing a dedicated section on Buddhist chants and recordings including written-out chants with pronunciation guidance.

- A question was raised about whether it is acceptable to sing even if one's voice is not considered strong or skilled.

- Facilitators affirmed that all voices are welcome, and added that giving oneself permission to sing creates a ripple effect that liberates others to do the same, particularly in a culture where fear of judgment around singing is widespread.

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Music as Spiritual Access Point**
- Music creates access to the heart and body in ways that thoughts and words alone cannot, making it a powerful vehicle for spiritual practice and teaching
- Songs and chants help practitioners constantly remember teachings that alleviate suffering and bring true happiness, since forgetting and remembering is a natural cycle in practice
**Chanting as Healing Vibration**
- Buddhist chanting transmits a vibration that is healing in addition to conveying teachings
- The chant OM MANI PADME HUM serves as a continuous reminder of Buddha nature — innate goodness, wisdom, and compassion — and can be practiced in any setting at any time
**Humor and Creativity in Dharma Teaching**
- Spoof songs offer an accessible, joyful, and memorable way to transmit Dharma teachings
- Teaching out of the box through music and song can strengthen both the wisdom and compassion wings of practice
**The Two Wings of Practice**
- Music, song, and chant are described as strengthening the two wings that support inhabiting life with greater wisdom, compassion, and joy
**Interconnectedness and Common Ground**
- La's grounding meditation referenced the earth as common ground reminding all beings of their interconnectedness
- The gathering of 190 practitioners from around the world was itself framed as a manifestation of sangha and shared aspiration
**Honoring Lineage and Ancestors**
- La's opening meditation explicitly honored wise and bright ancestors, both blood and cultural, known and unknown
- Specific acknowledgment was made of Asian ancestors who preserved and passed down mindfulness and Dharma teachings for centuries
**The Two Pockets Teaching — Humility and Sacred Worth**
- The Rabbi Bunim teaching presents a framework for holding both human smallness and irreplaceable sacred worth simultaneously
- Wisdom and compassion practice is what gives a person access to both truths when needed
- This teaching bridges Jewish spiritual tradition and mindfulness practice, illustrating the universality of these insights
**Cultural Sensitivity in Spiritual Teaching**
- When drawing from traditions not one's own, teachers are encouraged to acknowledge the source, learn deeply, and speak with respect and honor
- The distinction between intent and impact from MMTCP's multicultural interaction guidelines was cited as relevant to all teaching decisions, including music selection
**Permission and Liberation Through Voice**
- One person liberating their own voice in song creates a ripple effect of permission for others
- Fear of singing is widespread in contemporary culture due to concerns about perception, and teachers can model courage by singing regardless
**Closing Celebration as Practice**
- The dance party at the end of retreats is framed as a practice in itself — a way to celebrate effort, practice, and sangha through embodied movement
