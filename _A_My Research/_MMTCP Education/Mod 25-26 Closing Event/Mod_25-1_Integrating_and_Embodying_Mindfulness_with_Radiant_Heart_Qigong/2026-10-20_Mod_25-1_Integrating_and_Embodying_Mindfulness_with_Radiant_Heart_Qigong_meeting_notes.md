### Outline
### Welcome and Introduction to the Session

- Congratulations were extended to the participants on completing the MMTCP program and becoming certified mindfulness teachers.

- Today's session was outlined to include standing Qigong practice, seated practice, and a closing meditation.

- Participants were informed that they could remain seated or lie down if standing practice was not appropriate for their bodies.

### Standing Meditation and Wu Chi Posture

- Standing meditation posture, referred to as Wu Chi, was introduced as a foundational starting point for the practice, meaning "without boundary."

- Alignment in this posture was described as both structural and energetic, emphasizing openness and fluidity rather than rigidity.

- Participants were guided to feel the connection through the bottoms of their feet and to allow the breath to settle naturally into the abdomen.

- A sense of balance between heaven and earth was described as the intended quality of this standing posture.

### Radiant Body Breathing Practice

- The first moving Qigong practice introduced was Radiant Body Breathing, designed to promote natural circulation, openness, and clarity throughout the whole system.

- Participants were guided to allow the hands to flow outward with the in-breath and downward along the front of the body with the exhale, creating a soothing and smoothing effect.

- A complementary variation was introduced where the hands moved up the central column of the body and then flowed outward with the exhale.

- Whole-body breathing was emphasized, with attention directed to the palms of the hands, the bottoms of the feet, and the crown of the head as points of energetic awareness.

- A period of stillness following the practice was described as essential for building resilience and allowing the benefits to integrate through the body.

### Pulsing from the Core Practice

- A practice called Pulsing from the Core was introduced as particularly beneficial for the brain and nervous system.

- Participants were guided to fold slightly forward, rounding the back and relaxing the hips and pelvis to create a natural stretch in the lower back.

- With each in-breath, the shoulders were gently lifted and rolled back, and with each exhale, the sacral and lumbar area was allowed to gently open and pulse.

- This movement was described as nourishing and restorative, working through the spinal cord and into the brain through gentle, fluid motion.

- A complementary movement was introduced to open the front line of the body, creating an elliptical flow up the back, over the crown of the head, and melting down the front.

### The Equinox Spinal Spiraling Practice

- A practice called the Equinox was introduced, centered on a gentle spinal spiral from the base of the spine upward through the body.

- Participants were reminded to underdo range of motion, a key principle of embodied mindfulness in Qigong, and to avoid pushing into pain or resistance.

- The four natural phases of the breath were incorporated into this practice, including the in-breath, a brief natural pause, the exhalation, and a second natural pause.

- Hands were positioned in front of the hara at approximately solar plexus height, expanding with the in-breath and spiraling to the side with the exhale, with one hand pressing downward and the other extending outward.

- Bringing mindful presence to the movement was described as allowing the benefits to move more completely and deeply through the entire system.

### Yin Yang Breathing and Opening and Closing Practice

- A final standing practice called Yin Yang Breathing and Opening and Closing was introduced as a simple but effective practice for the heart and lung meridians.

- Participants were guided to extend the palms outward and open them upward with the in-breath, expanding across the chest and arms, and then bring the backs of the hands inward with the exhale to naturally bow the spine.

- This practice was noted to release the space between the shoulder blades and cultivate a natural fullness of breath and openness through the heart and lung channels.

- A return to standing meditation posture followed, with attention directed to the subtle circulation of energy through the body with clarity rather than amplitude.

### Seated Dao Yin and Spinal Spiraling Practice

- Participants transitioned to seated practice, beginning with a period of quiet settling before moving into seated Dao Yin.

- The seated spinal spiraling practice mirrored the standing version, moving from the base of the spine upward through the thoracic area and finally releasing the head gently to the side.

- One hand was drawn back with the elbow out to the side while the other crossed the body during the spiral, with the movement initiated from the base rather than the head.

- A distinction was drawn between times for pure, clear mindfulness presence and times for cultivation, with both being valid and complementary aspects of contemplative practice.

- Students were reminded to encourage their own future students to listen to their bodies first and not attempt to match any particular range of motion.

### Seated Meditation and Cultivation of Breath Qualities

- Following the Dao Yin, participants were guided into seated meditation beginning with a cultivation of specific breath qualities including softness, quietness, smoothness, and relative evenness.

- Softness was clarified as not limpness but rather a quality of openness through every cell of the body.

- After cultivating these qualities, attention was gently released from the breath and opened to the full range of sense gateways including sound, sight, taste, smell, and bodily sensation.

- Participants were then invited to rest in what was described as the Deep Witness, the ever-present, deathless, timeless awareness behind all phenomena.

- This quality of awareness was described by various names across traditions including awareness of awareness, deep presence, the Tao, and Wu Chi, all pointing to the same unnameable ground of being.

### Discussion on Teaching Qigong to Special Populations

- Marlene, a yoga teacher and recreation therapy supervisor working in a nursing home and hospital for ten years, expressed interest in learning Qigong to teach to wheelchair-bound patients with limited mobility.

- Qigong was highlighted as particularly well-suited for populations who may find other practices such as yoga physically too challenging, while still being potent and effective.

- Tejah Bell affirmed that even a small number of basic practices such as Radiant Body Breathing and standing alignment work are sufficient to offer meaningful benefit to students.

- The importance of the teacher embodying the practices personally was emphasized, with the principle that entraining the natural states in one's own nervous system is the foundation of effective teaching.

- Participants were encouraged to approach teaching as guiding rather than delivering cognitive knowledge, focusing on felt experience and somatic presence.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Wu Chi — Standing Meditation Posture**
- Described as "without boundary," a sense of settling and balance between heaven and earth
- Alignment is both structural and energetic, not rigid but open and fluid
- Arms slightly out to the side, breathing through palms of hands, bottoms of feet, crown of the head
- Serves as the returning posture between all moving practices
**Radiant Body Breathing**
- Hands flow out to the side with the in-breath, palms face inward and flow down with the exhale
- Complementary variation: hands rise up the central column and flow out to the side on the exhale
- Described as soothing, smoothing, and promoting natural circulation, openness, clarity, and natural boundaries
- Whole body breathing — everything moving in an easy and gentle way
**Pulsing from the Core**
- Begins with a forward fold, rounding the back, inclining the backs of the hands
- In-breath lifts the shoulders back; exhale settles the palms down, relaxing the sacral-lumbar area
- Complementary action opens the front line of the body — heart, sternum, chest, abdominal area
- Movement described as an ellipse flowing up the back and over the top of the head, melting down the front
- Described as nourishing, healing, and restorative for the spinal cord and brain
**The Equinox Practice**
- Spinal spiraling movement, turning lightly to the side
- Spiral originates from the base of the spine upward, completing with the head turning to the side
- Hands extend to the side; one hand presses down as the other extends, like opening a door
- Coordinated with the four phases of the breath
- Principle of underdoing range of motion emphasized
**Yin Yang Breathing and Opening and Closing**
- Hands extend with palms facing, then open out with palms up — opens heart and lung meridians
- Backs of hands incline on the closing, settling the spine and bringing the chin in
- Releases space between shoulder blades; cultivates openness through the heart and lung channels
**Seated Dao Yin**
- Seated version of the spinal spiraling practice
- One hand draws back with elbow out, shoulder relaxed; in-breath taken before spiraling across the body
- Movement leads from the base of the body upward, not from the head
- Followed by an integrating breath with hands pressing face downward, then turning over and up
**Cultivation of Breath Qualities**
- Qualities cultivated: softness, quietness, smoothness, relative evenness, natural fullness
- Softness described as openness through every cell, not limpness
- After cultivation, attention is gently released and mindfulness presence is brought to the natural rhythm of the breath
**Mindfulness and the Sense Gateways**
- Attention brought to sounds close by and far away
- Presence of form, color, shape through the sense gateway of sight
- Bodily tactile sensation held with ease and openness
**The Deep Witness**
- Described as that which is behind, always present, deathless, clear, timeless
- Also referred to as awareness of awareness, deep presence, the Tao, Wu Chi
- Settling in mindfulness practice provides the ground for letting go into being
**Teaching Qigong to Special Populations**
- Marlene works in a nursing home and hospital as recreation therapy supervisor for 10 years
- Identified Qigong as ideal for residents in wheelchairs with limited movement
- Tejah Bell confirmed Qigong is accessible and potent even for those who find yoga too physically challenging
