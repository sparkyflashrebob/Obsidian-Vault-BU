### Mod 05-3 Mindful Movement in the Context of Meditation

📅 October 16, 2026

👥 Main Teacher: Jonathan Foust

Tags: Mindful Movement, Meditation, Yoga, Dharana, Breathwork, Body Awareness, Retreat Teaching, Stress Relief, Concentration, Impermanence

#### Outline

#### Introduction to Mindful Movement in the Context of Meditation

- Explored in this session are different kinds of movement flows and how mindful movement can be effectively used within a meditation context.
- Guiding participants toward their physical edge is described as a real art, with the recommendation to stay within 70 to 75% of capacity to avoid strain.
- Referenced is the yoga concept of finding the place "where it hurts good," meaning the point where breath and awareness can open something up without causing harm.
- Slow motion movement is introduced as a powerful absorption and concentration technique designed to focus the mind during practice.

#### Guided Seated Mountain Pose and Hand Energization Exercise

- A seated mountain pose is introduced, with participants encouraged to sit strong and scoot forward from the back of their chair.
- Shaking out the hands is used as the opening movement to bring energy and awareness into the hands before the main exercise.
- Participants are guided to extend their arms with one hand up and one hand down, alternating between squeezing and lengthening the fists.
- Resting the hands three inches above the thighs follows the fist exercise, with attention directed toward tingling, pulse, vibration, or subtle energy in the hands.
- Coherence breathing is introduced at this stage, aiming for a slow count of five on both the inhalation and exhalation to create smooth, continuous breath.

#### Overhead Arm Hold and Slow Motion Descent

- Arms are guided to open out to the sides and rise until the hands are directly above the shoulders, with continuous slow and flowing breath maintained throughout.
- Pressing down through the floor of the pelvis and drawing the navel slightly toward the spine are offered as ways to support the posture during the hold.
- Sensations such as a building energy or mild burning in the hands are described as natural, with the option to lower the arms partially or fully if the intensity becomes too great.
- Releasing control of the breath and allowing it to become effortless marks the transition into the slow motion descent phase.
- An ultra slow motion lowering of the hands is guided, with the micro-movements of the hands moving through space serving as the primary anchor of attention.
- Moment-to-moment awareness of the hands is maintained whether they are still in transit or have come to rest, allowing a natural drawing inward of awareness.

#### Silent Reflection and Closing Inquiry

- Several minutes of shared silence follow the movement exercise, during which participants are invited to inquire what could relax, soften, or let go in that moment.
- Participants are then invited to identify two words that most accurately describe their inner experience as a way to bring gentle awareness to their present state.
- Deepening the breath and allowing the head to drift gently side to side are offered as ways to transition out of the stillness and return to ordinary awareness.

#### Adapting Movement Flows for Different Populations and Contexts

- A gentler variation of the shaking exercise is described for highly stressed participants, such as those arriving directly from work, where slow motion alone is used without any shaking.
- Dharana, or concentration practice, is identified as the underlying mechanism by which slow motion movement calms and soothes the mind by giving it a focused task.
- Combining mindful movement with meditation is described as particularly effective for stressed populations, as releasing gross physical tension first helps deliver people into subtler states of awareness.
- A class format of mindful movement flows followed by a lying down body scan and a 30-minute sit is presented as a highly effective structure for guiding participants into deep meditative states.
- Movement is also highlighted as a powerful tool for creating a shared group experience, as it collects collective attention and serves as a strong segue into meditation.
- Retreat settings are identified as especially well-suited for integrating movement, as sustained practice over time allows participants to directly experience impermanence through gross physical movement.
- Some participants are noted to have reported that they intellectually understood meditation teachings but only truly grasped them through mindful movement practice.

#### Teaching from Direct Experience Versus Teaching from a Script

- Teaching from an internalized, felt sense of a practice is described as fundamentally different from memorizing and delivering a script.
- Practicing a flow from the inside is emphasized as the foundation for authentic teaching, as it allows the teacher to guide from a place of genuine understanding.
- Different learning styles are acknowledged, with some participants benefiting most from direct experience, others from hearing peers' experiences, and others from asking clarifying questions.
- A mix of direct experiential practice and discussion is described as the intended approach for the course to ensure participants develop both embodied understanding and teaching clarity.

#### Closing Guided Meditation

- A brief closing meditation is led, beginning with a complete and effortless relaxation of the breath.
- Smoothing the forehead, relaxing the tongue so it fills the lower jaw, and releasing the root of the tongue down into the throat are offered as specific points of softening.
- Relaxing the jaw mechanism as a whole, softening the lower back and belly, and feeling the palms and soles of the feet are included as further anchors of awareness.
- A final inquiry into what could still relax or soften is offered before participants are invited to notice the quality of presence and gently return by deepening the breath and opening the eyes.

#### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to practice the flows directly in order to develop an embodied understanding that can inform their own teaching.

### Detailed Subject Matter

**The Edge of Capacity in Movement Practice**

- Jonathan describes guiding students to approximately 70–75% of their capacity
- The yoga concept of going to the place "where it hurts good" — the edge where breath and attention can open something up
- Strain signals going too far; the edge is where sensation is present but manageable

**Seated Mountain Pose and Hand Energization Exercise**

- Participants are guided to sit forward in their chairs, away from the back support
- Hands are shaken out to build energy, imagined as moths moving around
- Fists are squeezed and lengthened with alternating hand positions
- Hands are brought to rest three inches above the thighs to feel tingling, pulse, or vibration
- Arms are raised overhead with attention on breath and sensation in the hands
- Thumbs, fingers, and palms are progressively relaxed before the slow motion descent begins

**Coherence Breathing**

- Participants are guided to breathe at a slow count of five on inhalation and five on exhalation
- Breath is described as smooth, continuous, and flowing
- Navel is drawn slightly toward the spine; the floor of the pelvis is engaged

**Slow Motion as Dharana**

- The ultra-slow descent of the hands is used as a concentration anchor
- Attention is directed to micro-movements of the hands as they move through space
- An observer would barely perceive movement — this is the standard offered
- Concentration calms and soothes the mind; it gives the "puppy dog mind" a job to do
- This technique is identified as dharana, a powerful absorption method

**Adapting Movement for Different Groups**

- For children: getting energy going first, then creating a dramatic shift into absorption
- For stressed office workers: a gentler variation without shaking, beginning directly with slow motion
- For retreat settings: movement helps sustain experience over longer periods
- For scattered individuals: movement provides a necessary focal point before sitting practice

**Movement as a Teaching Tool**

- Movement collects group attention and creates a shared experience
- It serves as a powerful segue into meditation
- Combining mindful movement flows, lying-down body scan, and a 30-minute sit was described as an effective class structure
- A short dharma talk at the end of class was also mentioned

**Teaching from Inner Experience**

- Jonathan emphasizes practicing a flow from the inside to teach from that place
- Teaching from inner experience is described as fundamentally different from memorizing a script

**Impermanence Through Movement**

- Retreat participants have reported that mindful movement helped them directly experience the nature of impermanence
- Seeing everything changing through gross movement made teachings on suffering and holding on more accessible

**Closing Meditation Guidance**

- Participants are guided to relax the breath completely
- Specific relaxation cues include: smoothing the forehead, relaxing the tongue to fill the lower jaw, relaxing the root of the tongue, softening the lower back and belly, and feeling the palms and soles of the feet
- A final inquiry: noticing what could relax or soften, and noticing the quality of presence

### Spiritual and Societal Insights

**Meditation May Not Be the First Step for Everyone**

- Jonathan references a conversation with Gil Franzl at Spirit Rock in which Gil suggested that for many people, meditation practice may not be the most appropriate practice
- For individuals carrying a lot of gross, deep-seated tension or overstimulation, simply sitting and following the breath is described as asking a great deal
- Movement is presented as a more accessible entry point for such individuals

**A Stressed-Out Culture**

- Jonathan observes that contemporary culture is highly stressed
- People arriving from work are described as "jangled," illustrating the need for movement-based approaches before stillness practices

**Impermanence as a Lived Teaching**

- Retreat participants reported understanding teachings intellectually but not truly grasping them until they explored mindful movement
- Seeing the nature of impermanence through gross movement — everything constantly changing — made the teachings on suffering and clinging tangible and direct

**Subjectivity of Practice**

- A technique that does not work for one person may be profoundly effective for another
- Hearing another person's experience of a practice can deepen one's own understanding of its value
- This subjectivity is presented as informative and important for teachers to hold

**Dharana as a Universal Calming Mechanism**

- Concentration is described as inherently calming and soothing to the mind
- Giving the mind a specific sensory task — tracking hand sensation — is framed as working with the nature of mind rather than against it

### Stories

**Moving to Washington D.C. and Creating a New Class Format**

Jonathan Foust shares that when he moved to Washington D.C. and knew no one, he drew on his love of teaching and his background in yoga and meditation. Recognizing that people signing up for yoga were not necessarily signing up for meditation, he rebranded his offering as "mindful movement, deep relaxation, and sitting meditation." The class structure — mindful movement flows, a lying-down body scan, and a 30-minute sit followed by a short dharma talk — proved to be an incredibly powerful format for delivering people into meditative experience.

**Conversation with Gil Franzl at Spirit Rock**

Jonathan recounts a conversation approximately 15 years prior with Gil Franzl at Spirit Rock. Jonathan shared what he was doing in D.C., including his extensive background in movement and yoga. Gil responded by suggesting that for many people, meditation practice may not be the most appropriate practice. Jonathan states he has come to deeply understand and agree with this perspective over time.

**Teaching Stressed Office Workers in Arlington, Virginia**

Jonathan describes leading a regular class in Arlington, Virginia, where participants arrive directly from work in a highly stressed and scattered state. He notes that the full shaking exercise can sometimes be too much for this group, and so he uses a gentler variation — beginning directly with slow motion movement — to meet them where they are.

**Retreat Participants and the Experience of Impermanence**

Jonathan shares accounts from retreat participants who reported that while they intellectually understood teachings on impermanence and suffering, they did not truly grasp them until they engaged in mindful movement. Seeing everything constantly changing through gross physical movement gave them a direct experiential imprint of the teaching.

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice

**Physical Challenges**

- Burning sensation in the arms and hands when held overhead is acknowledged as a possible experience; participants are instructed to lower their arms if it feels like too much
- The instruction to customize — bringing arms down to rest and then raising them again fully or partially — addresses varying physical capacity among participants
- The exercise is noted as potentially challenging, described as giving "a little bit of an edge"

**Mental and Stress-Related Challenges**

- Overstimulation and gross deep-seated tension are identified as barriers to sitting meditation practice
- People arriving from stressful work environments are described as "jangled," making immediate stillness practice difficult
- The "puppy dog mind" metaphor is used to describe a scattered, restless mind that needs a concrete task to settle

**Effects on Practice**

- Slow motion movement and hand sensation awareness are presented as tools to shift the mind from scattered to absorbed states
- Moving from gross tension release into subtler states of awareness is described as a helpful progression
- For some practitioners, movement is the necessary precondition for meditation to be accessible or effective
- Subjectivity of techniques is noted — a practice that does not work for one person may be essential for another, which has implications for how teachers select and offer practices
