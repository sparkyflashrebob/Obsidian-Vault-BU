### Outline
### Overview of Racism as a Systemic Phenomenon

- Racism is defined not only as individual beliefs about people of different races but as a broader system of marginalization and oppression of people of color based on a socially constructed racial hierarchy that privileges white people.

- Systemic racism is described as a combination of systems, institutions, and factors that advantage white people while harming and disadvantaging Black, Indigenous, and people of color.

- This deeply ingrained and complex system has wide-ranging impacts on all people, and the session aims to examine its operation across multiple levels of analysis.

- Particular attention is given to how racism affects the work of mindfulness teachers, including their ability to establish meaningful relationships with both white students and students of color.

### Pause Relax Open Practice for Staying Present with Difficult Material

- A three-step mindfulness practice called Pause, Relax, Open is introduced as a tool for staying present when encountering difficult or race-related experiences.

- Originating from Insight Dialogue developed by Gregory Kramer, the practice was introduced at the East Bay Meditation Center by a mentor and is recommended for use in moments of racial tension or discomfort.

- Participants are guided to settle into a comfortable meditation posture, soften the eyes, invite a slight smile, relax the throat and belly, and notice the pressure of the body against its support.

- Relaxing the nervous system is emphasized as a foundational step before engaging with challenging content, with the technique referred to as Smile Yoga drawing on the teachings of Thich Nhat Hanh.

- When the mind wanders during practice, the instruction is to pause, settle into the present moment, invite relaxation, and then open the field of awareness.

- Signs reading Pause, Relax, Open are posted around the room during in-person sessions as a reminder to interrupt automatic, conditioned responses.

### The Four Levels of Oppression and Privilege Model

- A widely used model in diversity training called the Four Levels of Oppression and Privilege is introduced to help explain how racism and white supremacy operate across different dimensions of society.

- The four levels are identified as structural and institutional, cultural, interpersonal, and personal or intrapersonal, with personal transformation placed at the center of the model.

- Personal transformation is positioned at the center because people are the connecting link across all four levels and therefore must change their minds, hearts, and behaviors for meaningful progress to occur.

- Changing one's mind involves relinquishing white privilege and working to dismantle the myths and historical obscurations that perpetuate racial disparities.

- Changing one's heart means turning toward the pain caused by the system, listening to people of color, and believing their experiences, including acknowledging the grief felt by white people as well.

- Changing how one acts and interacts means refusing complicity through silence or inaction and responding in more constructive ways across racial divides.

- The model is presented as useful for providing a theory of change, strategizing meaningful interventions at various scales from a mindfulness class to large social institutions, and explaining the ecosystem of racial oppression.

- Each of the four levels supports, reflects, and reinforces the others, maintaining the racial status quo when left unchecked.

### The Structural and Institutional Level of Racism

- The structural and institutional level encompasses laws, regulations, and practices that may not be formally encoded in policy but nonetheless determine who holds power, who controls the narrative, and who controls the media.

- Redlining is cited as a concrete example of structural and institutional racism, with a video on the topic planned for participants to watch as part of the session.

- Housing, lending, education, and criminal justice are identified as areas deeply affected by structural racism such as redlining.

- Applied to mindfulness teaching, structural considerations include the location of classes and whether they are accessible by public transportation, the scheduling of class times and who can realistically attend, fee structures, and the expectations set for participants.

- Teachers are encouraged to reflect on these variables even when they do not have full control over them, recognizing how structural factors shape who can participate in their classes.

### The Cultural Level of Racism and White Supremacy Culture

- The cultural level is described as potentially the most significant because it can undermine positive legal and procedural changes when hearts and minds have not genuinely shifted.

- The failure to deliver the promised forty acres and a mule to freed slaves is offered as a historical example of how cultural attitudes about entitlement to Black labor and the othering of people of color overrode legal commitments, leading instead to indentured servitude and racial terror.

- White supremacy culture is clarified as not meaning that white culture is inherently bad, but rather that it becomes problematic when Anglo-Saxon Protestant or Northern European values are treated as the correct or default way of being.

- Examples of white supremacy cultural values include definitions of professionalism in dress and speech, an emphasis on efficiency over inclusion, and a product-oriented rather than process-oriented approach to work.

- These cultural values inadvertently support the racial status quo by creating environments that are difficult for people of color to navigate without adequate structural and interpersonal support.

- Diversity by the numbers is identified as a common organizational failure in which institutions recruit people of color without addressing the cultural biases or providing the supports necessary for them to thrive.

- Cultural norms are often unspoken, as illustrated by the example of knowing without being told that race was not a topic to be discussed, and these implicit norms must be actively identified and replaced.

- Mindfulness teachers are encouraged to honestly examine their mental image of an ideal student and consider whether it unconsciously excludes people who speak differently, are more expressive, or engage with the practice in culturally distinct ways.

### The Interpersonal Level and Cross-Race Dynamics

- At the interpersonal level, the different worlds inhabited by white people and people of color result in fundamentally different perceptions of the same events and experiences.

- White people are often taught that their way of living and experiencing the world is normative, which makes it difficult to recognize or validate the very different realities faced by Black, Indigenous, and people of color.

- The murder of George Floyd in May 2020 is used to illustrate this divergence, with the experience of a white person involving sadness and compassion contrasting sharply with the visceral, embodied fear felt by Black people about the safety of their own family members.

- In mindfulness class settings, when people of color share experiences of race-based stress and suffering, common white responses include disbelief, silence, or a lack of genuine understanding, which creates distance and signals that the space may not be truly welcoming.

- Trust is identified as a significant interpersonal challenge, as expecting a person of color to immediately see a white person as an individual and a good person erases the accumulated experience of harm caused by whiteness throughout that person's life.

- A concrete example of interpersonal racial dynamics is the pattern of white people not visually registering people of color in shared spaces, such as cutting in line, and then claiming innocence based on lack of intent.

- This pattern reflects learned social conditioning that shapes perception itself, and the appropriate response is not to deny the other person's experience but to hold both realities simultaneously.

- White supremacy operates interpersonally through two mechanisms, namely the power to define and reduce the story of people of color and the power to silence their experiences by prioritizing intent over impact.

- Denying someone's reality by citing lack of intention is identified as a form of white supremacy in action, and the both-and framework from multicultural agreements is offered as a more constructive alternative.

### Race-Based Stress and Inclusion in Mindfulness Teaching

- Mindfulness classes frequently address stress, but a critical question is raised about whether race-based stress and the stress caused by other forms of oppression such as homophobia are explicitly included in that conversation.

- The voices and authors brought into the mindfulness classroom are identified as a cultural signal about whose experiences are considered valid and central to the practice.

- Teachers are encouraged to reflect on whether the culture of their classes actively makes room for discussions of how systemic oppression shapes the lived experience and stress levels of their students.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Racism Defined**
- Racism encompasses individual beliefs but extends to the marginalization and oppression of people of color based on a socially constructed racial hierarchy that privileges white people
- Systemic racism is a combination of systems, institutions, and factors that advantage white people and harm and disadvantage Black, Indigenous, and people of color
- It is described as a deeply ingrained and complex system with wide-ranging impacts on all people
**The Four Levels of Oppression and Privilege**
- The model is used in diversity training and is presented as a framework for understanding cross-race interactions and strategizing meaningful change
- The four levels are: Structural/Institutional, Cultural, Interpersonal, and Personal/Intrapersonal
- Personal transformation sits at the center of the model because people are the link across all four levels
- Each level supports, reflects, and maintains the status quo when left unchecked
**Structural/Institutional Level**
- Encompasses laws, regulations, and practices — including those not formally encoded — that shape who holds power and who controls the narrative and media
- In mindfulness classes, structural factors include: class location, availability of public transportation, accessibility, scheduling times, fee structures, and expectations set for participants
- Teachers do not always control all these variables but are encouraged to consider how they shape participants' experiences
**Cultural Level**
- Identified as perhaps the most significant level because it can undermine positive legal or procedural changes
- Example given: freed slaves did not receive the promised 40 acres and a mule because hearts and minds had not changed regarding entitlement to Black labor and the othering of people of color
- Instead of reparative land grants, indentured servitude and reign by terror through lynchings followed
- White supremacy culture is not described as intrinsically bad but becomes problematic when white European-based values are treated as the correct or only way to be
- Examples of white supremacy cultural values: efficiency, product-orientation over process-orientation, specific definitions of professionalism in dress and speech
- These values inadvertently support the racial status quo
- Diversity by the numbers — recruiting people of color without structural supports or addressing staff biases — is cited as a common failure
- Cultural norms are often unspoken; Chrystal's own experience of knowing not to talk about race without being explicitly told is offered as an example
- The Black Lives Matter movement is cited as an example of changing cultural narrative and shifting hearts and minds of white people
- Mindfulness teachers are encouraged to examine their ideal image of a mindfulness student and broaden that lens to include people who speak differently or are more expressive
**Interpersonal Level**
- White people and BIPOC live in very different worlds shaped by their location on the racial divide
- White people are taught that their way of living and experiencing the world is normative
- Cross-race interactions often involve unintentional responses that support the status quo
- Example: not seeing a person of color in a line and cutting in front of them — the person of color has experienced not being seen repeatedly, while the white person may genuinely believe they simply did not notice
- White supremacy in interpersonal dynamics operates in two ways: defining the story for people of color and silencing their experience
- Denying impact by citing lack of intent is identified as a denial of the other person's reality
- The "both and" framing from multicultural agreements is referenced as a useful tool
**Personal/Intrapersonal Level**
- Refers to the ways individuals have been shaped by their location on the racial divide
- Racial conditioning is described as very deep and profound, operating in layers
- Acknowledging one's own internalized racism is described as freeing and healing
- The distinction is made between internalized racism and one's core or sacred self
**Personal Transformation**
- Requires changing one's mind: relinquishing white privilege and working to understand the origins of racial disparities
- Requires changing one's heart: turning toward the pain caused by the system, listening to people of color, and believing their experiences
- Requires changing how one acts and interacts: not being complicit through silence or inaction, and responding more constructively across racial divides
**Mindfulness Teaching Applications**
- Mindfulness teachers are encouraged to consider whether race-based stress and other forms of oppression-related stress are included in class discussions
- Teachers are asked to reflect on whose voices and whose readings are brought into the room
- The culture of a mindfulness class — including what is read and discussed — shapes who feels included
**Redlining**
- Presented as a concrete example of structural/institutional racism
- Described as having cascading effects across housing, lending, education, and criminal justice
- Supported culturally by messages that people of color are dangerous, other, and that their presence in a neighborhood lowers property values
**Pause, Relax, Open Practice**
- A three-step practice from Insight Dialogue, developed by Gregory Kramer
- Introduced at EBMC by mentor Deb
- Steps: Pause, Relax, Open
- Used to interrupt conditioned automatic responses, particularly in race-related tense moments
- Practiced for approximately five minutes at the start of the session

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Mindfulness as a Foundation for Racial Awareness**
- The capacity to stay present in difficult situations is identified as a core competence of mindfulness teaching and of living as a happy human being
- The Pause, Relax, Open practice is offered as a way to interrupt conditioned mind and automatic responses in racially charged moments
- Not running on conditioned automatic responses is framed as the goal of the practice
- Thich Nhat Hanh's Smile Yoga — inviting a slight smile into the eyes — is referenced as part of relaxing the nervous system
**The Sacred Self and Internalized Racism**
- Internalized racism is distinguished from one's core identity — described as not being the sacred presence that is the whole of who we are, referencing teacher Tara
- Acknowledging racism within oneself is described as freeing and healing rather than shameful
**Turning Toward Pain**
- White people are encouraged to turn toward the pain caused by the system of racism, including grief felt by white people themselves
- Listening to people of color and believing their experiences is framed as a spiritual and ethical practice
**Changing Hearts, Minds, and Actions**
- Meaningful change requires transformation at all three levels: mind, heart, and behavior
- Relinquishing white privilege is named as a necessary part of this transformation
- Dismantling the myths and obscurations of history is part of changing one's mind
**Societal Structures and Their Human Cost**
- The failure to deliver on the promise of 40 acres and a mule is presented as a pivotal moment where cultural change did not follow legal change, resulting in indentured servitude and terror
- Redlining's effects on housing, lending, education, and criminal justice are presented as an interconnected web of structural harm
- The racial hierarchy is described as a structure that was formed on and continues to exist to the present day
**Broadening Who Belongs in Mindfulness Spaces**
- Teachers are invited to question assumptions about what a mindfulness student looks like, how they speak, and how they engage with practice
- Inclusion in mindfulness spaces is framed as both a spiritual and societal responsibility

### Stories
**George Floyd and the Lived Experience Gap**
- Shared by Chrystal Johnson, reflecting on her own experience
- When the murder of George Floyd occurred in May 2020, Chrystal felt upset, angry, sad, and discouraged, with compassion for him and his family
- She contrasts this with the experience of an African-American person, for whom the event brings up the constant fear that their husband, son, brother, daughters, sisters, or mother could be stopped and killed for no reason
- Chrystal notes she did not feel it in her bones the way BIPOC do
**Not Being Seen in a Line**
- Shared by Kitsie Shum as an example of interpersonal racial dynamics
- A white person does not see a person of color who is ahead of them in line and cuts in front
- The white person believes they simply did not see the individual, but the person of color has experienced not being seen and having white people come in front of them many times
**Chrystal Being Called Out**
- Shared by Chrystal Johnson about her own experience
- Chrystal recounts having been called out for not seeing a person of color and having witnessed it happen with other white people
- She reflects that she sincerely believed she did not see the person but has come to be suspicious of that explanation, concluding she has learned not to see
**Chrystal Knowing Not to Talk About Race**
- Referenced by Kitsie Shum, drawing on Chrystal's own previously shared experience
- Chrystal somehow knew she was not supposed to talk about race — no one ever explicitly told her, but she could tell
- Used as an example of unspoken cultural norms that maintain the racial status quo
**40 Acres and a Mule**
- Shared by Kitsie Shum as a historical example
- Freed slaves were promised 40 acres and a mule but next to none received it because it was not enforced
- Hearts and minds had not changed regarding entitlement to Black labor and the othering of Black people and people of color
- The result was indentured servitude and reign by terror through lynchings
- Presented as a pivotal example of cultural level racism undermining structural/legal change
