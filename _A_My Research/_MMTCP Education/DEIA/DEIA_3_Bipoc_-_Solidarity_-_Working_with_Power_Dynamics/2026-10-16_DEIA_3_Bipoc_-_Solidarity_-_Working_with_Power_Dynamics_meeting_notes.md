### The Fluidity of Privilege and Social Location

- Able-bodiedness was raised as an example of a privilege that many able-bodied people fail to list as one of their identities, illustrating how privilege can be invisible to those who hold it.

- This omission points to a broader pattern in which people who are immersed in a particular privilege often cannot perceive it as such.

- Privilege is described as fluid rather than fixed, meaning it shifts depending on context, geography, and social environment.

- White privilege is often treated as the primary or only form of privilege discussed in diversity training, yet many other forms of privilege exist simultaneously.

- A widely cited measure suggests that having food, clothing, shelter, and a bed places a person in a more privileged position than approximately 83% of the global population.

- Conda Mason shared a personal account of living in London in the late 1970s and early 1980s, where Black American women were perceived as privileged by Black British women of Caribbean and West African descent due to employment status, living conditions, and the social capital associated with an American accent.

- That experience served as a formative illustration of how privilege is context-dependent and can appear in unexpected ways across different societies.

### Social Location as a Framework for Mindfulness Teaching

- Social location is a sociological term referring to the position an individual occupies within society based on characteristics that society deems significant.

- This position is not fixed and can vary across time, geography, and social context, making it a dynamic rather than static concept.

- For mindfulness teachers, awareness of social location is considered essential to being in right relationship with oneself, with students, and with colleagues.

- Just as awareness precedes choice in mindfulness practice, understanding one's social location enables more intentional and non-harming interactions.

- Race and ethnicity are among the most visible factors shaping access to power and privilege in many parts of the world, but they are not the only ones.

- Additional dimensions of social location include education, language, age, gender and gender expression, sexual orientation, geographic location, economic status, social networks, ability and disability, physical appearance, and body size.

- Each of these dimensions contains characteristics that are either privileged and considered mainstream by society or marginalized and oppressed by society.

- Privileged characteristics tend to ease access to employment, education, healthcare, and housing, while marginalized characteristics tend to result in reduced affirmation, fewer resources, and in some cases exposure to violence.

- Age was offered as an example of a social location that changes over a lifetime, with very young and very old people tending to be more marginalized than those in a societally valued middle range.

- Geographic location also shapes privilege, with urban centers generally conferring more social power than rural or peripheral areas, and the global north holding significantly more privilege than the global south on the whole.

- Class background was identified as a characteristic that tends to remain relatively stable across a lifetime, even when some degree of class mobility occurs.

- All of these dimensions can be imagined as vectors intersecting with one another, and an individual's social location exists at the point where all of those vectors cross.

### Power Dynamics Within BIPOC Communities

- A central question for this session is how BIPOC communities can avoid replicating the same power structures that have caused harm, simply in a different form.

- Without awareness of how power operates within mindfulness spaces, even those that are BIPOC-only, there is a risk of unconsciously recreating the dynamics of the dominant mainstream culture.

- Unawareness of able-bodiedness as a form of power can lead a teacher to offer instructions that are inaccessible to certain students or that implicitly privilege particular bodies.

- Unawareness of the power conferred by higher education can result in a teacher taking up disproportionate space in group settings because verbal fluency and academic confidence feel natural to them.

- Unawareness of the privilege associated with lighter skin tone can cause a teacher to miss opportunities to amplify darker-skinned colleagues who may be equally qualified but less visible.

- Institutionalized power refers to the ability to control circumstances, make choices, and access resources, and awareness of where one holds such power creates leverage points for acting in solidarity.

- Beyond institutionalized power, marginalized identities also carry their own distinct forms of power, including resource sharing within communities, cultural creativity expressed through art and music, a strong sense of collective identity and shared destiny, historical legacy, and a relational orientation toward other living beings and the natural world.

- These alternative forms of power are described as tools for promoting love, freedom, and justice, and for building genuine solidarity across BIPOC communities.

### The Power Flower Exercise

- The power flower is a diagram resembling a daisy with the participant's name at the center and a series of petals radiating outward, each labeled with a dimension of social location.

- Each petal is divided into two sections, with the outer section to be colored in if the participant holds a privileged or mainstream identity in that category, and the inner section to be colored in if the participant holds a marginalized or oppressed identity.

- A line extending from each petal provides space for participants to write how they personally name or describe themselves within that category.

- Blank petals are included to allow participants to add dimensions of identity that are personally significant but not already represented in the diagram.

- The exercise is intended to reflect how social locations are experienced within society rather than solely through inner personal experience, and these two perspectives may sometimes differ.

- An example was given of a participant with a thin body who holds societally privileged appearance status but whose thinness stems from an eating disorder, illustrating how the outer petal can reflect societal privilege while the health status petal reflects personal suffering.

- This distinction between societal-level privilege and individual lived experience is central to how the exercise is meant to be interpreted and completed.

- Participants are encouraged to complete the diagram individually before bringing it to their home group for shared discussion.

- Within the home group, each person shares their completed diagram, and the group explores the multidimensionality of mainstream and marginalized identities across its members.

- A specific reflection point during group discussion is to identify which identities remain relatively stable over time and which are more fluid or subject to change.

### The Two Truths Framework in Relation to Identity

- The power flower diagram is acknowledged as inherently incomplete, as no list of characteristics can fully capture the totality of who a person is.

- This limitation is connected to the concept of the two truths in mindfulness teachings, which holds that two simultaneous realities coexist.

- The first truth is the deeply personal and relational experience of living in a body with a specific race, gender, age, geography, and other social characteristics, all of which are real and consequential.

- The second truth is a universal experience in which there is no separation between beings and in which oneness and interconnectedness can be touched.

- Access to the universal truth is described as requiring a full and honest engagement with the relational truth, rather than bypassing the complexities of lived experience.

- BIPOC teachers are described as uniquely positioned to hold and teach both the relational and the universal dimensions of experience.

### Next Steps

- Trainees are to complete the power flower diagram individually, reflecting carefully on each dimension of social location as it applies to their own experience within society.

- Completed diagrams are to be brought to home group sessions for shared discussion, with attention to the diversity of mainstream and marginalized identities across the group.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Fluidity of Privilege**
- Privilege is not fixed or stable; it shifts depending on geography, culture, and social context
- The identity exercise revealed that able-bodied people rarely list able-bodiedness as an identity, pointing to how invisible privilege can be when one is immersed in it
- A statistic was cited: having food in the refrigerator, clothes in a closet, a bed, and a roof places a person better off than 83% of people on the planet
- Conda's London story illustrated how being Black and American conferred unexpected privilege abroad compared to Black British women of Caribbean or West African descent
**Social Location**
- Borrowed from sociology, social location refers to the social position an individual holds within their society based on characteristics deemed important by that society
- It is not fixed and is society-specific; elements can change over time and space
- Categories of social location discussed include: race and ethnicity, education, language spoken, age, gender and gender expression, sexual orientation, geographic location, economic status, social network, ability and disability, appearance, and body size
- Awareness of social location is described as essential for mindfulness teachers to be in right relationship with themselves, students, and colleagues
**Privilege and Marginalization Within Social Location Categories**
- Age: youthful adults are privileged; the very young and very old are marginalized
- Geography: urban center dwellers tend to be more privileged; rural and global south populations less so
- Ability: normative, able bodies are privileged; disabled bodies are marginalized; neurotypical individuals are privileged over those with mental illness or neurodivergence
- Education: higher formal education confers mainstream status; life experience without formal credentials can be marginalized, particularly within mindfulness spaces
- Appearance and body size: thin bodies are often privileged in society regardless of the personal circumstances behind them
- Class background: noted as one of the least likely characteristics to change over a lifetime
**Power Dynamics Within BIPOC Communities**
- The session focused specifically on power dynamics within POC and BIPOC communities, not only between BIPOC individuals and dominant white culture
- Key questions raised: How do we avoid replicating the same power structures that have harmed us? How do we build communities that reflect our values as mindfulness practitioners?
- Unconscious replication of dominant dynamics can occur if teachers are unaware of their own privileged identities, such as able-bodiedness, higher education, or lighter skin tone
- Examples given: inaccessible instructions due to unawareness of able-bodied privilege; taking up disproportionate verbal space due to educational privilege; missing opportunities to amplify darker-skinned colleagues due to colorism
**Community-Based Forms of Power**
- Institutionalized power is not the only form of power available
- Marginalized identities carry their own forms of power including: resource sharing within communities, creativity and cultural production (art, music, song, dance), capacity for building connection and belonging, strong sense of history and legacy, alignment with respect for other living beings and harmony with the earth
- These forms of power can be used to promote love, freedom, and justice and to build solidarity
**The Power Flower Exercise**
- A diagram resembling a daisy with the participant's name in the center
- Each petal represents an aspect of social location
- Outer petal section colored in if the characteristic is privileged or mainstream in society
- Inner petal section colored in if the characteristic is marginalized or oppressed by society
- A line extending from each petal is used to write how the participant names themselves in that category
- Blank petals are included for identities not already named in the diagram
- The exercise focuses on societal experience, not inner personal experience
- Example discussed: a thin person with an eating disorder colors the outer petal for appearance (societal privilege) and the inner petal for health status (invisible mental illness)
- Participants are to complete the diagram individually, then share and discuss in home groups
**Home Group Activities**
- Share and discuss the Power Flower diagram with the home group
- Identify which identities stay relatively stable over time versus which are fluid and changing
- Recall and discuss a time when institutionalized power dynamics were observed playing out within a BIPOC or POC community, noting the identities involved and how one responded
**The Two Truths**
- Drawn from mindfulness teachings, the Two Truths refer to the simultaneous existence of the personal relational experience (shaped by race, geography, age, gender, sexuality, body size) and the universal experience of oneness and non-separation
- Access to the universal is said to come through deeply engaging with, not bypassing, the relational experience
- BIPOC teachers are described as uniquely positioned to teach both the relational and the universal
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Privilege as Invisible When Immersed In It**
- When a person is surrounded by privilege, it becomes difficult to perceive as privilege at all
- The able-bodiedness example is offered as a gateway to compassion for those who cannot see their own privilege
- Recognizing invisible privilege opens the door to greater empathy across difference
**Choice Follows Awareness**
- A core principle drawn from mindfulness practice is applied to social location: one cannot make conscious, non-harming choices without first becoming aware of how one is positioned in relation to others
- Awareness of social location allows teachers to interact with students and colleagues in the most non-harming way possible
**Solidarity Through Leveraging Institutionalized Power**
- Understanding where one holds institutionalized power creates leverage points for change
- Solidarity is enacted by using one's power consciously: calling in colleagues with less visibility, recommending others for opportunities, ensuring accessibility in teaching
- Solidarity is framed as a mindfulness practice and a teaching practice simultaneously
**Marginalized Communities as Sources of Power and Wisdom**
- Communities on the margins have historically generated rich culture, art, and music
- Practices of resource sharing and mutual aid within under-resourced communities represent a form of collective power
- A sense of shared identity, shared destiny, and legacy is identified as a spiritual and communal resource
- Harmony with the earth and respect for living beings are named as values that emerge from marginalized identities
**The Two Truths as a Framework for Liberation**
- The relational and the universal are not in opposition; they are simultaneous truths
- Bypassing the messy details of lived experience to reach a sense of universal oneness is described as insufficient and incomplete
- Moving through the relational experience is the path to touching the universal
- BIPOC practitioners are described as uniquely equipped to hold and teach both dimensions

### Stories
**Conda Mason — Living in London in the Late 1970s**
Conda Mason shares a personal story from when she moved to London around 1979 after living in New York. She describes a community of Black American women who formed an expat group and their interactions with Black British women of Caribbean and West African descent. The Black British women pointed out that the Black American women acted with privilege. Conda describes being shocked, having never considered herself privileged as a Black woman in America. She observed that the American women were employed, earning money, and living in nicer accommodations compared to many of the Black British women, who were often unemployed. She identifies simply having an American accent as conferring privilege. This story is offered as her first direct experience of the fluidity of privilege.
**Kate — Story of a Trainee with an Eating Disorder**
Kate recounts an experience from a previous training in which a participant who had a thin body raised a question during the Power Flower exercise. The participant disclosed that her thinness was the result of an eating disorder and asked whether she should color the outer or inner petal for appearance. The group decided she should color the outer petal for appearance, reflecting societal privilege associated with thinness, and the inner petal for health status, reflecting her invisible mental illness and personal suffering. This story is used to illustrate the distinction between societal experience of privilege and personal inner experience.
**Kate — Personal Story of Community Resource Sharing**
Kate shares a brief personal account of becoming pregnant and having neighbors and community members show up with baby clothes, maternity clothes, and cribs, such that she and her partner barely had to purchase anything for their child. She offers this as an example of the power of resource sharing within communities that may not have significant financial wealth, illustrating a form of power available through marginalized identities.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Able-bodiedness and disability are identified as social location categories that directly affect access to resources, employment, healthcare, and housing
- Mental illness and neurodivergence are noted as forms of disability that can be invisible to others but significantly impact daily lived experience
- The example of a trainee with an eating disorder illustrates how a mental health condition can be invisible while simultaneously producing a body that is privileged by society
- Neurotypical individuals are described as privileged by society, while those with mental illnesses or other cognitive disabilities are described as marginalized
- Teachers who are unaware of able-bodied privilege may offer mindfulness instructions that are inaccessible to students with disabilities or that privilege certain bodies over others
