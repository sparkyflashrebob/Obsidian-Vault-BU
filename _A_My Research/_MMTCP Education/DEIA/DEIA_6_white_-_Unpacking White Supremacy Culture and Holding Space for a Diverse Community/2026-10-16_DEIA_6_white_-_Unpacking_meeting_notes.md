### DEIA 6 White – Unpacking White Supremacy Culture and Holding Space for Diverse Community
📅 October 16, 2026
👥 Kitsie (Facilitator/Main Teacher), Crystal (Co-Facilitator/Teacher)
Tags: DEIA, White Supremacy Culture, Inclusive Facilitation, Mindfulness Teaching, Race-Based Harm, Oppression, Privilege, Affinity Groups, Multicultural Awareness, Mindfulness Practice
### Outline
### Overview of Session Six Goals

- Developing the capacity to notice and address race-based harm in oneself, in daily life, and in teaching practice is identified as the central purpose of the six-session journey.

- Specific aspects of white supremacy culture that cause harm are to be examined alongside facilitation skills that support greater inclusiveness.

- Cultural values associated with white supremacy culture are clarified as not inherently bad, but harmful when presented as the only acceptable way of doing things.

- Harms beyond race are to be addressed by Crystal, extending the tools and information provided throughout the training to other dimensions of oppression.

- The final affinity group homework assignment is to be introduced, with the hope that participants will maintain connections with one another beyond the six-month program.

### Characteristics of European White Culture Versus Indigenous Cultural Values

- Material wealth and individualism are identified as core values of European white culture, where individual accumulation is equated with personal worth.

- Contrasting Indigenous values emphasize sharing, collective assets, and the survival and wellbeing of the group over individual gain.

- A punitive system of justice, focused on identifying and punishing perpetrators, is described as a European cultural import, in contrast to restorative justice systems that aim to heal the health of the community.

- Competition is highlighted as a defining feature of capitalist white culture, oriented toward being better than others, which stands in contrast to values of coexistence and harmony.

- European models of governance centered on power over others and centralized authority are contrasted with consensus-based governance and relational networks found in many Native communities.

- Variations exist across white American, Canadian, Brazilian, and Australian cultures, yet these general characteristics are presented as the shared framework for the session's discussion.

### Tips for Inclusive Facilitation in Mindfulness Classes

- Explicitly welcoming everyone into the room, including people of different races, ethnicities, and physical abilities, is presented as a direct antidote to the white cultural tendency to treat one's own way of being as the norm.

- Building community among participants, rather than positioning the teacher solely as an expert, is encouraged by welcoming each person individually and creating opportunities for participants to meet one another.

- Thoughtful use of language, examples, and quoted authors is recommended to ensure that teaching reflects the full range of communities and lived experiences rather than speaking only to a narrow segment of the population.

- Teaching alongside a person of color is offered as a particularly effective way to invite racial diversity, as students benefit from seeing teachers who reflect their own appearance, voice, and experience.

- Establishing multicultural interaction agreements at the outset of a class is emphasized, since without explicit norms the default reverts to dominant white cultural norms.

- The word "we" is identified as a loaded and powerful term that must be used carefully, as assuming a shared experience can leave those outside that implied group feeling excluded and out of place.

- A visualization exercise is suggested in which teachers imagine their ideal class, then honestly assess who was present in that mental image and consider how any aspect of it might be exclusionary.

- Invisible disabilities and access barriers are raised as additional dimensions of exclusion, noting that language assuming participants can always sit may inadvertently exclude people with physical limitations.

### Developing Ease with Diverse Perspectives and Experiences

- Either-or thinking, characteristic of white culture, is identified as a barrier to holding a genuine diversity of student perspectives and experiences.

- Student questions and comments are to be honored and elevated by the teacher rather than subtly suggesting that the student has not yet fully understood the material.

- Defensiveness and conflict avoidance are named as tendencies within white culture that can cause teachers to feel threatened when students question or call them in.

- Establishing a robust feedback process at the start of a class signals to students that their experience matters and that the teacher is genuinely open to hearing when something is causing harm.

- Responding skillfully when called out or called in, and addressing microaggressions as they arise rather than smoothing over them, is presented as essential to maintaining an inclusive class environment.

- Leaving a harmed person's experience unacknowledged causes them to feel excluded from the community of the class, reinforcing the very dynamics the teacher is working to dismantle.

- White fragility, the expectation of comfort and the sense of being attacked when race is raised, is described as something to move toward compassionately using mindfulness skills rather than away from.

- Feedback about race or harm is reframed as a generous sharing that helps teachers avoid causing unintended harm, rather than as a personal attack or a matter of shame.

### Managing Dominant Voices and Ensuring Equitable Participation

- Preventing any single participant from dominating the class is presented as an important way to avoid students feeling othered or marginalized during sessions.

- Both-and thinking is offered as a facilitative tool to remind participants that there is no single correct way to engage with mindfulness practice, and that different experiences can coexist.

- Paternalism, the assumption that the teacher holds superior expertise and has something to offer people who do not yet understand, is identified as a tendency that can slide into microaggressions.

- Holding one's own perspective with humility, as one experience among many, creates space for students to find their own authentic entry into the practice.

- Actively monitoring who is speaking and who is not, and inviting those who have not yet contributed, is described as a core facilitative responsibility.

- Small group structures with systems that give everyone equal speaking time are recommended as a practical method for ensuring more equitable participation.

- Reaching out individually to participants whose voices are missing from the group, and naming that their contribution is valued, is suggested as a direct and caring response to uneven participation.

- Transparent decision-making around time management is recommended, with teachers explaining in the moment the balance they are trying to strike between sharing and covering material, so that being moved past does not feel personal to students.

### Using Mindfulness Practice to Hold Steady When Tension Arises

- The goal of inclusive teaching is not perfection but rather creating an openness and level of safety that allows students to show up as fully as possible.

- Pause, relax, and open is offered as a practical tool for teachers to steady themselves and remain present when tension arises in the class rather than trying to calm or suppress it.

- Naming what is true and using the capacity to investigate, rather than moving on out of embarrassment, is presented as the skillful response to difficult moments.

- Creating space and time within the group to acknowledge that something has happened and to respond to its impact is described as a necessary step in honoring those who have spoken up.

- Acknowledging uncertainty about how to respond, committing to reflect on it, seeking support, and returning to the group with a response is offered as a realistic and honest approach when the right answer is not immediately clear.

- Inviting the class itself to reflect during a pause on what has occurred and what has arisen for them is presented as a way to draw on the collective wisdom of the group.

- Returning to difficult moments later in the session or in a subsequent class is acknowledged as a valid option, since the exact right response will not always be available in the moment.

### Expanding the Framework Beyond Race to Other Dimensions of Privilege and Oppression

- The training's focus on race and whiteness is acknowledged as intentional, given that racism is described as a profound and foundational injury in the United States requiring urgent attention from all mindfulness teachers.

- Racism is identified as a global issue extending beyond the United States to other settler colonial countries including those throughout the Americas, Australia, and New Zealand.

- European nations that initiated and profited from the slave trade, including Portugal, England, Spain, France, Germany, and Italy, are named as having spread white supremacy culture to their colonies in Africa and beyond.

- Additional examples of ethnic minority oppression are cited, including the Rohingya in Myanmar, Tibetans and Uyghurs in China, Turks in Germany, Dalits in India, Roma throughout Europe, and Mayans in Mexico and Central America.

- Socioeconomic class, the global oppression of women, bigotry and violence against LGBTQI and gender non-conforming communities, and barriers faced by people with visible and invisible disabilities are all named as further dimensions of privilege and oppression relevant to mindfulness teaching.

- Teachers are invited to examine the dimensions of identity in which they hold relative privilege as opportunities to make their teaching more accessible, and to draw on dimensions of subjugated identity as resources for their students' learning.

- Researching the history of the dynamics most salient in one's own culture and bringing mindful attention to how identities are constructed and how those constructions lead to needless suffering are offered as ongoing practices.

- The training expresses the hope that participants will feel more confident offering mindfulness classes that are as inclusive as possible so that the benefits of practice may be deeply and widely accessible.

### Next Steps

- Participants are to complete a course design assignment before their final affinity home group, including describing the learning culture they wish to create, writing an inclusive class description, identifying five inclusion practices, and outlining how they will continue learning and hold themselves accountable in this area.

- Attendance at the bi-weekly White Affinity Group is strongly encouraged as an ongoing opportunity to ask questions, reconnect with peers, and continue learning from new material brought to each session.

- Participants are asked to reflect on and share what they are taking away from the six sessions and one thing they are proud of in how they engaged with the material.

- Continued lifelong learning on both race-related issues and mindfulness teaching is framed as the expectation going forward, with the training serving as a foundation rather than a conclusion.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Characteristics of White/European Culture vs. Indigenous Cultural Values**
- European/white culture emphasizes individual material wealth, equating wealth with personal value
- Indigenous cultural values emphasize sharing, collective survival, and collective assets
- European justice systems are punitive; Indigenous systems are restorative, focused on community health
- White capitalist culture emphasizes competition and being the best; Indigenous values emphasize coexistence and harmony
- European governance emphasized power over others and centralized government; Indigenous governance emphasized consensus and relational networks
- These characteristics vary slightly across white American, Canadian, Brazilian, and Australian cultures but share general traits
- The culture itself is not inherently bad; it becomes white supremacy culture when presented as the only or superior way
**Tips for Inclusive Facilitation**
- Explicitly welcome everyone into the room, naming different races, ethnicities, physical abilities, and more
- Use a welcoming practice adapted to the local context
- Build community rather than positioning the teacher solely as the expert
- Be thoughtful about language, examples, and authors quoted — draw from a wide range of communities
- Teach with a person of color when possible, as representation matters to students
- Establish multicultural interaction agreements at the start of class; without them, the default reverts to dominant culture norms
- Be cautious with the word "we" — ensure it genuinely includes everyone in the room
- Do a visualization exercise of your ideal class and honestly assess who was included and who was excluded
**Developing Ease with Different Perspectives**
- Avoid either/or thinking; hold up student questions and comments rather than implying they haven't figured things out
- Establish a robust feedback process so students know their experience matters
- Invite feedback at the start of class and provide clear channels (in class, during breaks, via email)
- Address microaggressions as they arise rather than smoothing over them
- Move compassionately toward tension rather than away from it
- Apply mindfulness skills to discomfort around race; understand it as generous sharing, not an attack
- Recognize white fragility — the expectation of comfort — and work against it
**Managing Classroom Dynamics**
- Prevent any one person or group from dominating the conversation
- Use "both/and" framing to remind students there is no single way to practice mindfulness
- Hold personal perspective with humility as one experience among many
- Actively notice who is speaking and who is not
- Invite those who haven't spoken; encourage participation in large groups
- Ensure small group structures give everyone equal speaking time
- Reach out individually to those whose voices are missing; name that their voice is valued
- Seek consultation if people of color or other groups consistently remain silent
**Transparent Decision-Making**
- Time management in class is an ongoing challenge between sharing and covering material
- Inform students at the start of class about this tension
- Narrate decision-making in the moment so students don't take it personally when they are not called on
- Invite feedback when someone's hand is missed
**Using Practice When Tension Arises**
- Use pause, relax, open to steady yourself as a teacher
- Aim to create openness and safety so students can show up fully
- Name what is true rather than trying to calm things down or move past them
- Create space and time to acknowledge what has happened and respond to its impact
- Acknowledge when you don't know how to respond; commit to reflecting and following up
- Invite the class to reflect during a pause on what arose for them
- Recognize that other students may have ideas on how to proceed

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Mindfulness as a Tool for Addressing Harm**
- Mindfulness practice is presented as a resource for navigating racial stress and tension in the classroom
- The pause, relax, open technique is offered as a grounding tool when tension arises during facilitation
- Applying mindfulness skills to discomfort — investigating rather than avoiding — is framed as a core teaching competency
- The capacity to hold steady in the face of tension is described as modeling the practice itself
**The Construction of Identity and Suffering**
- Race is described as a construction — something learned and made up — that causes real harm
- Constructed identities across race, gender, class, disability, and sexuality are presented as sources of needless suffering
- Those in positions of privilege are described as having been taught not to see the constructed nature of identity
- Awareness of how identity is constructed is offered as a path toward reducing suffering
**Collective vs. Individual Frameworks**
- The contrast between individualism (white/European culture) and collective survival (Indigenous values) is presented as a foundational societal insight
- Restorative justice — restoring community health — is offered as an alternative to punitive systems
- Consensus governance and relational networks are presented as alternatives to centralized power structures
**Inclusive Teaching as Spiritual Practice**
- Welcoming everyone explicitly is framed as an antidote to the assumption that one's own way is the norm
- Holding one's perspective with humility is described as allowing others to find their own way into practice
- The goal of inclusive facilitation is described as ensuring the benefits of mindfulness are deeply and widely accessible
- Lifelong learning on race and mindfulness is presented as an ongoing commitment, not a destination
**Privilege and Responsibility**
- In whatever dimensions of identity one holds relative privilege, there is an opportunity to examine and address exclusion
- In whatever dimensions of identity one holds a subjugated position, personal experience becomes a resource for students
- The spectacular violence of American racism is named as requiring urgent attention from everyone teaching mindfulness
