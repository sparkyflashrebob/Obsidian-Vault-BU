### Outline
### Session Overview and Structure

- Introduced as the fourth session in the series, this meeting is titled "What to Do When You Are Called Out."

- Planned activities include a brief mindfulness practice, a talk on racial microaggressions, a film demonstrating examples, and applied exercises for responding to situations where harm has been caused.

- Connecting the mindfulness practice to real-world scenarios across the racial divide is a central aim of the session design.

### Pause, Relax, Open Mindfulness Practice

- Presented with an emphasis on its off-cushion applicability, the practice is framed as a tool available in triggered moments outside of formal meditation settings.

- Participants are guided to pause and arrive in the present moment, noticing sensations as they settle.

- Relaxation is invited through a slight smile and a softening of the belly, allowing each participant to engage in the way that works for them.

- Expanding the field of awareness outward as far as comfortable completes the three-part sequence of pause, relax, and open.

- Returning attention gently when the mind wanders is reinforced as a natural and expected part of the practice.

### Definition and Origins of Racial Microaggressions

- Defined by Dr. Derald Wing Sue, a psychologist and leading researcher in the field, microaggressions are described as brief and commonplace daily verbal, behavioral, or environmental indignities that communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative racial slights and insults toward people of color.

- Characterized as seemingly small yet highly impactful, these interactions represent one of the key ways racism is expressed and white supremacy culture is reinforced and maintained.

- Racist assumptions underlying microaggressions may be unconscious or so deeply embedded in cultural practices that people socialized as white may not recognize the harm being caused.

- Phrases such as "working like a slave," "cracking the whip," "jewing him down," "she gypped me," "illegal aliens," "ghetto," "good neighborhood," and "good school" are cited as examples with origins in slavery and other racist practices.

- Questions such as "where are you from," directed repeatedly at people of color, can convey a sense of not belonging, even when the intent appears innocent.

### Impact of Microaggressions on People of Color

- Harmful effects on physical and mental health are documented, including increases in depression, anxiety, physical illness, and chronic stress-related conditions.

- Microaggressions leave BIPOC individuals feeling excluded or othered in important settings such as work, school, and mindfulness classes.

- Relationships between BIPOC and white people are damaged by these interactions, fueling the perception that white people do not understand or respect the experiences of people of color.

- A significant asymmetry exists in how these interactions are perceived, as what may seem insignificant or accidental to a white person does not appear that way to the person of color experiencing it.

- Denial of impact by the person who committed the microaggression is itself identified as a mechanism for protecting a positive self-image as a white person.

### Themes and Examples of Racial Microaggressions

- Othering is one central theme, expressed through questions like "where are you from" or comments like "you speak such good English," directed at people of color who are native English speakers, or requests to touch a Black person's hair.

- Conveying that someone is less than is another theme, illustrated by calling a Black person "articulate" in a way that implies surprise, or questioning whether a Latina student is attending "the Harvard in Massachusetts" as if her attendance there is implausible.

- Invisibility is expressed through misidentifying a woman of Indian descent by another Indian woman's name, repeatedly mispronouncing someone's name, stepping in front of a person of color in line, or only calling on white students in a classroom.

- Statements such as "I don't see color," though intended to signal non-racism, actually communicate a refusal to acknowledge a significant part of a person's identity.

- Denying the reality of racism takes forms such as claiming a Black spouse proves one is not racist, offering encouragement like "if you work hard, you'll get somewhere" without acknowledging systemic barriers, or dismissing a person of color's account of an insult by saying "I'm sure they didn't mean it that way."

- Second-class citizen treatment manifests in actions such as mistaking a Latino customer for a waiter, expressing surprise that a BIPOC person is a doctor, or seating a dark-skinned couple at a remote table when unreserved tables in the main area are available.

### Relevance for White Mindfulness Teachers

- Committing microaggressions as a white mindfulness teacher is described as virtually inevitable given that white supremacy culture was absorbed as normal during upbringing.

- Difficult emotions such as shame, self-blame, and anger may arise when a student points out that a microaggression has occurred, and the teacher may feel convinced the student misheard or misunderstood.

- Responding skillfully using mindfulness and self-compassion is presented as a way to build connection rather than enforce separation when called out.

- Framing the response not as a defense of being a good white person but as an expression of commitment to seeing clearly and supporting student well-being is emphasized as the appropriate orientation.

- Openness to feedback from people of color is described as a positive development, as it enables learning and genuine connection.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The "Pause, Relax, Open" Practice**
- Kitsie led the group through a brief mindfulness practice designed to be accessible off the cushion or seat
- The three-part practice involves: Pause — arriving in the present moment and noticing sensations; Relax — inviting ease, bringing a slight smile, softening in the belly; Open — expanding the field of awareness outward as far as comfortable
- The practice is intended to be available in real-time moments of being triggered or called out
**Definition of Racial Microaggressions**
- Defined by Dr. Derald Wing Sue, psychologist and major researcher in the field
- His definition: "brief and commonplace daily verbal, behavioral, or environmental indignities, whether intentional or unintentional, that communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative racial slights and insults towards people of color"
- Described as seemingly small but very impactful
- Identified as one of the important ways racism is expressed and white supremacy culture is reinforced and maintained
- Racist assumptions underlying them may be unconscious or deeply embedded in cultural practices
**Phrases with Racist Origins**
- "Working like a slave," "cracking the whip," "Jewing him down," "she gypped me," "illegal aliens," "ghetto," "good neighborhood," "good school" — all identified as having origins in slavery and other racist practices
**Themes of Microaggressions**
- You Are Other: "Where are you from?" asked of non-white people or those with accents; "You speak such good English" said to someone with Asian features; "Can I touch your hair?" said to a Black person
- You Are Less Than: "You are so articulate" said to a Black person; questioning whether a Latina student is attending Harvard in Massachusetts as if she could not belong there
- I Don't See You: Calling a woman of Indian descent by another Indian woman's name; saying "I don't see color"; repeatedly mispronouncing someone's name; stepping in front of a person of color in line; only calling on white students in class
- Denying the Reality of Racism: "I'm not racist, my best friend/partner/spouse is Black"; "If you work hard, you'll get somewhere" said to a young person of color without acknowledging systemic barriers; "I'm sure they didn't mean it that way" said to dismiss a person of color's experience of an insult
- Second-Class Citizen: Waving over a Latino man in a restaurant assuming he is a waiter when he is a customer; expressing surprise that a BIPOC person is a doctor; escorting a dark-skinned couple to a remote table in a restaurant when unreserved tables are available in the main area
**Impact of Microaggressions**
- Harm the physical and mental health of people of color
- Lead to increases in depression, anxiety, physical illness, and chronic stress-related conditions
- Leave BIPOC feeling excluded or othered in work, school, and mindfulness class settings
- Harm relationships between BIPOC and white people
- Fuel the sense that white people do not understand or respect the experience of people of color
- Reinforce and normalize racism
- Their impact is often denied by the person speaking, which is identified as a way of protecting a "good white self"
**Responding When Called Out**
- As a white mindfulness teacher, committing microaggressions is described as nearly inevitable given socialization in white supremacy culture
- Difficult emotions that may arise when called out include shame, self-blame, and anger
- One may be convinced the other person misheard or misunderstood
- Mindfulness and self-compassion are offered as tools for responding skillfully
- The goal is to build connection rather than enforce separation
- Framed not as a question of being a good white person but as a mindfulness practice of seeing clearly and supporting student well-being
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Mindfulness as a Tool for Social Accountability**
- The "Pause, Relax, Open" practice is presented not only as a seated meditation but as a real-time intervention for moments of interpersonal difficulty and being called out
- Mindfulness is positioned as a means of seeing clearly — a core practice applied to the social and racial domain, not only to inner states
**The Asymmetry of Racial Experience**
- A significant insight offered is that interactions that seem insignificant or accidental to white people do not seem that way to people of color — this asymmetry is central to understanding why microaggressions matter even when unintended
**Feedback as Connection**
- Receiving feedback from people of color about the impact of one's words or actions is framed as a positive and relational event — an opportunity to learn and be in connection rather than a threat or attack
**White Supremacy Culture as Socialization**
- The session frames white supremacy culture not primarily as individual malice but as a system of socialization that shapes unconscious assumptions and language, making microaggressions an expected outcome of that upbringing rather than a sign of individual moral failure
**Denial as a Protective Mechanism**
- Denying the impact of microaggressions — saying "I didn't mean it that way" — is identified as a mechanism for protecting a self-image as a good white person, rather than a genuine engagement with the harm caused
**Seeing Clearly as Practice**
- The response to being called out is reframed from defensiveness to the mindfulness practice of seeing clearly, connecting personal spiritual practice to social responsibility
