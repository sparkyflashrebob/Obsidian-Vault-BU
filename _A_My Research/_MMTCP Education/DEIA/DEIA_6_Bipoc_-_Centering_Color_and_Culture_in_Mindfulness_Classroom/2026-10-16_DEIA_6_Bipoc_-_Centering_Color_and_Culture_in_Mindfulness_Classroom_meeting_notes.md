### Outline
### White Supremacy Culture Framework in Mindfulness Spaces

- Presented as a reference framework is the work of diversity educators Tema Okun and Kenneth Jones, who outlined characteristics of white supremacy culture in a 2001 manual.

- Without explicit effort, BIPOC teachers and practitioners may default to dominant cultural norms, making active centering of one's own experience essential.

- Perfectionism is identified as one characteristic of dominant culture, manifesting in mindfulness spaces as little tolerance for being a learner and an unrealistic demand for mastery.

- A sense of urgency appears in mindfulness contexts as students seeking quick fixes or expecting rapid enlightenment after minimal practice.

- Defensiveness within dominant cultural spaces can show up in mindfulness classrooms when teachers do not allow students sufficient room to question or challenge deeply held beliefs.

- Quantity over quality manifests as a consumer-driven approach to mindfulness, prioritizing more classes, books, and retreats over the depth and quality of one's actual practice and presence.

- Fear of open conflict leads to an overemphasis on politeness in mindfulness spaces, preventing the kind of appropriate challenge that supports collective growth.

- Individualism represents a mission drift in mindfulness, narrowing the focus from collective liberation and interdependence to personal stress reduction.

- Objectivity, rooted in Enlightenment-era thinking, risks communicating that emotions are inherently destructive or that more stoic practitioners hold more valid perspectives.

- The right to comfort reflects a belief among those with power that psychological ease must be maintained at all times, which can inhibit the productive discomfort necessary for growth.

- Each characteristic can be actively countered in mindfulness teaching by embracing imperfection, reframing conflict as a source of creativity and intimacy, and continuously connecting students to collective experience and interdependence.

### External Practices for Centering Culture and Identity in Mindfulness Teaching

- Land acknowledgement is presented as a foundational external practice, grounding teachers in truth-telling and honoring the history and rights of indigenous peoples whose land one occupies.

- Using the term "unceded territory" reflects the historical reality that First Nations peoples never legally signed away their lands, and the website native-land.ca is recommended as a resource for identifying the relevant indigenous territory.

- Acknowledging one's own ancestral lineage during introductions is offered as another way to bring one's full self into the teaching space and to invite students to do the same.

- Creating a welcoming physical or virtual space where students see themselves reflected is emphasized as essential to helping participants feel safe and included.

- Ancestor altars, photographs of meaningful figures, cultural symbols such as the LGBTQI flag, and objects representing spiritual leadership are all cited as ways to make the teaching environment more personally and culturally resonant.

- The East Bay Meditation Center in Oakland is highlighted as a model of inclusive altar and space design, incorporating elements from many cultures and social justice movements.

- In virtual settings, teachers are encouraged to bring candles, photographs, water, flowers, or other meaningful objects into their Zoom backgrounds and to invite students to do the same.

- Incorporating poems, quotes, and references from BIPOC authors and thinkers such as James Baldwin, Toni Morrison, Joy Harjo, Ada Limón, June Jordan, and Isabel Allende is recommended to make language and content more culturally inclusive.

- Song lyrics are described as a powerful teaching tool, capable of illustrating mindfulness principles such as forgiveness and impermanence while reflecting the teacher's personal and cultural background.

- All cultural additions including songs, poems, and quotes should be intentionally connected back to core mindfulness principles rather than used gratuitously, ensuring they serve the depth of the teaching.

### Teaching as a BIPOC Practitioner in Predominantly White Spaces

- Holding the teacher seat, a position of significant power, while simultaneously occupying a marginalized social position creates a complex dual role that many BIPOC teachers navigate regularly.

- Feelings of hypervisibility, concern about projected stereotypes, and vigilance around microaggressions are common experiences for BIPOC teachers working in predominantly white mindfulness environments.

- Leaning into the teachings themselves is offered as a strategy for managing being triggered in such spaces, with the loving kindness practice specifically cited as a tool for cultivating love toward students one may fear or feel tension with.

- Silently offering metta to individual students during pauses in instruction is described as a method of integrating personal practice with teaching responsibilities in a way that transforms the internal experience of the teacher.

- Amplifying love rather than suppressing fear is framed as the original purpose of the metta practice as taught by the Buddha, and as a resource for BIPOC teachers navigating difficult environments.

- Students in mindfulness classes may seek to process their own racial conditioning or ask their BIPOC teacher to evaluate whether something is racist, creating a choice point around boundaries and capacity.

- Capacity to engage in race-related conversations with students involves assessing available time, genuine desire to engage, and emotional stability, particularly in the aftermath of racially charged events.

- Declining such conversations with care and redirecting students to resources or future check-ins is presented as a healthy and appropriate boundary-setting practice.

- Offering metta and compassion to oneself as a BIPOC teacher is emphasized as equally important as offering it to students, given the difficulty of holding empowered and marginalized roles simultaneously.

### The Role of Self-Awareness and Pause in Mindfulness Teaching

- Knowing one's own triggers as a teacher is identified as a critical dimension of mindfulness practice, developed through sustained meditation and careful observation of one's own mental and emotional responses.

- When triggered during teaching, the practice of pausing is described as a powerful tool that creates space for both the teacher and students to settle and for wisdom to arise.

- Silence in the teaching space is reframed not as awkward or problematic but as an opportunity to breathe, feel into what is most needed, and respond from a place of wisdom rather than reactivity.

- A non-reactive mind is described as the foundation of freedom on the mindfulness path, with the gap created by pausing identified as the space where wisdom becomes accessible.

### The Value of Co-Teaching and Community in Mindfulness Practice

- Co-teaching is presented as a direct antidote to the hyper-individualism prevalent in many societies, enriching both the teacher's experience and the quality of learning for students.

- Building a teaching team allows teachers to share the responsibility of holding space, to support one another, and to model the collective nature of the path toward liberation.

- Sangha and community are emphasized as essential supports for BIPOC mindfulness teachers, with the encouragement to maintain connections formed during training rather than continuing the path in isolation.

### Closing Reflections on the BIPOC Mindfulness Teaching Path

- The six-session series is framed as a starting point rather than a comprehensive treatment, with the expectation that participants will continue learning and exploring beyond the formal training.

- BIPOC teachers are encouraged to release the pressure of perfectionism and to recognize that their lived experience of navigating oppression and microaggressions constitutes a unique and valuable asset in the teaching space.

- Bringing one's whole self into teaching, including personal history, cultural background, and embodied experience of suffering, is described as the very thing that makes a teacher most effective and authentic.

- The entire mindfulness path is characterized as a path toward liberation, healing internal violence toward oneself and external violence toward others.

- Peace is affirmed as genuinely possible, with even brief moments of contact with peace described as meaningful evidence that freedom is attainable.

### Next Steps

- Participants are invited to journal on the kind of learning culture they wish to create in their classroom or practicum, envisioning how they and their students will engage with one another beyond conventional mindfulness norms.

- Reflection is encouraged on specific stories, traditions, artwork, or cultural references from one's own background that can be used to illustrate particular mindfulness teachings.

- Journaling reflections are to be brought to affinity home groups for shared discussion and to support a meaningful closing of those group relationships.

- Participants are encouraged to develop a personal plan for continuing their learning and to pursue that plan in community rather than in isolation.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Characteristics of White Supremacy Culture in Mindfulness Spaces**
- Framework developed by Tema Okun and Kenneth Jones in a 2001 manual
- Perfectionism: little tolerance for being a learner; demand for mastery; can appear among students and teachers in mindfulness spaces
- Sense of urgency: desire for a quick fix; students expecting enlightenment after only a few days of practice
- Defensiveness: treating criticism or questioning as a threat; teachers not allowing students to challenge deeply held beliefs
- Quantity over quality: consumer approach to mindfulness; prioritizing more classes, books, retreats, and longer meditations over genuine quality of attention and freedom
- Fear of open conflict: lack of tools for addressing conflict; overemphasis on politeness rather than growth-oriented challenge
- Individualism: lack of awareness of interdependence; mindfulness reduced to personal stress reduction rather than collective liberation
- Objectivity: overreliance on neuroscience framing; risk of communicating that emotions are destructive or that stoic practitioners are more valid
- Right to comfort: belief among those with power that they are entitled to emotional and psychological comfort at all times; resistance to being challenged
**Countering Dominant Culture Tendencies as a Teacher**
- Use the mindfulness space to celebrate imperfection; model not always having the right answers
- Reframe conflict as a source of creativity and an opportunity for deepening intimacy through mindful communication and wise speech
- Continuously encourage students to think beyond personal suffering and connect with collective experience
- Encourage students to contemplate how their personal experience and collective experience are interrelated
**External Practices for Presencing Culture in Teaching**
- Land acknowledgement: stating the indigenous land one is on before teaching; using native-land.ca to identify tribal names; using the phrase "unceded territory of" to honor that First Nations peoples never legally signed away their lands; adding land acknowledgement to Zoom display name
- Ancestor lineage: introducing oneself by naming parents and grandparents; inviting students to bring their own ancestors into the space
- Welcoming physical space: displaying photographs of meaningful figures, family members, or spiritual leaders; incorporating items such as candles, water bowls, flowers; using flags such as the LGBTQI flag; creating altar spaces that reflect multiple cultures and identities
- Virtual space: encouraging participants on Zoom to bring meaningful objects to their own space; examples include candles, photos, water, flowers
- Culturally diverse quotes and poems: sourcing material from BIPOC writers such as James Baldwin, Toni Morrison, Joy Harjo, Ada Limón, June Jordan, Isabel Allende; including teachers such as Thich Nhat Hanh and Larry Yang
- Song lyrics: incorporating lyrics from culturally resonant music into talks; examples include blues songs for forgiveness talks, Jimi Hendrix, Billie Holiday's "God Bless the Child," and Joni Mitchell's "Down to You" for impermanence; sometimes singing the lyrics aloud; always connecting lyrics back to mindfulness principles
**Being a BIPOC Teacher in Dominant Spaces**
- Tension of simultaneously holding the teacher seat as a position of power while being in a marginalized societal position
- Experiences of being one of very few people of color in a retreat or classroom setting
- Feelings of pressure, hypervisibility, concern about stereotypes, and vigilance around microaggressions
- Using the loving kindness practice as a stealth internal practice while teaching; silently offering metta to students between instructions
- The Buddha originally offered metta as an antidote to fear; amplifying love so that fear becomes less dominant
- Outcome of leading with love: students feeling seen and appreciated; receiving more positive responses from participants
**Knowing and Working with Triggers**
- Mindfulness practice enables teachers to see and know themselves more deeply
- Identifying personal triggers through meditation practice and observing the mind
- The power of pause: stopping, allowing silence, taking a breath, and finding the wisest response in that gap
- A non-reactive mind described as the path to freedom and liberation
**Healthy Boundaries Around Race Conversations**
- Students may seek to process their thinking around race and racism with a BIPOC teacher
- Teachers have the right to assess their own capacity before engaging: time available, genuine desire to engage, emotional stability
- Appropriate responses when capacity is low: expressing care, naming the importance of the topic, suggesting a follow-up time, or offering resources
**Team Teaching**
- Teaching with a partner counters hyper-individualism
- Provides mutual support, shared creative process, and richer experience for students
- Kate and Kanda's co-teaching of the six-session series offered as a direct example
**Non-Reactive Mind and Liberation**
- The mindfulness path described as a path toward freedom and liberation
- Greatest freedom identified as the freedom of a non-reactive mind
- Wisdom found in the gap created by pausing
- Peace described as possible and touchable even if momentary

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Mindfulness as a Tool for Dismantling Dominant Culture**
- Without explicit effort, mindfulness spaces can replicate the harms of dominant culture
- Mindfulness offers an opportunity to actively dismantle acculturation to dominant norms
- The same trajectory used in mindfulness teachings — bringing awareness to suffering in order to find freedom — can be applied to examining how dominant culture operates in the classroom
**Land as a Spiritual and Historical Anchor**
- Land acknowledgement is described as a form of truth-telling and reconciliation
- Acknowledging unceded indigenous territory honors the inherent rights of First Nations peoples to the land they stewarded
- The practice is described as humbling and grounding for the teacher
**Ancestors as Living Presence in Teaching**
- Naming one's ancestor lineage in a teaching introduction brings the whole self into the space
- Inviting students to bring their ancestors into the space creates a sense of collective presence and belonging
- Photographs of ancestors or meaningful figures on an altar serve as a grounding resource for the teacher
**Collective Liberation Over Individual Attainment**
- Mindfulness risks becoming a purely individual pursuit if teachers are not intentional
- The original orientation of mindfulness teachings points toward collective liberation and the recognition of interdependence
- BIPOC teachers are encouraged to continuously redirect students toward the collective dimension of practice
**Loving Kindness as an Antidote to Fear**
- The Buddha offered metta originally as an antidote to fear
- Amplifying love in the heart diminishes the dominance of fear
- This principle can be applied by BIPOC teachers navigating discomfort in predominantly white spaces
**The Non-Reactive Mind as the Path to Freedom**
- Freedom is not located outside oneself but is available in the present moment
- A non-reactive mind — one that knows how to pause and act wisely in the gap — is identified as the direct path to liberation
- This freedom is described as especially significant for BIPOC individuals who face additional layers of suffering beyond the universal human condition
**BIPOC Experience as an Asset in Teaching**
- Living experience of oppression, microaggressions, and systemic marginalization is described as a wealth of knowledge
- This lived experience allows BIPOC teachers to go deeper and bring personal authenticity to their teaching
- BIPOC teachers are described as bringing something unique to the mindfulness community that enriches it
**Community and Sangha as Spiritual Necessity**
- The path of mindfulness is not meant to be walked alone
- Sangha and community are described as essential supports for sustaining practice and teaching
- Team teaching and affinity home groups are offered as structural expressions of this principle

### Stories
**Kate's Retreat Teaching Experience at Spirit Rock Meditation Center**
- Shared by Kate, referring to her own experience approximately ten years prior
- Kate was teaching yoga on a metta retreat at Spirit Rock Meditation Center in California
- The retreat immediately followed a dedicated retreat for people of color, resulting in an attendee group that was almost entirely older white participants
- Kate was one of very few people of color on campus
- She felt uncomfortable, pressured, hypervisible, and concerned about stereotypes and microaggressions
- She chose to integrate metta as a silent internal practice while teaching: offering loving kindness to individual students between movement instructions
- She reflected on the teaching that the Buddha offered metta as an antidote to fear
- By cultivating love moment after moment, she found herself feeling tender toward the participants and sensed they were feeling tender toward her
- She reported receiving more appreciative notes from participants on that retreat than on any other
- She described people who reminded her of past difficult experiences turning out to genuinely see and appreciate her
**Kanda's Childhood in the Baptist Church**
- Shared by Kanda, referring to her own upbringing
- Kanda grew up in a Baptist church she loved, appreciating the music, people, and sermons
- The church spoke of a path to liberation and freedom
- She did not fully understand how that freedom would come
- Many years later, upon discovering the mindfulness path, she recognized it as the freedom she had been seeking
- She realized the freedom she had been looking for externally was available internally
**Kanda Sending Sunflower Seeds to Zoom Participants**
- Shared by Kanda, referring to her own teaching practice
- For one gathering conducted on Zoom, Kanda mailed each participant a sunflower seed and a small amount of soil in advance
- Participants grew the seeds so that by the time of the Zoom call, each person had a small sunflower on their altar
- Described as a beautiful and inclusive gesture that helped participants feel present and connected
**Billie Holiday Song in a Metta Talk**
- Referenced by both Kate and Kanda
- Kanda gave a talk on metta in which she sang Billie Holiday's "God Bless the Child"
- Kate, as a witness and student, described the moment as causing many in the room to burst into tears
- Kate cited this as firsthand evidence of the power of bringing cultural music into mindfulness teaching
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Students arriving at mindfulness classes with a desire for a quick fix, expecting results such as enlightenment after only five days of meditation
- Students sitting in meditation for extended periods on retreat experiencing physical discomfort including back pain and neck pain; yoga teaching offered as a support for these physical needs
- BIPOC teachers experiencing emotional discomfort, feelings of pressure, hypervisibility, and vigilance around microaggressions when teaching in predominantly white spaces
- Kate describing moments of emotional shakiness following racially charged tragedies, resulting in reduced capacity to process race-related conversations with students
- The challenge of holding the dual role of empowered teacher and marginalized individual simultaneously described as emotionally demanding
- Kanda noting that the world is filled with triggers for BIPOC individuals, adding layers of difficulty beyond the universal human experience of suffering
- The importance of knowing one's own triggers as a teacher and having tools to respond wisely rather than reactively
