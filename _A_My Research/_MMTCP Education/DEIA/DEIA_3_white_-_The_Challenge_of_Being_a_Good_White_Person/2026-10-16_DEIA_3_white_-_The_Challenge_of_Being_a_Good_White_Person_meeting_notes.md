### Outline
### Overview of the Third DEIA Session Structure

- Titled "The Challenge of Being a Good White Person," this third session in the series focuses on white people as a valuable source of change in racial equity efforts.

- Structured to include an adapted metta meditation, a talk on self-defensive behaviors, a transformation practice centered on opening to suffering, and an introduction to home group activities.

- White conditioning is noted as often invisible to those who hold it, and most likely to surface in cross-race interactions, making a strong humility practice especially important.

- A teaching on humility by the Dalai Lama is referenced as supplementary material available in the session resources.

### Adapted Metta Meditation for Racial Conditioning

- Participants are positioned as essential workers in the effort to create real racial equity, operating within a deeply unfair and abusive system not of their own making.

- Difficult emotions such as blame, shame, and aversion are acknowledged as common responses that tend to stall progress and cause disengagement rather than support change.

- Bringing compassion to one's own racial conditioning is presented as the only viable foundation for modeling compassion when actively interrupting racism.

- Four adapted phrases are offered for the metta practice, inviting participants to wish themselves safety from inner and outer harm, strength in body and mind, and the ability to hold their racial conditioning with compassion and understanding.

- Participants are encouraged to modify the phrases so they resonate personally, with the intention of watering the seeds of loving kindness toward oneself.

### Defining the Good White Self and Its Defense Mechanisms

- A distinction is drawn between white culture, meaning the cultural practices of white people, and white supremacy culture, which positions those practices as the superior or default way of being in the world.

- The "good white self" refers to the racialized dimension of a fixed self-identity, specifically the desire not to be seen as racist, complicit in injustice, or comfortable with the harms of white supremacy.

- Defending this identity involves rejecting information that contradicts one's sense of being a good person, which keeps individuals stuck, prevents learning, and perpetuates white privilege without awareness.

- Mindful attention to physical sensations, thoughts, emotions, and impulses arising in racially charged situations is identified as the primary tool for recognizing when these defenses are being activated.

- An illustrative example is shared in which a student pointed out that a lecture implied all Native Americans were dead, triggering an immediate defensive physical and emotional response before reflection revealed an internalized assumption rooted in how American history had been taught.

- That defensive reaction is identified as serving to protect the speaker from the emotional weight of acknowledging the ongoing suffering of Native American communities, which remain severely under-resourced and disproportionately harmed by events such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

- Two competing internal orientations are described, one that seeks to protect a comfortable identity and one that genuinely wants to see the truth and build capacity to act differently, with the session directed toward the latter.

### Specific Examples of Defending the Good White Self

- Denial of oppression and white privilege takes several forms, including claiming not to see color, asserting that society is a meritocracy, implying that personal hard work alone explains one's position, and using spiritual concepts such as "we are all one" to bypass the reality of racial harm.

- Spiritual bypass, a term coined by John Wellwood in the 1980s, is defined as using spiritual ideas and practices to sidestep personal and emotional unfinished business, and in this context it functions to deny the existence or importance of racial oppression.

- Making use of Black, Indigenous, and people of color includes repeatedly asking them to explain their experiences, making them responsible for creating equity within organizations, seeking their approval or a "cool white person" stamp of validation, and presuming familiarity or assuming all BIPOC wish to engage with white people.

- Enacting privilege at the interpersonal level includes withdrawing from conversations about race, asserting the right to be seen purely as an individual rather than as a member of a racial group, using emotional distress as a conversation stopper, accusing others of playing the race card, and prioritizing good intent over harmful impact.

- Enacting privilege at the intrapersonal level includes assuming one's own perspective is objective, requiring people of color to convince one of their valid experiences, blaming BIPOC for causing distress when they share their own pain, treating feelings as facts, and using confusion or self-doubt as a way to disengage from the work.

- A personal workplace example is shared in which redirecting a race-focused discussion toward a gay identity was recognized as an attempt to escape the discomfort of sitting in the racial hot seat, and a fellow white colleague's calm redirection in that moment is described as a powerful and instructive model of accountability.

- Excessive apologizing directed at people of color after causing harm is identified as another form of burden-shifting, placing the emotional labor of reassurance onto BIPOC rather than taking responsibility independently.

- Familiarity with this list of defenses is presented as practically valuable because recognizing a pattern in the moment allows one to name it, apologize, and move forward without becoming mired in it.

### Turning Towards Suffering as a Mindfulness Practice

- A quote from Thich Nhat Hanh is introduced, stating that when we see suffering, compassion is born in us, and that staying in touch with the world's suffering through various means is necessary to keep compassion alive.

- The purpose of mindfulness practice is described not as turning away from pain or eliminating it, but as looking deeply into it to understand its true nature and remain fully present with whatever arises.

- Turning away from suffering is equated with turning away from compassion and moving toward pity, which is a separating rather than joining attitude that treats others as fundamentally unlike oneself.

- Avoiding pain is identified as harmful to white people themselves, to other white people, and to BIPOC, while getting to know pain is described as offering greater capacity for skillful choices and authentic relationships.

- White people have been systematically taught not to see or feel the suffering of BIPOC through mechanisms such as residential segregation, dehumanizing labels in political and media discourse, and cultural images that portray non-white people as entirely other.

- The functioning of white supremacy is described as dependent on the cooperation of white people, specifically on their not caring, not feeling compassion, and not recognizing their own participation in the system.

- Once a connection is made with the pain of oppression and the unearned privileges that come at others' expense, individuals are positioned to ask whether this reality aligns with their values.

- For those with trauma histories or heightened sensitivity, engaging with this material can be especially overwhelming, making it important to pace oneself and build capacity wisely rather than becoming flooded.

### Grounding Practice for Staying Present with Difficult Material

- A grounding practice is introduced to help participants build the stamina needed to stay with difficult experiences without becoming overwhelmed or shutting down.

- Participants are guided to settle into a comfortable and alert posture, take slow deep breaths, and anchor attention on a steady object of meditation such as the breath, bodily pressure, or sound.

- The distressing racial situation brought to mind earlier in the session is revisited, with participants invited to notice physical sensations, thoughts, emotions, and impulses as they arise without trying to eliminate or follow them.

- Attention is alternated between the grounding object and the distressing memory in order to build the capacity to remain present with difficulty while maintaining stability.

- Practical anchoring options are offered for moments of overwhelm, including opening the eyes, feeling the feet on the floor, holding a physical object, placing a hand on the heart, or taking a deep breath.

- Participants are reminded that these practices take time to develop and that this session is not a final opportunity, encouraging a patient and experimental approach to trying them on.

### Next Steps

- Participants are directed to watch a video about Corinne Gaines, a young African-American woman shot and killed by police on August 1st 2016, while practicing mindful observation of their arising experience.

- Following the video, participants are encouraged to continue mindfulness practice for several minutes, noting bodily sensations, emotions, and thoughts without judgment.

- Journaling about the experience after the video is recommended as a way to process and articulate what arose.

- Affinity home groups are identified as the setting where participants will have the opportunity to share their journal entries and engage further with the session's material.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Metta Meditation Adapted for Racial Awareness**
- Kitsie introduced a slightly adapted metta meditation specifically oriented toward racial conditioning
- Four phrases offered for practice:
  - May I be safe from inner and outer harm
  - May I feel strong in body and mind
  - May I hold my racial conditioning in ways seen and unseen with compassion and understanding
  - May I know my own true nature
- Participants were invited to modify phrases to suit their own experience
- The purpose was described as watering the seeds of loving kindness toward oneself
**White Culture vs. White Supremacy Culture**
- White culture refers to the cultural practices of white people
- White supremacy culture asserts that those practices are the best or only way of being in the world
- These are presented as distinct concepts
**The Good White Self**
- Defined as the racialized aspect of a supposedly fixed personal identity
- Rooted in the desire to be seen as a good person — not just accomplished, but morally good
- The problem arises when this identity is treated as something fixed to be protected rather than something enacted
- Defending the good white self keeps individuals stuck, prevents learning, and perpetuates white privilege
**Defense Mechanisms — Denial of Oppression and White Privilege**
- "I don't see color" — denial of racial difference and differential treatment
- Belief in meritocracy — suggesting that hard work alone determines outcomes equally for all
- "I worked hard for what I have" — implying others who are not privileged did not work hard enough
- Spiritual bypass — using spiritual ideas to sidestep racial pain; coined by John Wellwood in the 1980s, defined as using spiritual ideas and practices to sidestep personal, emotional, unfinished business
**Defense Mechanisms — Making Use of BIPOC**
- Wanting BIPOC to educate white people about their experience
- Making BIPOC responsible for creating equity within organizations
- Seeking approval or redemption from BIPOC — described as wanting the "cool white person stamp"
- Assuming all BIPOC want to befriend or engage with white people; presumption of familiarity
- Tokenism — placing people of color on diversity committees and expecting them to fix systemic issues
**Defense Mechanisms — Enacting Privilege Interpersonally**
- Withdrawing from or changing the subject in race-based conversations
- Asserting the right to be seen as an individual, a privilege not afforded to non-white people
- Using shame, guilt, or distress as a showstopper in racial conversations, redirecting attention to the white person's emotional state
- Accusing BIPOC of "playing the race card" when they name racial oppression
- Insisting on good intent over harmful impact — deflecting from the harm caused by focusing on what was meant
**Defense Mechanisms — Enacting Privilege Intrapersonally**
- Assuming one's own perspective is objective or correct
- Expecting BIPOC to convince white people that their experience is valid
- Blaming BIPOC for causing upset when they express their own painful experiences
- Believing feelings are facts — e.g., if one feels attacked, assuming an attack occurred
- "White person vacations" — using confusion, distraction, sadness, or doubt to disengage from the work
**Turning Towards Suffering**
- The purpose of mindfulness is not to turn away from pain but to look deeply into it
- Turning away from suffering also means turning away from compassion
- Pity is identified as the near enemy of compassion — it separates rather than joins
- White people have been systematically taught not to see or feel the suffering of BIPOC through segregation, dehumanizing labels, and media portrayals
- The system of white supremacy requires the cooperation and non-awareness of white people
- Once connected to the pain of oppression, individuals can ask whether it aligns with their values
- Building capacity to stay with pain is described as deeply healing for white people
**Grounding Practice for Difficult Experiences**
- Participants were guided to settle into a comfortable posture and focus on a steady object of meditation
- Practices introduced: pause, relax, open; RAIN practice; grounding in breath, body pressure, or sound
- Participants were guided to bring to mind a racially charged situation they regretted and notice physical sensations, thoughts, emotions, and impulses without pushing away or following
- The practice was framed as building stamina to stay present without being overwhelmed or shutting down

- Bringing compassion to one's own racial conditioning is framed as the only way to model compassion when interrupting racism

**Humility as a Practice**
- Racial conditioning is often invisible to white people and most likely to surface in cross-race interactions
- A strong humility practice is described as essential; a teaching on humility by the Dalai Lama is referenced as supplementary material
**Mindfulness as a Tool for Racial Awareness**

- Naming defenses in advance — as Crystal and Kitsie do in this session — allows practitioners to recognize them in the moment and choose a different response

### Stories
**Crystal's Experience with a Student's Feedback**
- Crystal recounts that when she and Kitsie first taught this class together, she gave a talk on whiteness in America
- After the talk, a student told Crystal that she spoke as if all Native Americans are dead
- Crystal's initial reaction was physical — she felt her body catch fire, began to blush, felt tightness in her chest, and had thoughts rejecting the feedback
- She used the pause, relax, open practice, took a deep breath, and reflected
- She realized that while she would never consciously believe all Native Americans are dead, she had internalized from middle school history classes the idea that the American genocide was in the past and over
- She recognized that this defense protected her from sitting with the ongoing suffering of Native American communities
- She noted that millions of Native Americans are living in under-resourced, highly stressed conditions, exacerbated by COVID-19 deaths and illness
- She identified that acknowledging this implicates her as a white person and that part of her would rather deny it to protect her identity as a good white person
**Kitsie's Experience at a Diversity Meeting**
- Kitsie describes being in a diversity meeting at Rotunda Mental Health Clinic
- Therapists of color were sharing how race affects them, their clients, and their experience in a predominantly white workplace
- Kitsie responded by saying "Well, as a gay person..." — shifting the conversation to an identity where she was on the receiving end of oppression rather than the perpetrating end
- She acknowledges this was a way of escaping the race-based discomfort by changing the subject
- Another white lesbian in the meeting intervened, saying she had said the same thing before and redirecting the conversation back to the original topic
- Kitsie describes this intervention as having a significant impact on her and as an inspiring model of how to respond in such moments
