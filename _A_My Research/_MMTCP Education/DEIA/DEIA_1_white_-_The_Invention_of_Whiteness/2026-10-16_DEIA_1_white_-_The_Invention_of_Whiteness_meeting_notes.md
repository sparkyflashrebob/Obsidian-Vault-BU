### Outline
### Welcome and Program Introduction

- Presented as a journey of bringing mindfulness and compassion to the ways participants have been shaped by systemic racism, with an acknowledgment that significant healing is available through this work.

- Diversity within the white-identified group is welcomed, encompassing varied national and cultural backgrounds, economic situations, body types, abilities, levels of prior training experience, ages, faith traditions, spiritual practices, activist identities, languages, genders, and sexual orientations.

- Described as believed to be the first mindfulness teacher certification program to explicitly take a stand that mindfulness must account for race and other systemic oppressions, moving the practice beyond self-care and wellness toward the ethics of non-harming.

- Combining mindfulness with anti-racist theory and perspectives is presented as creating a synergy that enhances and enlarges both practices.

- Participants are told they do not need to set out to change the world to have a positive impact, as developing awareness of how systems have shaped them and modeling new ways of relating across racial divides is itself meaningful.

- Upon completing the two-year program, participants are expected to have the skills to make meaningful apologies when harm is caused, to address harmful interactions as they arise in their classes, and to create mindfulness environments that are truly welcoming to all.

- The training focuses primarily on race within the United States context, while acknowledging that racism and white supremacy extend globally and that the tools provided are applicable across many dimensions of oppression and privilege, including sexism, transphobia, ableism, religious persecution, class, and caste.

- Participants from outside the United States are asked to try on the information being shared and to remain curious about any strong reactions they may have, as reactivity is understood to signal something worth examining further.

### Purpose of the White Affinity Group Structure

- Working on race within a white affinity group is explained as a response to the call from Black, Indigenous, and people of color asking white people to do their own work rather than learning at the emotional, physical, and spiritual expense of people of color.

- White participants are understood to have a specific role in compassionately and courageously reaching out to other white people to address racism and racial harm as it arises.

- A key goal of the affinity group is to counter the illusion of separation, challenging the notion that individuals can be unaffected by their history and social context.

- Aspects of dominant US culture, particularly the emphasis on individualism and competition, are identified as contributing to a tendency among white people to compete over who is the better white person, and the training aims to counter this by discouraging the othering of fellow participants.

- The affinity group is framed as a valuable resource for participants' mutual journeys rather than a space for judgment or comparison.

### Agreements for Multicultural Interactions

- Having explicit guidelines for group interactions is described as essential to creating an inclusive environment, because when people from different cultures gather without intentional structure, the default tends to be dominant white culture, which is not comfortable for BIPOC individuals.

- The first agreement is to try on new ideas or ways of doing things that may feel unfamiliar or uncomfortable, with the understanding that discomfort signals new ground rather than danger.

- Refraining from blaming or shaming oneself or others is the second agreement, with the emphasis placed on understanding what has happened and recognizing that everyone is working from their best intentions.

- Moving up or moving back is a practice that invites frequent speakers to step back and quieter participants to bring their voices forward, acknowledging that those on the privileged end of racial dynamics may feel more comfortable speaking up.

- Practicing self-focus means speaking from one's own experience and avoiding speaking on behalf of others or the group as a whole.

- The both-and practice encourages substituting the word "and" for "but" when speaking, in order to honor multiple realities simultaneously rather than negating one perspective with another.

- Confidentiality means taking away learnings from the group without identifying the individuals who shared them, and checking with someone before revisiting something they shared in a group setting.

- Every participant holds the right to pass at any moment if they do not wish to speak.

- Mindful listening requires full attention to what another person is saying rather than preparing one's own response while they are speaking, creating openness to the surprise of others' experiences.

- The final agreement focuses on acknowledging impact over intent, meaning that when someone indicates harm has been caused, the priority is to believe that person and acknowledge the impact of one's words or actions rather than redirecting focus to one's own intentions, as denying another person's reality is itself a tool of oppression.

### The Invention of Whiteness and Race as a Social Construction

- The foundational point of the session is that race is not a biological reality but a social construction, meaning it is a category created for social and political purposes rather than one grounded in genetics.

- No genetic markers exist for race, and the visible characteristics used to categorize people by race, such as skin color, hair texture, and eye color, have no correlation with traits such as intelligence, athleticism, or criminality.

- Greater genetic difference exists between people of the same so-called race than between different races, further demonstrating that racial categories do not reflect biological reality.

- Historical attempts by scientists in the US and Europe to measure skulls and categorize people into races consistently ranked the European or Caucasian group at the top, illustrating how the categorization was constructed to serve existing power structures rather than to reflect external reality.

- Although race is not biologically real, it is a profound social reality with significant impact on the lives of both white people and BIPOC individuals.

- The construction of whiteness began to gather momentum in the 15th century, driven by European powers expanding outward for land and resources and by the initiation of the transatlantic slave trade.

- Assertions of European and later white superiority were used over subsequent centuries to justify the theft of land, the murder of indigenous inhabitants, and the brutal enslavement of African people, grounded in the claim that white people are genetically superior and entitled to take land and resources.

- The Doctrine of Discovery, particularly the papal bull issued by Pope Alexander VI in 1493, authorized Christian explorers working for Christian monarchs to seize any land not already occupied by Christians, fueling white supremacy by framing European settlers as instruments of divine design with cultural superiority.

- This doctrine was applied by European countries across the Americas, Australia, New Zealand, Africa, and Asia to build empires and claim land through settler colonialism, supported by the concept of terra nullius, which held that land was vacant and free for the taking even if inhabited by non-Christian peoples.

- Under this authorization, Europeans forced indigenous people from lands they had occupied for thousands of years, practiced genocide, forced relocation, and engaged in forced assimilation, including the residential school system where Native children were taken from their families, forbidden from speaking their languages or practicing their cultures, subjected to high death rates and widespread physical and sexual abuse.

- In Canada during the 19th and early 20th centuries, approximately 30 percent of Native children were taken into residential schools, with even higher percentages in the United States and around 15 percent in Australia, leaving a traumatic impact that reverberates to the present day.

- The groundwork for anti-Black racism, as traced by Ibram X. Kendi in Stamped from the Beginning, rested on two justifications for the slave trade, namely that Africans were heathens who would benefit from Christian salvation, and that Africans were inferior and bestial.

- Portuguese traders brought the first African slaves to Portugal in 1444 and completed the first transatlantic slave voyage to Brazil in 1526, with an estimated 12 million human beings taken captive over subsequent centuries, approximately 40 percent going to Brazil, 20 percent to the British West Indies, and 10 percent to the United States.

- Enslaved Africans' labor was exploited to create vast fortunes for European royal houses and colonial settlers, enriching European elites at the direct expense of African people.

### The Creation of Whiteness in the United States

- In the early years of the American colonies, the primary labor force consisted of European indentured servants arriving from 1607 and African slaves arriving from 1619, and these two groups worked together, lived together, intermarried, and raised families without a strong enforced sense of racial hierarchy.

- Over time, the small elite of landowners recognized that solidarity between African slaves and European servants posed a serious threat to their power, a danger made concrete by Bacon's Rebellion in 1676, in which African slaves and European indentured servants rose up together against the landowning class.

- In response, the elite used the legal system and social custom to create a sharp separation between the two groups, first by making slavery permanent and heritable for Black people and outlawing relationships between Black and white people.

- Increasing privileges were extended to European indentured servants, including land grants upon completing their terms, the right to own land, positions as overseers of enslaved people, and roles in slave patrols, which are understood as the foundation of the modern policing system in the United States.

- By the time of the first US census in 1790, the term "white" had fully replaced the term "European," and the census categories of white males over 16, white males under 16, white women, other free people, and slaves codified the racial definition of whiteness into law.

- Between 1830 and 1930, approximately 60 to 65 million people emigrated from Europe, out of a population of roughly 115 million in 1850, with about 32 million going to the United States and millions more to Argentina, Canada, Brazil, Australia, New Zealand, and elsewhere, reinforcing white elites in settler-colonized lands.

- White supremacy was maintained across these countries through a range of practices including restricting citizenship to white people, using lynchings as systemic violence against Black, Chinese, and Mexican people, imposing immigration restrictions, enforcing legal segregation in the American South and social segregation in the North, implementing the White Australia policy to exclude Asian immigrants, and pursuing racial whitening policies in Brazil through mass European immigration.

- Whiteness varies in its definition by national context, further demonstrating its constructed nature, as seen in the contrast between the United States, where whiteness has been strictly reserved for those of exclusively European ancestry, and Brazil, where a 2000 survey found that a majority of self-identified white Brazilians acknowledged Indigenous and African heritage, reflecting a different colonial history in which the early Portuguese settler elite was predominantly male and formed families with Indigenous and African women.

- Mindfulness practice is identified as a powerful tool for investigating the internal experience of race as a social construction, helping individuals understand how racial identity is constructed within themselves and how they personally experience and have been shaped by it.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Race as Social Construction**
- Race has no genetic or biological basis; it is a made-up category with social and political purposes
- There are no genetic markers that define race
- Racial categories are based on superficial characteristics: skin color, hair color and texture, eye color
- There is more genetic difference between people of the same so-called race than between races
- Race is very much a social reality despite not being a biological one, with profound impact on everyone's life
- Scientists in the U.S. and Europe historically measured skulls and used other practices to categorize people into races, always ranking European/Caucasian at the top
**The Doctrine of Discovery**
- A series of papal bulls asserting the superiority of Christian people and granting them the right to seize lands not occupied by Christians
- The key papal bull was issued in 1493 by Pope Alexander VI
- Included the concept of terra nullius — "vacant land" — meaning land could be claimed as empty even if inhabited by non-Christian peoples
- Used by European countries in the Americas, Australia, New Zealand, Africa, and Asia to build empires and claim land
- European settlers claimed they were instruments of divine design with cultural superiority
**Settler Colonialism and Indigenous Peoples**
- Europeans forced Indigenous people from lands occupied for thousands of years
- Indigenous people were labeled primitive, inferior, and uncivilized
- Practices included genocide, forced relocation, and forced assimilation
- Residential schools kidnapped Native children from their homes to eliminate Indigenous culture and language
- High death rates, physical and sexual abuse were common in residential schools
- In Canada during the 19th and early 20th century, approximately 30% of Native children were taken
- In the United States the percentage was higher; in Australia approximately 15%
- These schools existed across the Americas, Australia, and New Zealand
**The Transatlantic Slave Trade and Anti-Black Racism**
- Ibram X. Kendi's book Stamped from the Beginning traces the European history of anti-Blackness
- Two justifications for the slave trade: Africans as heathens needing salvation, and Africans as inferior or bestial
- In 1444, the Portuguese brought the first African slaves to Portugal
- In 1526, the first transatlantic slave voyage to Brazil was completed
- Approximately 12 million human beings were taken captive over subsequent centuries
- Approximately 40% went to Brazil, 20% to the British West Indies, 10% to the United States
- Enslaved Africans' labor created vast fortunes for European royal houses and colonial settlers
**The Creation of Whiteness in the United States**
- Early colonial labor force consisted of European indentured servants (arriving 1607) and African slaves (arriving 1619)
- In early colonial America, African slaves and European indentured servants worked together, lived together, and intermarried
- Bacon's Rebellion in 1676 — a joint uprising of African slaves and European indentured servants against the landowning class — alarmed the elite
- The elite used the legal system and social custom to separate Africans from European servants
- Slavery was made permanent and heritable for Black people
- Marriage and relationships between Black and white people were outlawed
- Increasing privileges were granted to European indentured servants: land upon completion of service, right to own land, roles as overseers of slaves, service on slave patrols
- Slave patrols are identified as the formation of the modern U.S. policing system

**Mass European Immigration and Global White Supremacy**

- This reflects the constructed, socially defined nature of race

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Myth of White Superiority as a Tool of Power**
- The assertion of white superiority was constructed and deployed over centuries to justify theft of land, murder of inhabitants, and enslavement of human beings
- It was not an observed external reality but a socially defined and legally enforced category
- The particulars of whiteness vary by national context, further demonstrating its constructed nature
**The Invisibility of White Privilege**
- White privilege is largely invisible to white people because they do not experience the friction of a system working against them
- White people typically do not see the person who did not get the job or the apartment; they simply experience their own access
- The system of race works for white people, making it difficult for them to perceive
**The Illusion of Separation**
- A key theme is countering the illusion that individuals are separate from their history and social context
- U.S. cultural values of individualism and competition can lead people to deny the impact of systemic forces on their lives
- The focus on the self and competition can create a dynamic of "who is the better white person," which the training works to counter
**Racism as Structural, Not Just Attitudinal**
- Holding the value of being against racism does not prevent one from acting out racism in interactions
- Changing laws without changing people and practices leaves inequity intact
- The structural benefits of whiteness — such as the GI Bill — were not equally available to people of color, yet this was rarely made visible
**The Role of White People in Addressing Racism**
- White people have a specific role in compassionately and courageously reaching out to other white people to address racism and racial harm
- For too long, white people have learned about race at the emotional, physical, and spiritual expense of people of color
- The call from Black, Indigenous, and people of color is for white people to do their own work
**Mindfulness as an Ethical Practice**
- Mindfulness is framed not only as personal wellness but as an embodiment of the ethics of non-harming
- Becoming aware of how one has been shaped by systems creates the opportunity to respond differently and model new ways of relating across racial and other divides

### Stories
**Chrystal Johnson — Growing Up in an All-White Community in Southern California**
Chrystal Johnson shares that she grew up in Southern California in the 1960s and early 1970s in an all-white neighborhood attending all-white schools. The civil rights movement was seen as something happening far away in the American South, where the "racist white people" lived. Her community believed racism did not exist among them. She was taught that everyone is equal and should be treated with respect and kindness. In her senior year of high school, the school integrated by bringing Black football players from Southeast San Diego to join the football team. A Black football player began walking her between classes, a social custom indicating romantic interest. A girlfriend pulled her aside and told her she should not date him. Chrystal understood the message and the serious social consequences that would follow. She realized she was not supposed to talk about it — the subject was never raised again, and she had no one to ask for guidance. She describes this as a profound contradiction: the stated value of equality alongside unspoken rules that enforced racial separation.
**Kitsie Shum — Growing Up in a Liberal Family with Structural Racism Invisible**
Kitsie Shum shares that she was raised in a family that considered itself liberal and believed in racial justice in theory. Her mother was from the South and her maternal grandmother was described as less racist than her father's side of the family from Pennsylvania, who were described as profoundly racist and anti-Semitic. By the time Kitsie was old enough to engage with these issues, her family had moved to an all-white suburb. Her family talked about racism being bad and even discussed redlining. She notes that Birmingham, Michigan, where she grew up, was reportedly the first all-white city to pass a fair housing ordinance, and her parents supported that effort. However, the demographics of the area did not change for decades. A lasting image for her is of African-American women waiting at a bus stop in the rain in this wealthy suburb, with no shelter, before taking an hour-long bus ride back to Detroit. She reflects that the structural benefits her family received — such as the GI Bill — were not available to Black people and people of color in the same way, yet this was never made visible. The family's opposition to racism as a value did not translate into awareness of how the system was actively working in their favor.
