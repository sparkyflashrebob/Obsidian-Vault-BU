### DEIA 5 White – Panel: Calling Other White People
📅 October 16, 2026
👥 Crystal (Lead Facilitator), Kitsie (Co-Facilitator)
Tags: DEIA, Racial Awareness, Mindfulness, White Supremacy Culture, Microaggressions, Calling In, Calling Out, Compassion, Embodied Learning, Role Play, Affinity Groups
### Outline
### Session Overview and Learning Objectives

- Racial awareness training for white mindfulness teachers is the focus of this ongoing series, with the current session building on previous work around microaggressions in mindfulness classes.

- Responding skillfully when microaggressions arise is described as an essential skill for creating an inclusive environment for practice and learning.

- Prior session content covered how to respond when one is personally called out or called in, while this session focuses on developing skills to call other white people in.

- Embodied learning techniques are introduced as the primary method for developing these interpersonal skills throughout the session.

- The session structure includes a discussion of the pull toward calling people out, tips for calling in, a live demonstration, and an introduction to the affinity home group exercise.

### Opening Mindfulness Practice - Pause Relax Open

- A two-minute guided practice called pause, relax, open is offered at the start of the session as a tool for gathering attention and energy before engaging with difficult material.

- Participants are invited to take a comfortable position, close their eyes or lower their gaze, and take several deep breaths while noticing physical sensations without trying to change them.

- Attention is then directed to the state of the mind, observing whether it is agitated or active, again without identifying with or following thoughts.

- A final stage of the practice involves opening awareness beyond the body-mind into a larger field, arriving in a state of readiness and presence for what is to come.

### The Role of White People in Dismantling Systemic Racism

- A core belief shared by Crystal and Kitsie is that white people have a specific role to play in dismantling systemic racism and white supremacy, described as powerfully destructive and harmful forces present in much of the world.

- That role is defined as compassionately reaching out to other white people in order to interrupt harm.

- A common question raised in white communities is whether simply refraining from causing harm is sufficient, and the answer offered is that it is not, because the system of racial oppression requires active cooperation to be sustained.

- Failing to intervene is framed as supporting the system of harm, while courageous and compassionate outreach to other white people is described as planting seeds of meaningful change.

- Modeling this behavior in a teaching role is highlighted as particularly impactful, as it demonstrates a new way of handling racial situations to students who may never have witnessed it before.

### White Cultural Factors That Pull Toward Othering

- A focus on the self is identified as one aspect of white culture that can prevent engagement with collective change, keeping attention on individual behavior rather than broader transformation.

- Competition and the desire to be seen as the best are described as additional factors that activate judging and comparing mind, predisposing people to other the white person who has said something unskillful.

- Calling out rather than calling in is framed as watering seeds of shame and blame, which gives rise to reactivity and aversion, likened to pouring poison on the seeds of change.

- A cultural value of niceness and not wanting to rock the boat is identified as another barrier, where speaking up and provoking an upset response can feel like having done something wrong.

- Microaggressions themselves are noted as not being nice, and the boat being protected through silence is identified as the boat of white supremacy culture.

- Speaking up breaks a white cultural rule about never discussing race, which means negative responses are possible and effectiveness cannot be guaranteed, making relationship-building skills especially important.

### Embodied Learning Exercise - Watching the Video with Two Different Orientations

- Participants are directed to watch an external video titled an example of racial bias, unconscious racial bias in the workplace, as the basis for an embodied learning exercise.

- For the first viewing, participants are asked to focus specifically on their reaction to what the white woman in the video does, and to physically embody that reaction, such as wanting to look away or distance themselves.

- Crystal describes her first-viewing reaction as a physical cringe, a desire to distance herself from the white woman, and critical thoughts questioning what the woman was thinking.

- Kitsie describes a near-panic response during the first viewing, wanting to intervene and simultaneously wanting to flee, with a strong sense of disconnection and pain.

- For the second viewing, participants are asked to settle into pause, relax, open and optionally place a hand on the heart to cultivate compassion while watching.

- Crystal's second viewing brought greater awareness of the pain in the interaction, including the woman's separation and cluelessness, and prompted reflection on similar moments in her own past.

- Kitsie's second viewing allowed for holding both distress for the African American colleague and recognition of the white woman's lack of racial awareness, resulting in a greater felt sense of being able to reach out to her.

- The purpose of the two-viewing structure is to give participants the embodied experience of how othering works and how shifting orientation can move one toward connection rather than distance.

### Tips for Calling In Other White People

- Staying rooted in the intention of courageous or fierce compassion is offered as the first tip, as it serves as a guiding anchor during difficult moments.

- Practicing pause, relax, open is recommended as an invaluable way to gather energy and attention before responding skillfully.

- Joining with the other person by assuming the absence of malicious intent is suggested as a way to move toward rather than away from them.

- Verbal joining phrases such as "I'm wondering if maybe you're not realizing how that landed" or "I can understand how you see that" are offered as ways to signal solidarity and shared learning rather than superiority.

- Using self-focus rather than leading with "you" is recommended to reduce defensiveness, with an example being "I noticed I felt uncomfortable when you said this."

- Acknowledging having used similar language oneself in the past is noted as another form of self-focus that can be genuine and disarming.

- When people of color are present, it is important not to speak on their behalf or ask them to confirm the accuracy of what is being said, as the speaker should be acting on their own account.

- Engaging the other person with curiosity, such as asking what they were thinking when they said something, creates spaciousness in the conversation and allows for checking what specifically is being responded to.

- Speaking plainly and descriptively rather than euphemistically or with emotional reactivity is advised, with specific factual language being more effective than broad labels like "that was so racist."

- A participant-contributed tip called WAIT, standing for why am I talking, is introduced as a way to check whether the motivation to speak is rooted in wanting approval or in genuine commitment to interrupting harm.

- Separating the person from the action is identified as critical, ensuring the feedback targets a specific word or behavior rather than the person's value or character.

- Using a placeholder such as "ouch, something doesn't feel right, can we come back to this" is recommended for moments when discomfort is present but the source is not yet clear.

- Simply saying something, even imperfectly, is emphasized as a significant step given the tendency to freeze or wait for someone else to speak.

### Role Play Demonstration - Three Versions of Responding to a Microaggression

- Crystal and Kitsie demonstrate three versions of responding to the same microaggression, the phrase "working like a slave," to illustrate the difference between calling out, using a placeholder, and calling in.

- In the first version, Kitsie calls out Crystal's use of the word slave immediately and sharply, referencing chattel slavery and telling her she has no business using the term.

- Kitsie reflects that the call-out felt righteous in the moment but was clearly not going anywhere, and was accompanied by a sense of knowing better than the other person.

- Crystal reflects that being called out in that way made her feel interrupted and angry, as she had been trying to share about a difficult week.

- In the second version, Kitsie uses a placeholder, acknowledging the hard week while noting discomfort with the word slave and asking to return to it later without yet knowing exactly why.

- Crystal reflects that the placeholder version left her slightly uneasy and curious, but appreciative that the conversation was not entirely derailed.

- In the third version, Kitsie calls Crystal in by first affirming the difficulty of her week, then naming the distraction caused by the word slave, asking if they could address it briefly, acknowledging having used the word herself, and describing how it landed as trivializing slavery without assuming harmful intent.

- Crystal reflects that the calling-in version felt normalizing and made the feedback actually hearable, noting that the acknowledgment of shared experience was what made the difference.

- Kitsie acknowledges that the level of clarity demonstrated in the third version is rarely available in real moments, reinforcing the value of the placeholder as a practical tool.

### Evaluating the Effectiveness of Calling In

- Effectiveness of calling in cannot be judged solely by how the other person responds, as even a skillful intervention may still produce defensiveness or upset due to internalized shame around race.

- Conversely, a cold call-out can receive praise without having planted any seeds of meaningful change, and blaming and shaming others actually harms those seeds by evoking resistance.

- A more reliable internal indicator of being on the right track is noticing one's own internal experience during and after speaking, with feelings of excitement, triumph, or anger suggesting that othering has occurred.

- Feelings of confusion, pain, discomfort, or even a degree of personal shame are described as signs that genuine connection has been maintained and that the speaker is acting from a place of wanting to prevent harm rather than superiority.

- Throwing another white person under the bus by discussing their unskillful behavior with people of color in order to appear woke is identified as a manifestation of the good white self dynamic that should be avoided.

- Going directly to the person who caused harm, rather than seeking approval from others, is presented as the more compassionate and constructive course of action.

- Bringing compassion to oneself and to others around racial conditioning is framed as parallel processes, with the way one others fellow white people mirroring how one approaches one's own conditioning.

### Next Steps

- Participants are directed to complete the affinity home group exercise, which involves role-playing calling-in scenarios from a provided set of three options using the process demonstrated in the session.

- Debriefing within the home groups is to focus only on what worked better in the placeholder and calling-in versions, in keeping with the agreements to refrain from blaming and shaming.

- Groups are reminded to share time equally so that all participants have the opportunity to role-play.

- The agreements of self-focus, refraining from blaming and shaming self and others, and understanding the difference between intent and impact are to be maintained throughout the exercise and debriefing.

- Specificity in feedback is emphasized as a key goal, as more specific observations are more likely to be understood and less likely to result in repeated harm.

- The next gathering is identified as session six.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Role of White People in Dismantling Racism**
- Crystal and Kitsie assert that white people have a specific role to play in dismantling systemic racism and white supremacy
- That role is to compassionately reach out to other white people to interrupt harm
- Simply not doing harm oneself is insufficient; non-intervention supports the system of racial oppression
- Intervening creates seeds of change, especially when modeled in a teaching role
**White Cultural Factors That Pull Away from Intervention**
- Focus on self: leads to thinking only about individual behavior rather than collective change
- Competition and judging mind: predisposes people to other the white person who said something unskillful
- Niceness and not rocking the boat: speaking up can feel like doing something wrong
- Microaggressions are not nice; the boat being rocked is that of white supremacy culture
- Silence allows harm to fester without acknowledgment
**Calling Out vs. Calling In**
- Calling out: shaming and blaming, waters seeds of shame, pours poison on seeds of change, evokes resistance and defensiveness
- Calling in: based on compassion and connection, does not compound harm, engages people in mutual efforts to relieve suffering
- Effectiveness of calling in cannot be judged solely by how the other person responds
- A cold call-out can receive praise without sowing meaningful change

**Tips for Calling In Other White People**
- Stay rooted in the intention for courageous or fierce compassion
- Practice pause, relax, open to gather energy and attention for skillful response
- Join with the person: assume no malicious intent, move toward rather than away
- Use verbal joining: "I'm wondering if maybe you're not realizing how that landed," or "I can understand how you see that"
- Acknowledge one's own learning curve; present as a fellow learner, not an authority
- Use self-focus language: begin with "I noticed I was uncomfortable" rather than "you"
- Example: "I noticed I felt uncomfortable when you said this, and this is what came up for me"
- Acknowledge having used similar language oneself when genuine and accurate
- Do not speak for people of color present or put them on the spot to confirm your statements
- Speak for oneself, not as a savior acting on behalf of others
- Engage with curiosity: "I'm wondering what you were thinking when you said that"
- Speak plainly and descriptively rather than euphemistically or with pure emotional reactivity
- Example of specific language: "When you said this is a safe space for everyone, I was surprised. I know that people of color here have often said it's not safe for them"
- Use the WAIT check: Why Am I Talking? Check whether motivation is approval-seeking or genuine commitment to interrupt harm
- Separate the person from the action: focus on what was said or done, not on the person's value
- Use a placeholder when uncertain: "Ouch, something's not sitting right with me, can we come back to this?"
- Say something rather than freezing; even a placeholder is a meaningful step
- Bring in others present to help debrief the situation

- Wanting approval from people of color is a form of attachment to the good white self

- Othering others mirrors how one approaches one's own racial conditioning; compassion is needed for both

**Affinity Home Group Exercise**
- Participants choose from three provided scenarios to role play calling in another white person around a racial microaggression

- Debrief focuses only on what worked better in the placeholder and calling in versions
- Agreements during exercise: refrain from blaming and shaming self and others, practice self-focus, understand the difference between intent and impact
- Specificity of feedback helps the person understand and reduces likelihood of repetition
- Share time equally so everyone has a chance to role play
### Spiritual and Societal Insights

- Placing a hand on the heart to call forth compassion is offered as a somatic anchor during difficult interactions

**Collective Responsibility and Systemic Harm**
- Racial oppression requires cooperation; silence is a form of support for the system
- Individual non-harm is insufficient; collective change requires active intervention
- Modeling calling in within a teaching role has a ripple effect on observers who may never have witnessed this approach before
**Shame, Blame, and the Seeds of Change**
- Calling out waters seeds of shame and blame, producing reactivity and aversion

- One's internal experience during and after calling in is a more reliable indicator of skillfulness than the other person's response

- Confusion, pain, discomfort, and awareness of one's own similar conditioning signals genuine connection and presence
**Compassion Toward Self and Other as Inseparable**

- Bringing compassion to others requires bringing it to oneself first
