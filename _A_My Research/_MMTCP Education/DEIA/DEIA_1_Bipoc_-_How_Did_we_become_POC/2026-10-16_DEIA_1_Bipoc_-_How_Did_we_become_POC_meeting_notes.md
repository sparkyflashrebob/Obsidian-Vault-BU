### Outline
### Overview and Purpose of the DEIA Training Series

- Developed over six months, the training focuses on diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility for participants who identify as Black, Indigenous, and people of color.

- Creation of the program was requested by past cohorts of the Mindfulness Meditation Teacher Certification Program, particularly the first and second cohorts, who expressed a need for a separate, safer space to discuss race-related experiences.

- Jack Kornfield and Tara Brock commissioned Conda Mason and Kate Johnson to develop the training to better support emerging mindfulness teachers of color.

- A parallel training for white colleagues is being facilitated simultaneously by Kitsie Schoen and Crystal Johnson, reflecting a complementary and coordinated approach to DEIA education.

- Smaller affinity home groups, distinct from mentor groups, are established as spaces where BIPOC participants can reflect on their experiences in a more intimate and nurturing setting.

### Scope of Inclusion and Welcome Extended to Participants

- All racial and ethnic groups are explicitly welcomed, including Latino, Latina, and Latinx individuals, First Nations and Indigenous peoples from around the world, those of African, Middle Eastern, Asian, and Pacific Islander descent, mixed-race individuals, and those with European ancestry.

- All gender identities are welcomed, including women, men, transgender, and gender-nonconforming individuals, alongside all sexual orientations including gay, lesbian, bisexual, two-spirit, queer, and heterosexual.

- Emotional states of all kinds are acknowledged and welcomed, including nervousness, hope, despair, confusion, and overwhelm, recognizing that working with identity and internalized oppression can evoke a wide range of feelings.

- Participants of all ages, abilities, disabilities, political views, and linguistic backgrounds are included, with recognition that English is the primary language of the program but many other languages are spoken by attendees.

- Those who have prior knowledge of race and racism, whether from academic settings, workshops, or lived experience, as well as those with no prior formal exposure, are equally welcomed.

### Benefits of the Training for BIPOC Mindfulness Teachers

- Marginalized individuals often come to mindfulness and meditation practice seeking healing from the specific harms caused by racial injustice and oppression, and the training aims to prepare teachers to steward those experiences for their students.

- Participants are supported in bringing their whole selves to their teaching practice and in relating effectively with students across a wide range of identities and lived experiences.

- Practical tools are provided for recognizing and responding to racism, colorism, and microaggressions that BIPOC teachers may encounter not only in public but also within mindfulness organizations, from fellow teachers, and even from students.

- Coming together as a community of BIPOC practitioners is itself identified as a benefit, offering richness, heart, and vibrancy that deepens collegial relationships.

### Six-Session Training Curriculum Overview

- Session one addresses the historical origins of race and the concept of people of color, exploring how racial categories came to exist and what that means for mindfulness teachers.

- Session two covers the twin dynamics of race-based oppression and white supremacy.

- Session three examines solidarity within the BIPOC community, focusing on how dynamics of privilege and oppression manifest even within communities of color around issues of class, color, and geography.

- Session four focuses on resourcing and self-care strategies for teachers who experience microaggressions, bias, or racism while teaching.

- Session five features three guest teachers of color who will share their experiences of teaching mindfulness around the world.

- Session six addresses how to center color and culture in the mindfulness classroom and how to build inclusive communities and classes.

### Historical Origins of Race as a Social Construct

- The term race emerged in the 16th century as a neutral classificatory label with no hierarchical meaning, used simply to categorize groups of human beings.

- During the 17th century, Europeans began using race as a system of ranking, positioning themselves as superior to the peoples they were colonizing and enslaving.

- Bacon's Rebellion of 1676 in Virginia was a pivotal moment in American racial history, representing an interracial uprising of indentured servants, both white and black, as well as Indigenous people, against the planter class elite.

- Following the rebellion, colonial authorities deliberately separated white indentured servants from Black and Indigenous ones, granting white servants land and elevated status, thereby creating a racial hierarchy designed to prevent future cross-racial solidarity.

- This separation formalized the concept of whiteness as a political and social category, establishing a permanent upper class defined by white identity and a permanent lower class, constructed to justify slavery and genocide and to protect elite economic interests.

- Race is not a biological concept but a social construction, with no genetic or scientific basis, as all human beings share 99.9% of the same DNA.

- From a scientific standpoint, all humans are more closely related to one another than chimpanzees are within their own species, and in a genetic sense, all people today trace their origins to Africa.

### Origins and Evolution of the Term People of Color

- The phrase people of color originated in 18th-century France as a designation for mixed-race individuals in colonized territories, distinguishing them from full French citizens and from Black people.

- Usage of the term spread to South Africa, where the word colored remains in common use today to describe those who are neither white nor Black.

- In the late 20th century, the term persons of color was introduced in the United States to replace the condescending labels minority and non-white, and was defined by a shared experience of marginalization rather than by a specific racial category.

- At the 1977 National Women's Conference, women who were neither Black nor white joined the Black Women's Agenda and collectively renamed it the Women of Color agenda, marking a significant moment of political solidarity across racial lines.

- The term people of color was intended to represent pride, respect, creativity, and joy, and to move racial discourse beyond a simple Black-and-white binary by creating an inclusive designation for the many groups that do not fit neatly into either category.

### Introduction and Significance of the BIPOC Designation

- The acronym BIPOC, standing for Black, Indigenous, and People of Color, has emerged as a replacement for the term POC in contemporary usage.

- Distinguishing Black and Indigenous people within the acronym aims to emphasize the specific and historic nature of the oppression experienced by those two groups, rather than subsuming them under a general category.

- Throughout the training, BIPOC will be the primary term used, though POC may also appear on occasion.

- For participants outside the United States who may not relate to these designations, the training will provide support in exploring whether and how these terms apply to their own identities and experiences.

### The Dual Truth Framework for Understanding Race

- Two simultaneous truths are presented as central to the training's philosophical foundation, the universal truth that all humans are one people, and the relative truth that the lived experience of racial marginalization is real and consequential.

- Race, though a social construct with no biological basis, produces real experiences of both joy and suffering, making it essential to address within mindfulness teacher training.

- Invisibility and not being seen are identified as among the most painful aspects of racial marginalization, underscoring the importance of awareness and recognition for teachers working with diverse students.

- Mindfulness is framed as a tool for investigating the delusions embedded in hierarchical, human-created racial frameworks, with the goal of helping practitioners reconnect with their true nature and ultimately live in greater harmony with one another.

- Complexity and nuance are emphasized throughout, acknowledging that experiences of race vary significantly depending on geography, majority or minority status, and individual context, with no single experience being universally applicable.

### Reflective Questions for Affinity Home Groups

- Participants are invited to reflect on and journal about when they first became aware of race, both their own and others'.

- Exploration of the relationship between skin color and caste or class in each participant's specific geographic and cultural context is encouraged.

- Participants are asked to consider how the relationship between skin color and caste or class has personally impacted their lives.

- Reflection on whether participants identify with the designations person of color or BIPOC, and the reasons behind that identification or non-identification, is included as a key discussion prompt.

### Next Steps

- Participants are expected to complete journaling and reflection exercises based on the four provided questions before meeting in their affinity home groups.

- Affinity home groups will convene to share personal stories and discuss the reflective questions in a smaller, more intimate setting.

- The series will continue with subsequent sessions covering white supremacy, intra-community solidarity, teacher resourcing, guest speakers, and culturally centered classroom building.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Origin of the Term "Race"**
- In the 16th century, "race" emerged as a classifying term for grouping human beings, without hierarchy or power attached to it
- In the 17th century, Europeans began using race as a system of ranking, placing themselves as superior to those they were colonizing and enslaving
**Bacon's Rebellion (1676) and the Racialization of Class**
- Bacon's Rebellion was a class war in Jamestown, Virginia, in which indentured servants — both white, black, and Indigenous — united against the planter class elite led by Governor Berkeley
- The rebellion succeeded temporarily; Jamestown was burned down, but collapsed after Bacon died
- In response, Berkeley separated white indentured servants from Black and Indigenous ones, granted white servants land and elevated status, and coined the designation "white" as a racial category
- This created a permanent upper class of white people and a permanent lower class, designed to justify slavery and genocide and protect the economic interests of the elite
- This is identified as a significant turning point in the history of race in America
**Race as a Social Construction**
- Race has no genetic or scientific basis
- All human beings share 99.9% of the same DNA
- All humans are more closely related to each other than chimpanzees are within their own groups
- In a scientific sense, all people today trace their origins to Africa
- Science defines people by DNA; society defines people by the color of their skin
**Origin of the Term "People of Color"**
- The term originated in 18th-century France to designate mixed-race people in colonized territories — those lighter than Black people and darker than Europeans
- The term "colored" remains in use in South Africa today to describe those who are neither white nor Black
- In the late 20th century, "persons of color" was introduced in the United States to replace the terms "minority" and "non-white"
- The term was defined by the shared experience of marginalization
- In 1977, at a national women's conference, women of other ethnicities joined the Black Women's Agenda, changing it to "Women of Color," marking a significant moment of political solidarity
**Evolution to BIPOC**
- BIPOC stands for Black, Indigenous, and People of Color
- The term is increasingly replacing POC because it specifically emphasizes the historic oppression of Black and Indigenous people
- The training primarily uses BIPOC, though POC may also be used

**Complexity of Racial Identity Globally**
- The POC and BIPOC designations are primarily U.S.-based and may not resonate with international participants

- Participants are encouraged to reflect on how color has been leveraged to reinforce caste systems in their own regions
- The training acknowledges that racial identity is nuanced and not cut and dry
**The Two Truths**
- Universal truth: all people on the planet are one people, inextricably linked and interconnected
- Relative truth: the lived experience of a person marginalized by the color of their skin is real and has consequences
- Both truths exist simultaneously and must be held together

### Stories
**Conda Mason – Her Own Story**
Conda Mason shares her personal story of growing up in a loving family in a Black and Mexican neighborhood in Southern California, describing herself as a happy, light-filled child with a deep sense of something bigger than herself. From a young age, she would experience a dissolution into particles as she fell asleep — a feeling of vastness and letting go. At age 12, her family moved to an all-white neighborhood where they were among the first Black families. It was there she first encountered racism and was called names she had never heard before. Over time, her inner light dimmed. She continued through school and into college at UC Berkeley, where she attended her first Kundalini Yoga class. During savasana at the end of the class, she reconnected to the spaciousness and bigness she had felt as a child. She realized through meditation that she had not known her light had gone out until it came back on. This experience made her a devoted practitioner of contemplative practices.
**Kate Johnson – Her Own Story**
Kate Johnson shares her story of first becoming aware of race at age three or four in preschool. While playing on the swings with another girl, the girl asked Kate whether she was Black or white, saying she needed to know before she could play with her again. That evening, Kate asked her mother the same question while in the bathtub. Her mother paused, then told her she was tan and beautiful and could play with whoever she wanted. Kate recognized that "tan" was not a recognized category and that she would have to figure out her identity on her own. This feeling of not belonging — not white enough for white kids, not Black enough for Black kids — followed her through her youth. Her mother is white and American; her father is Black and originally from Honduras. She also identified as Latina but could not speak Spanish. It was not until college, when she encountered a People of Color Student Union and was welcomed by the coordinator, that she first felt genuine belonging. She eventually came to identify as both Black and Latina, and now speaks Spanish. The term "people of color" was the entry point into that belonging and into the mindfulness teachings.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Conda Mason describes the psychological impact of moving into a white neighborhood at age 12 — encountering racism for the first time, being called names, and experiencing a gradual dimming of her inner sense of self and light
- She notes that this impact was normalized and worn unconsciously — she did not realize how much her sense of self had been affected until her reconnection through yoga and meditation
- Kate Johnson describes the ongoing psychological burden of not fitting into any racial category — feeling not white enough or Black enough — and the sense of not belonging that followed her through her teens
- The training acknowledges that marginalized people often come to meditation to heal from the harm and hurt of racial injustice
- Microaggressions and racism are noted as experiences that BIPOC meditation teachers may face not only in public but within mindfulness organizations, from other teachers and from students
- Feelings of nervousness, overwhelm, hope, despair, terror, and confusion are all acknowledged as valid responses to engaging with identity and internalized oppression in this training context
