### Review of Previous Microaggression Exercise

- Home groups were used as the setting for sharing and learning from each other's experiences with these unpleasant encounters.

### Introduction to the Panel Session on Teaching Mindfulness While BIPOC

- The session was framed as a continuation of prior work, this time featuring three prominent mindfulness teachers sharing real-life stories.

- Anushka Fernanda Pol was introduced as a Vipassana teacher in the Theravada Buddhism tradition.

- Maricela Gomez was introduced as a teacher within the Interbeing Society of Thich Nhat Hanh.

- Reverend Angel Kyoto Williams was introduced as a prominent teacher in the Zen Buddhism tradition.

- The stated purpose of the panel was to give students a lived sense of what these teachers have encountered in their mindfulness communities and how they responded most skillfully.

### Panelists' Identification with BIPOC and POC Terminology

- Anushka identified primarily as a person of color, with BIPOC being a newer but also applicable term, and further identified as South Asian and Sri Lankan American.

- Awareness of racial identity developed much earlier in life for Anushka, around age three, well before any connection to mindfulness or Buddhist practice at age nineteen or twenty.

- Maricela described growing up in Belize as a Creole person in a majority brown and black country, where racial hierarchy was nonetheless clear due to British colonial structures and the concentration of power among white people.

- A sense of racial inferiority did not develop until arriving in the United States, where the experience of not seeing oneself reflected in the dominant culture created a new and challenging psychological dynamic.

- Reverend Angel identified as politically Black, choosing that identity as an alignment and a deliberate stance rather than using the term African American, which was seen as a way of avoiding the Black-white dichotomy.

- Race was described by Reverend Angel as both a social construct and an embodied reality, meaning that identity is shaped not only by self-identification but also by how one is perceived and categorized by others.

- Navigating questions of being "Black enough" from within the Black community, as well as pressure to align with whiteness from outside it, were described as ongoing and complex dimensions of racial identity.

- The complexity of racial identity was noted to shift depending on context, geography, and the specific community one is situated within.

### Microaggressions and Racial Stereotyping Within Mindfulness Communities

- The same patterns of racial bias encountered in other professional contexts tend to persist within mindfulness and meditation settings, as all participants bring their conditioning into those spaces.

- Anushka described experiencing the common dynamic of audience members directing questions to white or male colleagues rather than to her, even after she had just delivered a presentation.

- Confidence in one's own understanding, developed through sustained practice, was offered as a way of remaining grounded when others fail to recognize one's authority or expertise.

- Positive projections were also noted as a form of stereotyping, with Anushka describing instances where being South Asian led others to assume heightened spiritual authority, a dynamic she sometimes used strategically to disrupt fixed assumptions.

- Maricela identified a pervasive pattern in retreat and day-long settings where teachers, including teachers of color, deliver content as though the entire audience is white and middle class.

- Failing to account for the diverse identities, economic backgrounds, and life experiences of practitioners was described as a significant regression that excludes many people from feeling genuinely welcomed into mindfulness spaces.

- A call was made for teachers to be aware not only of who is present in the room but also of who is absent, and to speak to those experiences as well.

- Reverend Angel described being repeatedly asked by people across different communities whether she knew a single Black woman practitioner, illustrating how isolated people of color were in Zen spaces at the time of her entry into practice.

- Writing a book about Blackness at thirty years old and within only eight years of practice was described as a deliberate and vocal act of pushing back against the hierarchy and the erasure of racial identity in Zen communities.

- An essay titled "No Color in the Room" was written to challenge the exclusion of practitioners of color from a teachers-only conference, arguing that representative practitioners should be included when teachers of color are absent.

- Being a sanctioned teacher in Zen was acknowledged as providing a degree of institutional protection that not all practitioners of color have access to, and the importance of understanding one's own context was emphasized.

### The Relationship Between Racial Identity and Dharma Practice

- The claim that identity has nothing to do with the Dharma was identified as a position most often held by people of a particular, typically dominant, identity, which itself reveals the limits of that claim.

- A distinction was drawn between absolute reality, in which there is no biological basis for race, and relative reality, in which race has ongoing and tangible impacts on people's lives and experiences.

- Whiteness was described not merely as a characteristic of white individuals but as a cultural construct that operates as a default in spaces that do not actively center other identities and experiences.

- Aspects of mindfulness teaching such as norms around time, physical comportment, tone of voice, and the concept of right speech were identified as potential mechanisms through which cultural dominance can be reinforced and practitioners of color repositioned within a hegemonic framework.

- Racial analysis was presented as an essential complement to inward identity work, rather than something separate from or in tension with contemplative practice.

- Many teachings received in predominantly white spaces were described as shaped by unconscious impulse rather than malice, with the tendency being for teachers to present their own understanding as universal.

- Compassion toward those who are centered in their own perspective was offered alongside a firm insistence on the responsibility of practitioners of color to remain rooted and not cede the ground to the centering of whiteness.

### The Role of Mindfulness Teachers in Addressing Societal Suffering and Racial Justice

- People come to mindfulness primarily because they are seeking relief from suffering, and that motivation provides the foundation for understanding why racial and social justice must be part of the practice.

- Racism, colonization, and related forms of structural oppression were described as forms of suffering that cannot be excluded from the scope of what mindfulness addresses without undermining the integrity of the practice itself.

- Ignoring the suffering caused by racism while claiming to address all other forms of suffering was compared to repeatedly removing an unwanted ingredient from a soup that keeps reappearing, suggesting that avoidance is neither skillful nor sustainable.

- Maricela argued that sufficient conditions now exist, with enough voices and enough momentum, for the integration of racial justice into mindfulness teaching to be considered not optional but necessary.

- The historical origins of lay mindfulness practice were connected by Anushka to the colonization of Buddhist countries such as Burma and Sri Lanka, illustrating that the very conditions that brought mindfulness to the West are inseparable from histories of race and power.

- Practitioners from countries outside the United States were acknowledged as needing to translate the American framing of racial dynamics into their own cultural and historical contexts, while still recognizing that analogous dynamics of ethnicity and oppression exist globally.

- Investigating identity, including racial identity, through mindfulness of mind and body was described as essential to understanding the self and the boundaries drawn between self and other, and to achieving genuine freedom from suffering.

### Strategies for Self-Resourcing as BIPOC Mindfulness Teachers

- Talking with colleagues after a difficult racial encounter was identified by Anushka as a primary means of avoiding isolation and processing what had occurred.

- Spending time in nature, allowing feelings to move through, and returning to a sense of one's own identity were also described as helpful resourcing practices.

- Maricela emphasized a preventative approach to resourcing, grounded in consistent daily mindfulness practice so that when difficult moments arise, the practitioner is already sufficiently prepared to meet them with equanimity.

- Community and Sangha were described as essential resources, with the experience of meditating with others during a storm serving as a vivid illustration of how collective practice provides grounding and stability.

- A tendency among people of color to default to individualism and self-sufficiency was identified as a learned response to internalized messages of weakness, and turning toward community was offered as a corrective practice.

- Small everyday rituals such as burning sage and lighting candles were described as meaningful acts of realignment with the elements and with one's own sense of self.

- Reverend Angel emphasized the foundational importance of physical wellness, including sleep, nutrition, and exercise, as prerequisites for being able to access one's mindfulness practice and respond skillfully under pressure.

- A distinction was drawn between hurt and harm, with the suggestion that not everything that causes hurt feelings constitutes harm, and that developing attunement to this difference helps practitioners avoid losing their grounding unnecessarily.

- Maintaining command of one's own faculties and continuing to practice even when others act in willfully hurtful ways was presented as both a personal discipline and a form of resilience.

**Race as Construct and Embodied Reality**
- The facilitator notes there is no biological basis for race, yet it has real-life implications
- Reverend Angel distinguishes between race as a social construct and race as an embodied construct — people internalize racialization in how they move, speak, and perceive the world
- Anushka notes that racial identity formation can precede spiritual practice by many years
**Microaggressions in Mindfulness Spaces**
- Anushka states that the same racial dynamics present in other professional contexts will continue in mindfulness settings because everyone brings their conditioning
- Anushka describes experiences of people directing questions about her presentation to white or male colleagues rather than to her
- Anushka also describes receiving positive projections as a South Asian woman — being assumed to be more spiritual — and using that dynamic skillfully
- Maricela identifies a pervasive pattern of teachers — including BIPOC teachers — teaching as if the entire audience is white and middle-class, ignoring the presence and experience of non-white, non-middle-class practitioners
- Reverend Angel describes being asked repeatedly, across many years and locations, whether she knew a single Black woman practitioner named Kodo — illustrating the assumption that all Black practitioners must know each other
- Reverend Angel wrote an essay called "No Color in the Room" pushing back against a teachers' conference that excluded practitioners of color by requiring teacher status for attendance
**Teaching to Diverse Audiences**
- Maricela calls for teachers to be aware not only of who is in the room but who is not, and to speak to those absent voices
- The facilitator notes that the MMTCP training includes an international cohort, with students from countries where BIPOC and POC terminology does not apply in the same way, creating confusion about identity and the purpose of the BIPOC track
- Reverend Angel encourages students to use their mindfulness practice to observe their own unconscious alignments — who they listen to more, who they take more seriously — as a way of understanding their own racialization
- Anushka notes that students from other countries are receiving an American framing of suffering and will need to translate it into their own contexts
**Mindfulness and Political Reality**
- Maricela argues that suffering is the core of Dharma teaching, and racism and colonization are forms of suffering; to exclude them from mindfulness teaching is to ignore a major dimension of reality
- Maricela states that teachers who say mindfulness is not about race are, in her view, acting unwisely given current conditions
- Anushka connects the historical spread of mindfulness to lay people directly to colonization of Buddhist countries such as Burma and Sri Lanka, noting the history is not neutral
- Reverend Angel states that the default in any space where culture and identity are not actively engaged will be whiteness — as a cultural construct that maintains itself implicitly through norms around time, body movement, speech volume, and tone
- Reverend Angel notes that teachings can be altered to re-situate practitioners within cultural dominance, and that racial analysis is therefore essential
**Resourcing Oneself**
- Anushka: talks with colleagues to avoid isolation; spends time in nature; allows feelings to move through; reflects on remembering or releasing a fixed sense of self
- Maricela: focuses on preventative practice — continuous daily mindfulness as muscle-building so that difficult events are easier to absorb; draws on Sangha and community; uses small rituals such as candles and sage to realign with the elements; notes that people of color often resist asking for community support due to internalized messages about strength
- Reverend Angel: maintains a deeply rooted daily meditation practice; attends to sleep, nutrition, and physical health to avoid drawing from reserves; distinguishes between hurt and harm to avoid over-personalizing events; cultivates heartfulness alongside mindfulness; uses practice to return to center when slipping

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Relative and Absolute Reality in Dharma**
- Reverend Angel articulates that there are no true absolutes except birth, old age, sickness, and death; everything else exists within a context
- She invokes the concept of upaya (skillful means) — the Buddha's teaching that responses must be appropriate to context, not fixed absolutes
- She notes that people who claim identity has nothing to do with the Dharma tend to be people of a particular identity, which itself reveals the relative nature of that claim
**Embodied Racialization**
- Reverend Angel explains that racialization is not only a social label but something people carry in their bodies — in habits of movement, speech, and perception
- This embodied dimension means racial dynamics are present even when not explicitly named
**Colonization and the Origins of Western Mindfulness**
- Anushka traces the arrival of mindfulness in Western lay contexts to the colonial period, when Buddhist countries under European rule began efforts to preserve and disseminate their traditions
- This history means the very existence of Western mindfulness training is shaped by racial and colonial dynamics
**Suffering as the Universal Entry Point**
- Maricela frames all engagement with mindfulness — secular or spiritual — as a response to suffering
- She argues that racism, colonization, and settlerism are forms of suffering, and that a Dharma practice that ignores them is incomplete
- She uses the metaphor of a mushroom that keeps rising in a soup — conditions have now sufficiently matured that these conversations can no longer be set aside
**Whiteness as Default**
- Reverend Angel explains that whiteness functions as a cultural default — not through malice but through blindness — and that practitioners of color must actively resist ceding ground to that centering
- She notes that norms such as punctuality, quiet bodies, and non-confrontational speech are culturally coded and can be used to suppress the expression of people of color under the guise of mindfulness
**Community as Spiritual Resource**
- Maricela describes being on a Zoom Sangha call during a hurricane in New Orleans and feeling anchored by community even as the storm raged around her
- Both Maricela and Anushka emphasize that community — colleagues, Sangha — is essential for practitioners of color navigating racial harm
**Confidence in One's Seat**
- Both Anushka and Reverend Angel describe the importance of being grounded in one's own understanding and identity so that external projections or aggressions do not displace the practitioner
- Reverend Angel notes that as a sanctioned Zen teacher, institutional recognition provides a degree of protection, and encourages students to understand their own context similarly

### Stories
**Anushka — Presentation Redirected to White Colleagues**
Anushka describes a recurring professional experience in which, after delivering a presentation, audience members directed their questions to her white or male colleagues rather than to her. She identifies this as a common dynamic in professional settings where authority is assumed to reside with white or male individuals, and notes it continues in mindfulness contexts.
**Anushka — The Older White Woman at a Teaching Retreat**
Anushka recounts being assigned to work with an older white woman at a larger teaching event. The woman was reluctant to work with Anushka, assuming the older white male teachers had more knowledge. Anushka responded by playfully asserting her Sri Lankan heritage and proximity to the origins of Buddhism, which helped shift the woman's assumptions and allowed them to work together.
**Reverend Angel Kyoto Williams — "Do You Know Kodo?"**
Reverend Angel describes that for approximately ten years, wherever she went in Zen practice communities, people would look at her and ask whether she knew a single Black woman practitioner named Kodo who lived in New Jersey. She uses this story to illustrate the assumption that all Black practitioners must know one another.
**Reverend Angel Kyoto Williams — Writing "Being Black" at 30**
Reverend Angel describes writing her first book about Blackness at age 30, at a time when she had approximately eight years of Zen practice — considered a novice in Zen's long transmission timeline. She describes this as a deliberate act of pushing back against the hierarchy and the erasure of race in practice communities.
**Reverend Angel Kyoto Williams — "No Color in the Room" Essay**
Reverend Angel wrote an essay by this title in response to a teachers' conference that excluded practitioners of color by requiring teacher status for attendance. She argued that if there were no teachers of color, the conference needed to allow representative practitioners of color to attend in order to have any voices of color present.
**Reverend Angel Kyoto Williams — Moving from Queens to Brooklyn**
Reverend Angel describes growing up in a multicultural environment in Rego Park and Lefrak City in Queens, then moving to Brooklyn where racial and ethnic divides were more sharply drawn — particularly among Caribbean communities, along lines of proximity to American-ness, country of origin, and skin color.
**Maricela Gomez — Hurricane in New Orleans**
Maricela describes being in New Orleans for her mother's birthday when a hurricane arrived unexpectedly with 50–60 mile per hour winds. She was simultaneously on a Zoom call with her Sangha meditating. She describes feeling held and anchored by the community even as the storm raged around her, using this as an illustration of the power of Sangha as a resource.
**Maricela Gomez — Growing Up in Belize**
Maricela describes growing up in Belize, a Creole country where the majority of people were brown and Black, yet power was held by white people and the British Crown. She notes that Belize only became independent in the late 1970s and that the Queen remained on the currency. She describes developing racial consciousness not through inferiority — because her community was the majority — but through clear awareness of where power resided. Her sense of inferiority developed only after immigrating to the United States.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Maricela describes developing an inferiority complex after immigrating to the United States from Belize, attributing it to the shift from being part of a racial majority to a minority, and to not seeing oneself reflected in the dominant culture; she notes she is still healing from this
- Reverend Angel describes the experience of not being considered "Black enough" by Black communities due to mixed heritage, fair skin, and manner of speaking, requiring her to consciously learn social cues of Blackness later in life
- Reverend Angel warns against operating from a depleted reserve — noting that poor sleep, poor nutrition, and accumulated stress impair the ability to access mindfulness skills and respond skillfully
- Reverend Angel distinguishes between hurt and harm, cautioning practitioners not to conflate the two, as over-personalizing events can destabilize practice and wellbeing
- Maricela notes that people of color often resist drawing on community support due to internalized messages about needing to be strong and self-sufficient, describing this as pulling oneself up by a non-existent bootstrap
- Anushka notes the importance of not being moved from one's seat by others' failure to recognize or respect one's knowledge and experience, framing this as a matter of inner confidence rather than external validation
- Reverend Angel notes that norms within mindfulness communities — such as expectations around quiet bodies, non-confrontational speech, and punctuality — can be used to suppress the expression of practitioners of color, presenting a specific challenge to their full participation and authentic presence
