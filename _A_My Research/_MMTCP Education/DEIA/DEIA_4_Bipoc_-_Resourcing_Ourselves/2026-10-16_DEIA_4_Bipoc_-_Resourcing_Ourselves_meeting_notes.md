### Outline
### Overview of Session Goals and Context

- Building on prior sessions that explored social locations and identity, this session focuses on how practitioners can respond to other people's projections and harmful experiences.

- Mindfulness spaces such as mentor groups, retreat centers, and teaching environments are specifically identified as places where painful and confusing experiences can occur.

- Practical guidelines for recognizing, healing from, and skillfully responding to harmful interpersonal experiences are presented as the core learning objectives of the session.

### Defining Racism, Implicit Bias, and Microaggressions

- Racism is defined using the Oxford dictionary as prejudice, discrimination, or antagonism directed at someone based on the belief that one's own race is superior.

- Overt racist acts are described as blatant and easy to identify, with examples including calling an entire racial group derogatory names or telling people born in a country to "go back home."

- Implicit bias, also referred to as unconscious bias, is defined as attitudes or stereotypes that affect understanding, actions, and decisions outside of conscious awareness and control.

- A key distinction is drawn between racism, which is overt and intentional, and implicit bias, which operates unconsciously and beyond an individual's direct control.

- Every person is noted to hold unconscious beliefs about various social and identity groups, as these biases have been absorbed through systemic racism and become embedded in the psyche.

- An orchestra audition example is used to illustrate how implicit bias can operate even when deliberate steps are taken to eliminate it, as conductors unconsciously selected against women based solely on the sound of their footsteps.

- Microaggressions are defined as commonplace verbal, behavioral, or environmental actions that communicate hostility toward oppressed or targeted groups, and they are described as indirect, subtle, and often unintentional.

- These acts are identified as an outgrowth of implicit bias, meaning they emerge from unconscious conditioning rather than deliberate intent to harm.

### History and Origins of the Term Microaggressions

- The term microaggression was coined in the 1970s by Dr. Chester Pierce, a Black psychologist whose concept emerged from both his lived experience and academic expertise.

- Dr. Pierce is noted as a pioneering figure who was the first Black man elected to his high school, the first to play on an integrated football team south of the Mason-Dixon line, and the first Black full professor at Harvard's Mass General Hospital.

- His work with NASA, as a consultant on Sesame Street, and the naming of Pierce Peak in Antarctica are cited as further evidence of his extraordinary achievements alongside his experience of microaggressions.

- Dr. Derald Wing Sue, a Chinese American psychologist and professor at Columbia University, is identified as a leading expert who expanded the academic study of microaggressions, implicit bias, and racism.

- Two books by Dr. Sue are recommended as key resources, titled "Microaggressions in Everyday Life" and "Overcoming Our Racism."

### Examples of Microaggressions in Everyday Life

- Telling a person of color that they are "so articulate" is identified as a microaggression because it implies an underlying belief that articulateness is unexpected from that racial group.

- Mistaking one person of color for another solely based on shared race, despite no physical resemblance, is presented as another common form of microaggression.

- Repeatedly asking an Asian American person where they are "really from" after they have stated they are from an American city communicates the implicit message that they do not belong in that country.

- Asking a Black woman whether her hair is real or requesting to touch it is described as a deeply invasive and dehumanizing microaggression.

- Crossing to the other side of the street upon seeing Black or Brown men, or clutching a handbag in an elevator, are cited as behavioral microaggressions rooted in racial stereotyping.

- Disbelieving that a Black man created a sophisticated technological innovation, and questioning where he obtained it, is presented as an example of how microaggressions deny the intellectual capabilities of people of color.

### Impact of Racism and Microaggressions on Mental and Physical Health

- Research has shown that experiencing racial microaggressions is significantly linked to depressive symptoms and a negative worldview, particularly among young people of color.

- Racism is described as producing traumatic stress responses including anger, physical reactions, avoidance, intrusion, hypervigilance, and low self-esteem.

- Studies have directly linked racism to heart disease in African Americans, illustrating how chronic racial stress translates into measurable physical health consequences.

- Collective racial discrimination is identified as creating a deep mistrust of institutional systems, including the medical system, which leads to reduced engagement with preventative care.

- The disproportionate contraction and death rates from COVID-19 among Black and Brown communities are attributed to the compounding effects of racism, microaggressions, lack of preventative care, and limited access to services.

- These impacts are framed as directly relevant to mindfulness teachers, who must be aware of what their students may be carrying into the classroom and how it affects their wellbeing.

### Decision-Making Framework for Responding to Microaggressions

- Responding to a microaggression presents a catch-22 in which speaking up risks being labeled as oversensitive or as "pulling the race card," while staying silent normalizes the harmful behavior.

- Silence is noted to send an implicit message to the perpetrator that the behavior is acceptable, thereby reinforcing rather than interrupting the harm.

- Three core questions are offered as a framework for deciding how to respond, asking whether the incident actually occurred, whether a response is warranted, and how that response should be delivered.

- The guiding principle for mindfulness practitioners is identified as causing the least amount of harm possible while remaining truthful and actively interrupting harm to oneself or others.

### Dr. Sue's Five Strategies for Responding to Microaggressions

- Making the invisible visible is presented as the first strategy, which involves acknowledging that a microaggression occurred and naming its impact using the multicultural agreement of intent versus impact.

- Saying "ouch" or stating "I know you were trying to be funny, however the impact on me was..." are offered as accessible ways to call in the behavior without escalating the situation.

- Disarming the microaggression is the second strategy, achieved by highlighting shared values and demonstrating the benefit of awareness, such as saying "I realize you didn't mean to say something racially charged, which is something neither of us wants."

- Educating the offender is the third strategy, which involves explaining what happened, providing historical context where relevant, and using wise speech and I-statements throughout.

- Practitioners are encouraged to tap into the understanding that everyone, including the perpetrator, suffers from the effects of racism, as this perspective can help diffuse emotional reactivity.

- Awareness of power dynamics is identified as essential, particularly for teachers who hold positional authority and must be careful not to cause harm to students through their responses.

- Resourcing oneself is presented as the final strategy, which may include ending a discussion temporarily, arranging a one-to-one conversation later, or checking in with the affected person by asking what they need in that moment.

### The RAIN Practice as an Internal Resourcing Tool

- RAIN is introduced as an internal process for navigating the aftermath of a microaggression, with each letter standing for Recognize, Accept, Investigate, and Nurture.

- Recognizing involves acknowledging that something real and harmful has occurred rather than dismissing or minimizing the experience.

- Accepting does not mean condoning what happened but rather acknowledging the truth of the moment without denial.

- Investigating means locating the experience in the body, noticing where tension or discomfort is physically held, rather than mentally amplifying the distress.

- Nurturing involves giving oneself what is needed in that moment, drawing on personal practices that provide comfort and restoration.

- Using a physical touchstone such as a quartz crystal is offered as a practical grounding tool that can be kept in a pocket and used to return to a sense of calm during difficult moments.

- Seeking support from someone with institutional power or from a trusted friend for emotional offloading is also recommended as part of the resourcing process.

### Larry Yang's Compassion Hierarchy for Difficult Moments

- A saying attributed to teacher Larry Yang is shared as a guiding framework for moments when practitioners cannot respond perfectly to harm.

- The hierarchy moves from aspiring to be loving and compassionate, to being kind, to being nonjudgmental, to not causing harm, and finally to causing the least amount of harm possible.

- This framework is offered as a compassionate acknowledgment that practitioners will not always respond ideally, and that striving toward the least harmful response is itself a meaningful practice.

### The Pause Relax Open Practice for In-the-Moment Response

- Pause Relax Open is introduced as a complementary practice to RAIN, designed to be used in real time during a conversation rather than as a post-encounter internal process.

- This practice originates from the Insight Dialogues body of work, which focuses on mindful speaking and listening, and it underpins the wise speech approach encouraged throughout the session.

- Pausing involves temporarily stopping speech, thought, and action in order to shift attention into the immediate bodily experience, thereby interrupting habitual reactive patterns.

- Relaxing involves consciously releasing physical tension held in common areas such as the jaw, eyes, shoulders, and belly, as well as softening mental rigidity or agitation.

- Opening involves expanding awareness beyond one's own internal experience to take in the wider environment and the people present, with a soft and diffuse gaze rather than a hard or forced one.

- This practice is recommended for use whenever something is heard that conflicts with one's values, so that responses can be chosen deliberately rather than driven by reactivity.

### Next Steps

- Participants are to reflect on a personal experience of a microaggression, write down what happened and how they responded, and consider how the techniques from the session might inform a different response in the future.

- A second journaling exercise asks participants to reflect on how they currently resource themselves both externally and internally when confronted with racial microaggressions or implicit bias.

- Both reflections are to be shared within home groups as a collective skill-building exercise around responding to microaggressions and practicing self-care in their aftermath.

- The Pause Relax Open practice is to be carried into daily life and home group discussions as an in-the-moment tool for mindful response.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Racism**
- Defined using the Oxford Dictionary as prejudice, discrimination, or antagonism directed at someone of a different race based on the belief that one's own race is superior
- Described as an overt act — easy to see and identify
- Examples given: calling an entire race rapists; telling people born in a country but not of the dominant culture to "go back home"
- Rooted in the belief that one race is superior to another
- Categorized as an act of aggression and violence
**Implicit Bias / Unconscious Bias**
- Defined as attitudes or stereotypes that affect understanding, actions, and decisions in an unconscious manner — outside of conscious awareness and control
- Distinguished from racism in that it is not intentional or overt
- All people hold unconscious beliefs about various social and identity groups
- Described as having seeped into the psyche through racist structures and systems
- Example given: an orchestra audition where musicians were placed behind a curtain to prevent visual bias, yet the sound of women's shoes still triggered unconscious gender bias, resulting in men being selected disproportionately
**Microaggressions**
- Defined as commonplace verbal, behavioral, or environmental actions that communicate hostility toward oppressed or targeted groups
- Described as indirect, subtle, intentional or unintentional
- Identified as an outgrowth of implicit bias
- Coined by Dr. Chester Pierce in the 1970s — a Black psychologist, Harvard professor, NASA consultant, Sesame Street consultant, and first Black man to play on an integrated football team south of the Mason-Dixon line
- Further developed by Dr. Derald Wing Sue, a Chinese American psychologist at Columbia University, author of Microaggressions in Everyday Life and Overcoming Our Racism
- Examples provided:
  - Telling a person of color "you're so articulate" — implying their people are not
  - Mistaking one person of color for another solely based on race
  - Repeatedly asking an Asian American "where are you really from" after they say Portland, Oregon
  - Asking a Black woman "is that your real hair?" or requesting to touch it
  - Crossing the street to avoid Black or Brown men
  - Clutching a handbag in an elevator near a person of color
  - Disbelieving a Black coder created his own innovation, repeatedly asking where he got it from
**Health Impacts of Racism and Microaggressions**
- Studies show significant negative impact on mental and physical health
- Greater exposure to racial microaggressions correlates with increased depressive symptoms and a negative worldview
- Racism linked to traumatic stress responses: anger, physical reactions, avoidance, intrusion, hypervigilance, and low self-esteem
- Research has linked racism to heart disease in African Americans
- Collective racial discrimination creates a lack of trust in systems and institutions
- Distrust of the medical system leads to reduced preventative care engagement
- These combined factors — stress, lack of access, distrust — identified as contributing to disproportionate COVID-19 contraction and death rates among Black and Brown people
**The Catch-22 of Responding**
- If you respond to a microaggression, you risk being labeled oversensitive, accused of playing the race card, or seen as not embodying mindfulness
- If you do not respond, you normalize the behavior, the harm is internalized, and the perpetrator learns it is acceptable
- Three questions to ask: Did it actually occur? Should I respond? How should I respond?
- Goal as mindfulness practitioners: cause the least amount of harm possible, not escalate harm, while remaining truthful and stopping harm from continuing
**Dr. Derald Wing Sue's Five-Point Framework**
- Make the invisible visible: acknowledge what happened, call it in rather than call it out, name the impact using intent vs. impact framing
- Disarm the microaggression: highlight shared values, emphasize that neither party wants racially charged interactions, explain the benefit of awareness for future behavior
- Educate the offender: explain what happened, provide historical context if relevant, use wise speech and I-statements
- Tap into shared humanity: recognize that everyone, including the perpetrator, suffers from racism; use this understanding to diffuse the moment
- Resource yourself: end the discussion if necessary, arrange a one-on-one follow-up, ask "what do I need in this moment?" or check in with the affected person
**Multicultural Agreements Referenced**
- Intent vs. impact: the intention behind a microaggression may be unconscious, but the impact is real and must be addressed
- Use of I-statements: speak from your own perspective
- Wise speech: awareness of the words used and their effects
**RAIN Practice Applied to Racial Harm**
- Recognize: acknowledge that something happened and that it is real
- Accept: accept the truth of the moment without condoning what occurred
- Investigate: locate the experience in the body — where is the tension, tightness, or reaction landing physically
- Nurture: give yourself what you need; remind yourself that what was said does not define the whole of who you are
**Relax, Pause, Open Practice**
- Drawn from Insight Dialogues, a practice for mindful speaking and listening
- Pause: stop what you are doing, saying, or thinking; shift into immediate bodily experience; interrupt habitual reactive patterning
- Relax: release physical tension in the jaw, eyes, shoulders, and belly; soften the mind from reactivity, agitation, or fixed thinking
- Open: expand awareness beyond the self; take in the wider environment and the people present; soften the gaze to a diffuse, receptive quality rather than a hard stare
**Resourcing Oneself**
- Use a touchstone object — Konda uses a quartz crystal — to return to serenity during difficult moments
- Speak to someone with institutional power if harm occurs in a formal setting; go to a mentor before escalating
- Offload with a trusted friend to process and release
- Breathe: the breath calms the nervous system and the mind
- Nurture yourself with the knowledge that a microaggression does not define your whole identity
- In a classroom setting, address incidents in the group when possible rather than taking them aside, to avoid sending the message to others that the behavior is acceptable
**Home Group Assignments**
- Journaling prompt one: Reflect on a time a microaggression happened to you; write down what happened and how you responded; review today's techniques and consider what you might do differently next time
- Journaling prompt two: Reflect on how you resource yourself when confronted with a racial microaggression or implicit bias — both externally and internally
- Share both responses with your home group to build collective skills
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Mindfulness and Racial Harm**
- Racial microaggressions and implicit bias occur even in mindfulness spaces — mentor groups, retreat centers, and teacher-student relationships
- Bringing unconscious harm into conscious awareness is framed as a spiritual and ethical responsibility for mindfulness practitioners and teachers
- The goal of causing the least harm possible is presented as a core principle of mindfulness practice applied to interpersonal conflict
**Shared Humanity as a Spiritual Resource**
- Konda invites practitioners to recognize that everyone — including the perpetrator of a microaggression — suffers from racism
- Tapping into this shared suffering is offered as a way to diffuse emotional reactivity and respond from a place of compassion rather than anger
**Wise Speech as Practice**
- Wise speech is presented not merely as a communication technique but as a mindfulness practice — being aware of the words used and their impact in real time
- The Relax, Pause, Open practice is described as foundational to wise speech
**Societal Structures and Individual Experience**
- The session situates interpersonal and internalized oppression within the broader framework of structural and institutional racism introduced in prior sessions
- The normalization of microaggressions is identified as a societal harm — people who experience them frequently may stop noticing them, which compounds the psychological damage
- The disproportionate impact of COVID-19 on Black and Brown communities is presented as a direct downstream consequence of systemic racism, medical distrust, and chronic stress
**Teaching as a Site of Power and Responsibility**
- Mindfulness teachers are reminded that they occupy a position of power relative to students
- This power differential makes it especially easy to cause harm, and equally important to model skillful response to microaggressions
- Addressing incidents in the group rather than privately is framed as a teaching responsibility — silence or removal of the incident sends a message of permissiveness to the whole group
**Graduated Compassion — Larry Yang**
- Presented as a spiritual framework for navigating moments when full compassion is not accessible
- Offers a descending ladder of intention: from loving and compassionate, to kind, to nonjudgmental, to not causing harm, to causing the least harm possible

### Stories
**The Orchestra Audition — Konda**
- To avoid gender bias, an orchestra had auditioners perform behind a curtain so evaluators could only hear the music
- Despite not being able to see the performers, evaluators could hear the sound of women's shoes and unconsciously used that auditory cue to identify and disadvantage female musicians
- Men were disproportionately selected, demonstrating how implicit bias operates even when deliberate steps are taken to prevent it
**The Black Coder — Konda**
- Konda describes a friend who is a highly skilled coder and created a unique, previously unseen innovation
- When he presented his work to white organizations, people repeatedly questioned whether he had actually done it himself, asking "where did you get this from?"
- Presented as an example of a microaggression rooted in unconscious bias about the capabilities of Black people
**Whoopi Goldberg Comment — Konda**
- Konda shares that she was frequently told she looked just like Whoopi Goldberg
- The only shared characteristic was that both had dreadlocks at the time
- Offered as a personal example of the microaggression of mistaking people of the same race for one another
**The Word "Lame" — Kate**
- Kate recounts repeatedly using the word "lame" in conversation with a friend
- The friend gently interrupted and asked what she meant by the word, explaining that his partner is disabled and the word had caused her pain
- The friend helped Kate find alternative words; Kate describes the moment as humbling but also as an act of deep friendship
- Used to illustrate how calling someone in — rather than out — can strengthen rather than damage a relationship, and how acknowledging being the perpetrator and apologizing is an important part of the process
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Mental Health Impacts**
- Studies cited show that increased exposure to racial microaggressions correlates with higher rates of depressive symptoms
- Microaggressions contribute to a negative worldview, particularly in young people who internalize these experiences early
- Racism is described as producing traumatic stress responses including anger, avoidance, intrusion, hypervigilance, and low self-esteem
**Physical Health Impacts**

- Chronic stress from collective racial discrimination is identified as a physiological burden
- Lack of trust in the medical system, stemming from racism, leads to reduced preventative care, compounding physical health risks
**Impact on Mindfulness Practice and Teaching**
- Microaggressions occur in mindfulness spaces — retreat centers, mentor groups, and classrooms — creating confusion and pain in contexts meant to be safe
- The emotional and cognitive burden of deciding whether and how to respond to a microaggression is itself described as stressful
- Mindfulness teachers face a particular tension between the code of kindness associated with their role and the need to interrupt harm
- The normalization of microaggressions can cause practitioners to lose conscious awareness of ongoing harm, undermining the foundation of mindful presence
- Practitioners are encouraged to use breath, RAIN, Relax-Pause-Open, and touchstone objects as tools to regulate the nervous system and maintain capacity for skillful response during and after difficult encounters
