### Introduction to Race-Based Oppression and White Supremacy

- Shared among all BIPOC communities is the experience of race-based oppression, which forms the foundation of the session's focus.

- Acknowledged by Conda Mason is that oppression takes many forms, including gender-based and ability-based oppression, though the training centers specifically on race-based oppression.

- Described as an objective, systemic reality rather than a matter of opinion, white supremacy is presented as a concept built into the structures of society.

- Framed as a six-part series, the training dedicates significant attention to race-based oppression due to its pervasive and ongoing societal impact.

### Compassion as a Foundation for Engaging with Oppression

- Reflecting on systems of oppression can be painful for BIPOC individuals, particularly when considering harm done to themselves and those they care about.

- Encouraged as the appropriate response to this suffering is compassion, described as the capacity to acknowledge suffering with an open heart.

- Recognized as a source of additional suffering is the reality that some BIPOC individuals may have had to deny, minimize, or even collude with oppressive systems in order to succeed.

- Noted by Kate Johnson is that participants in the training already occupy a position of relative privilege by virtue of having English language proficiency and internet access.

- Emphasized as central to the work of mindfulness teachers is the understanding that some of the suffering students bring to practice has its roots in systemic oppression.

- Affirmed as a key confidence point is that mindfulness practices have the capacity to ease the harmful impacts of oppressive systems and restore awareness of when one is being affected by them.

### Definition of Oppression and the Four Levels Framework

- Defined in this context, oppression refers to the mistreatment and denial of access to power and resources based on a person's identity.

- Drawn from the organization Race Forward, a U.S.-based group of thought leaders, is the framework proposing four mutually reinforcing levels of oppression that together comprise the system of racism.

- Applicable both to society at large and to mindfulness spaces specifically, the four levels are structural, institutional, interpersonal, and internalized oppression.

### Structural Racism and Its Presence in Mindfulness Spaces

- Defined as racial bias operating across institutions and society as a whole, structural racism involves the cumulative and compounding effects of history, culture, ideology, and interactions among institutions and policies.

- Systematically privileging white people and disadvantaging people of color, structural racism functions as an overarching system rather than an isolated occurrence.

- Cited as an illustrative example is the work of Dr. Angela Rose Black, a health disparities scholar at the University of Wisconsin, who questioned the universality of mindfulness-based stress reduction research.

- Observed by Dr. Black upon attending mindfulness-based stress reduction classes was that participants were almost entirely white, prompting her to investigate why people of color were underrepresented.

- Discovered upon deeper examination was that the research studies proving the benefits of mindfulness-based stress reduction had been conducted on cohorts that were almost entirely white.

- Found through her own research was that Black women had repeatedly been unintentionally harmed by white students, white instructors, and curricula grounded in whiteness within mindfulness spaces.

- Representing structural racism, this gap in research meant that people of color were not receiving culturally competent curricula or the relational quality needed to benefit equitably from mindfulness instruction.

### Institutional Racism and the Exclusion of Asian Voices in Mindfulness

- Defined as one level less broad than structural racism, institutional racism occurs within particular institutions or systems of power and produces racially inequitable outcomes through unfair policies and discriminatory practices.

- Cited as an example is an essay by Dr. Funi Su of San Jose State University, published in Lion's Roar magazine, titled "We've Been Here All Along."

- Explored in that essay is the systematic exclusion of Asian and Asian American voices from mindfulness and Buddhist publications in the West.

- Traced by Dr. Su is this absence back to anti-Asian sentiment in the United States around the time of the Second World War.

- Documented through letters, interviews, and historical records is the fact that the vast majority of prominent mindfulness teachers in the West remain white, despite these practices originating in Asian cultural traditions.

- Identified as a form of institutionalized racism is the pattern of publishing houses and media companies rejecting or overlooking submissions from Asian American teachers and leaders.

### Interpersonal Racism as Experienced in Mindfulness Settings

- Described as the most commonly recognized form of racism, interpersonal racism involves bias expressed between individuals through words or behavior in direct encounters.

- Recounted by Kate Johnson is a personal experience during a two-month silent meditation retreat, where the culture of the space emphasized silence, no eye contact, and no physical interaction.

- Approached while sitting quietly in the retreat library listening to a Dharma talk through headphones, Kate was tapped on the shoulder and told to leave and to go listen to her music elsewhere.

- Understood in that moment was that the person who approached her had passed by another retreatant doing the same activity and singled out Kate based solely on her appearance and race.

- Representing a clear instance of interpersonal racism, this encounter assumed Kate did not belong in the space despite her engaging in the same practice as others present.

### Internalized Oppression and Its Psychological Impact

- Defined as the fourth level of race-based oppression, internalized oppression refers to how individuals come to feel about themselves as a result of experiencing racism.

- Described as the aftermath of the interpersonal encounter at the retreat, Kate experienced a compulsion to perform twice as well as other retreatants in order to prove her right to be present.

- Manifesting internally was a painful combination of perfectionism and low self-worth, along with persistent fear of being approached again and told she did not belong.

- Illustrated through this experience is how a single interpersonal racist encounter can generate lasting internalized suffering that affects one's sense of self and belonging.

### White Supremacy as a Systemic Concept

- Comprising all four levels of race-based oppression together, white supremacy is presented not as an individual attitude but as a political, economic, and cultural system.

- Drawn from British educational researcher David Gilborn, the definition of white supremacy describes it as a system in which white people overwhelmingly control power and material resources.

- Included in this definition is the widespread presence of conscious and unconscious ideas of white superiority and entitlement, enacted daily across a broad array of institutions and social settings.

- Clarified explicitly is that acknowledging white supremacy does not mean hating individual white people or viewing all white people as bad.

- Affirmed is the possibility of loving individual white people while simultaneously challenging white supremacy wherever it manifests, with many white allies actively participating in that effort.

- Noted is that while not everyone is equally oppressed by white supremacy, everyone in society suffers as a result of its existence, including white individuals.

### Addressing Internalized Oppression and Redirecting Attention

- Acknowledged as a common experience is the shame that can arise when recognizing internalized oppression or the ways in which one may subtly reinforce oppressive dynamics.

- Emphasized is that thoughts and beliefs rooted in white supremacy, when they arise in individuals, are not a reflection of who those individuals fundamentally are and can be released through practice.

- Introduced as a guiding framework is the third principle of nonviolence outlined by Reverend Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., which calls for attacking forces of evil rather than persons doing evil.

- Identified as a risk is the tendency to direct righteous indignation toward individuals while allowing systemic forces such as healthcare, housing, and the law to escape scrutiny.

- Offered as an alternative approach is the aggression conciliation model from teacher Kazu Haga, whose book Healing Resistance appears on the optional reading list.

- Proposed in this model is channeling any aggression felt toward individuals toward the systems that have empowered those individuals and allowed injustice to persist.

- Distinguished in this approach is the difference between holding people accountable for harm and misidentifying individuals as the root source of systemic injustice.

- Directed toward all people in this framework is compassionate understanding expressed as friendship and love, while maintaining clarity about the systemic origins of harmful behavior.

### Applying the Systems Lens to Real-World Examples

- Offered by Conda Mason as an illustration is the example of loggers engaged in clear-cutting forests, where it is easy to direct anger at the individual workers rather than at the extractive capitalist system driving their actions.

- Noted is that those workers are typically fulfilling employment obligations to support their families, and that the deeper source of harm lies in an economic system that prioritizes extraction over regeneration.

- Described as a second example is the public response during the COVID-19 pandemic to mask mandates, particularly in the Midwest, where some individuals protested and targeted political figures such as a state governor as the perceived enemy.

- Reflected upon by Conda is her own initial reaction of vilifying those protesters, followed by a deeper recognition that many of them were driven by economic desperation and the urgent need to return to work.

- Identified as the underlying systemic issue is the lack of financial safety nets, healthcare access, and basic economic security for those individuals, rooted in broader structural inequities.

- Reinforced through both examples is the importance of looking past the visible face of harm to identify the systemic forces responsible, rather than directing energy solely at individuals.

### Next Steps

- Both the institutional analysis and the identity list are to be shared within home groups, with participants encouraged to compare and discuss similarities and differences in their responses.

- Participants are instructed to bring their identity lists to the next session, as Kate Johnson and Conda Mason plan to reference them directly in the following video.

- Martin Luther King Jr.'s third principle of nonviolence: attack forces of evil, not persons doing evil

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
- Compassion as a foundational response to suffering
  - Oppression is understood as a form of suffering at the societal level
  - The appropriate response of an open heart is compassion: acknowledging suffering, caring about it, and holding it with openness
  - BIPOC individuals may experience pain when reflecting on how systems of oppression have harmed them and those they love
- Complicity and privilege within oppressed communities
  - Some BIPOC individuals may have had to deny the existence of oppression, pretend it did not exist, or collude with oppressive forces in order to succeed
  - Feelings of guilt and shame about benefiting from certain systems that oppress others are themselves a form of suffering deserving compassion
- Mindfulness as a tool for awareness and healing
  - Mindfulness practices have the capacity to ease some of the harmful impacts of systems of oppression
  - They can restore the capacity to recognize when one is being impacted by or participating in these systems
  - Mindfulness enables practitioners to choose how they want to respond
- Thoughts rooted in white supremacy are not the self
  - Even when thoughts and beliefs rooted in white supremacy arise, they are not the person
  - Like any other form of suffering, they can be released
- Systemic thinking as a spiritual and civic discipline
  - Conda Mason reflected on the tendency to vilify individuals rather than examine the systems behind harmful actions
  - The example of loggers and capitalism illustrates how individuals are often acting within extractive systems rather than acting as independent agents of harm
  - The COVID mask mandate example illustrates how people protesting were often expressing economic desperation rooted in systemic disenfranchisement rather than simple selfishness
