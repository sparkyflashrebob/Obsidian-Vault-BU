### Outline
### Transitioning from Forgiveness and Conflict Work to Equanimity and Peace

- Building on prior sessions focused on forgiveness, multiculturalism, and conflict, the session moves into exploring equanimity and peace as the next area of practice.

- Personal and collective dimensions of conflict are described as inseparable, with Jack referencing Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s concept of the "mutual garment of destiny" to illustrate how individuals are woven into the broader fabric of the world's conflicts.

- Training teachers to help students hold both personal and collective experience simultaneously is identified as a core pedagogical responsibility.

### The Deliberate Alternation Between Heart Practices and Mindfulness Practices in Teacher Training

- Heart practices such as compassion, loving kindness, forgiveness, gratitude, and equanimity are deliberately alternated with mindfulness-based practices throughout the curriculum rather than following a fixed sequential model.

- This approach is designed to prepare teachers for real-world contexts such as schools, businesses, and clinics, where they must respond dynamically to the needs of their students rather than follow a rigid structure.

- Teachers are encouraged to draw from both wings of the training when designing ongoing programs or responding to the circumstances of the people they serve.

### Defining Peace and Equanimity Through Teachings and Imagery

- Peace is described not as an absence of change or difficulty, but as a quality of heart that can be maintained even amid the full range of human experience.

- Ajahn Chah's teaching of "stopping the war" is introduced, describing how human beings are in constant conflict with heat, cold, taste, sound, and one another, and inviting practitioners to step off the battlefield and find a place of peace larger than all those conflicts.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's story of the Vietnamese refugee boats is used to illustrate how even one calm and centered person can provide enough stability to help an entire group survive a crisis.

- A short poem about a yellow butterfly perched on the muzzle of a cannon is offered as a poetic image of how attention can be redirected away from conflict toward a different way of being.

- Suzuki Roshi's teaching is cited, stating that when one finds composure in the fact that everything changes, that composure itself is nirvana.

### Three Levels of Mindfulness as a Path to Peacefulness

- The first level of mindfulness involves awareness of the content of experience, where practitioners notice and name what is present, such as feelings, thoughts, sensations, and sounds, thereby expanding what neuroscience calls the window of tolerance.

- Loving awareness at this first level creates a space that is larger than any individual experience, allowing practitioners to be present with a greater range of inner and outer events without being overwhelmed.

- The second level involves awareness of the process of experience, recognizing that all experiences, whether joy or sorrow, praise or blame, gain or loss, are impermanent, ungraspable, and ultimately unsatisfying as resting places.

- Impermanence at this level is described using the Dhammapada and Diamond Sutra's imagery of a star at dawn, a flash of lightning, an echo, a rainbow, and a dream, pointing to the insubstantial nature of all phenomena.

- Resting in this meta-level awareness of impermanence is described as deepening wisdom and peacefulness, as practitioners begin to see that experiences are not fundamentally "me" or "mine."

- The third level involves turning mindfulness back toward consciousness itself, asking who or what is knowing the experience, and resting in the awareness that is ever-present, timeless, and unborn.

- Ajahn Chah's encounter with the master Ajahn Mun is recounted, in which Ajahn Mun told him that all his meditation experiences, however glorious, were merely like movies on a screen, and that the only liberating question is "to whom do they happen."

- Shifting identity to consciousness itself rather than to the contents of experience is described as the most liberating and peaceful orientation available to a practitioner.

- Following this shift, the practitioner is invited to return to the world, as described in the Zen ox-herding pictures, and to tend to the content of life, such as a cup of tea, a child, or the earth, with a peaceful heart and an appreciation of impermanence.

### Near Enemies of Equanimity and Common Misunderstandings

- Indifference is identified as the primary near enemy of equanimity, as it masquerades as peace but is actually rooted in fear, withdrawal, and unhealthy detachment rather than spacious loving presence.

- Passivity is named as another common misunderstanding, where people fear that becoming peaceful means losing motivation or the ability to act effectively in the world.

- Loss of control is also cited as a fear associated with equanimity, along with unfamiliarity with contentment as a state, which is described as something contemporary culture does not promote or sell.

- Equanimity is described as a necessary balance to loving kindness and compassion practice, because excessive cultivation of love without wisdom can tip into attachment, where one believes they can fix or save another person.

- The teaching that another person's happiness and suffering depend on their own actions and thoughts, not on one's wishes for them, is presented as the wisdom that disentangles practitioners from codependence and grasping.

### Dimensions and Qualities of Equanimity

- Spaciousness is described as a core dimension of equanimity, illustrated by the image of a spoonful of salt dissolved in a pond rather than a cup, where the vastness of mind renders difficulties clear and refreshing rather than overwhelming.

- Timelessness is identified as another dimension, with Aldous Huxley's critique of idolatrous progress cited to illustrate how substituting time for eternity keeps people perpetually oriented toward the future rather than resting in the present moment.

- Ease is described as a Taoist quality of equanimity, illustrated through a story of children building sandcastles, defending them, fighting over them, and then abandoning them entirely when called home, pointing to how attachment and conflict dissolve naturally when perspective shifts.

- Disentanglement from the constructed nature of all things, including companies, civilizations, and personal histories, is offered as a fruit of equanimity, allowing practitioners to see life as an amazing dance without making that view superficial.

- Suzuki Roshi's teaching on sitting in meditation even when a child is suffering from a hopeless disease is cited to illustrate that equanimity does not mean turning away from the world but rather taking one's seat in the middle of it.

### Teaching Equanimity to Diverse Audiences

- Evoking the felt sense and value of a peaceful heart is described as the first pedagogical step, inviting students to reflect on how it feels to live stirred up and conflicted versus how it feels to live with inner peace.

- Students are also invited to reflect on people they know who move through the world with a peaceful heart and to consider what it is like to be in their presence.

- Jack describes teaching equanimity to a group of accomplished women of color and initially feeling nervous that the teaching about others' happiness depending on their own actions might sound callous or uncaring given their lived experiences of struggle.

- The response from the group was one of deep appreciation, as many of the women identified with the burden of being looked to as saviors within their communities and found the equanimity teaching genuinely meaningful and liberating.

### Pedagogical Approach to Delivering Teachings on Equanimity

- Tara reflects on how Jack's delivery modeled a back-and-forth movement between evoking direct experience and offering conceptual frameworks, such as naming the four levels of mindfulness and the qualities of equanimity.

- This alternation is described as serving different learning styles and as a principle that applies not only to live talks but also to curriculum design, which can engage multiple dimensions of a student's being rather than following a purely logical sequence.

- A story about a vice president who resolves his embarrassment about wetting his pants rather than addressing the physical problem is used as a pop quiz to test whether participants can distinguish true equanimity from its near enemy of indifference.

- An older woman's reaction to equanimity teachings, describing them as stripping away the richness of human experience and making everything too even-keeled, is cited as an example of a common misunderstanding that teachers must be prepared to address.

- Ajahn Buddhadasa's concept of "everyday nirvana" is introduced as a powerful teaching tool, pointing students toward small ordinary moments of non-grasping and non-resistance, such as the moment before sleep, a hug, or cutting vegetables, as accessible tastes of peace.

- These small moments are described as naturally sustaining living beings, providing periods of coolness, wholeness, and ease without which people would either die or become insane.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to integrate both heart practices and mindfulness practices fluidly when designing curricula or responding to live groups, rather than relying on a fixed model.

- Pointing students toward everyday moments of natural ease and non-grasping is identified as a valuable and accessible way to make equanimity teachings tangible and relevant.

- Addressing near enemies and common misunderstandings proactively when teaching equanimity is recognized as essential to ensuring students receive the teachings accurately and without resistance.

- **Equanimity as Balance to Loving Kindness**
  - Extensive loving kindness and compassion practice can lead to attachment
  - Equanimity practice introduces the wisdom that another's happiness and suffering depend on their own actions and thoughts, not on one's wishes for them
  - This disentangles practitioners from codependence and grasping
  - Equanimity balances the heart quality of love with the wisdom quality of non-attachment
- **Dimensions of Equanimity**

  - Timelessness: equanimity releases from the bondage of time; Aldous Huxley's warning against substituting time for eternity

- **Everyday Nirvana**
  - Ajahn Buddhadasa's concept of everyday nirvana — small natural moments of non-grasping and non-resisting throughout the day
  - Examples: the moment before sleep, gardening, a hug, cutting vegetables
  - Ajahn Buddhadasa: "Without such moments living things would either die or become insane"

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
- **The Interconnectedness of Personal and Collective Suffering**
  - Practitioners naturally feel woven into the fabric of the world's conflicts, not just their own personal ones
  - Dr. King's "mutual garment of destiny" frames the inseparability of individual and collective experience
  - Teachers of mindfulness must be equipped to help students hold both dimensions simultaneously
- **Peace as an Active, Engaged State**
  - True peace is not withdrawal, passivity, or indifference to life
  - Ajahn Chah's teaching frames peace as stepping out of the battlefield, not abandoning the world
  - Thich Nhat Hanh's image of the calm person on the refugee boat illustrates that one grounded presence can change the outcome for many
  - William Butler Yeats' image: making the mind like still water so that beings gather around to see their own images and live with a clearer, fiercer life
- **Consciousness as the Ground of Peace**
  - The deepest peace arises from recognizing consciousness itself as timeless, unborn, and ever-present
  - This is not a personal "I" witnessing but consciousness itself, which cannot be stopped
  - Suzuki Roshi: "When you realize the fact that everything changes, and find your composure in it, there you find yourself in nirvana"
  - The Dhammapada and Diamond Sutra: experiences are like a star at dawn, a flash of lightning, an echo, a rainbow, a dream — they have no fixed substance
- **Equanimity and Social Responsibility**
  - Equanimity does not mean indifference to injustice or suffering; it means engaging from a place of inner stability
  - The teaching on non-attachment to outcomes is especially relevant for community leaders and caregivers who risk being overwhelmed
  - The practice of equanimity supports sustained, wise engagement rather than burnout or codependence
- **The Mystery of Interconnection**
  - The elephant story from Botswana illustrates that humans are not alone and are woven together with all beings
  - Equanimity opens practitioners to the mystery and unbearable beauty of life alongside its sorrows
  - The Zen oxherding image points to returning to the world with bliss-bestowing hands after resting in the ground of consciousness
- **Impermanence as a Societal Teaching**
  - Whole civilizations, major events, and personal histories all arise and vanish
  - Y2K and childhood are offered as examples of things that seemed significant and are now gone
  - Seeing with the eyes of a sage means recognizing this impermanent dance without superficiality

### Stories
- **The Refugee Boat** — Jack Kornfield, referencing Thich Nhat Hanh: When crowded Vietnamese refugee boats met with storms or pirates, if everyone panicked all would be lost, but if even one person remained calm it was enough to show the way for everyone to survive.
- **The Yellow Butterfly on the Cannon** — Jack Kornfield, sharing a poem: A yellow butterfly perched on the muzzle of a cannon slowly opens and shuts its wings, illustrating the possibility of a whole other way of being amidst conflict.
- **Ajahn Chah and Ajahn Mun** — Jack Kornfield, recounting his teacher Ajahn Chah's story: After years of forest practice and many experiences of meditation, light, samadhi, and jhana, Ajahn Chah went to the greatest master of his day, Ajahn Mun, told him about all his experiences, and asked how he was doing. Ajahn Mun told him he had missed the point — those experiences are just experiences like movies on a screen. The only liberating question is to whom do they happen.
- **The Children's Sand Castles** — Jack Kornfield, sharing a teaching story: Children playing by a river built sand castles, defended them, fought over them, and then when their mothers called at evening, no one cared anymore and they stamped them down and walked away. Used to illustrate the arising and passing of attachment and conflict.
- **Salt in a Cup versus Salt in a Pond** — Jack Kornfield, sharing a traditional image: A spoonful of salt in a cup of water makes it undrinkably salty, but the same spoonful in a pond leaves the water clear and refreshing, illustrating the spaciousness of equanimity.
- **The Elephant Council in Botswana** — Jack Kornfield, recounting a story about a dear friend: A group of people went to the Okavango Delta in Botswana on a flatbed truck to sit in peaceful council and make prayers of connection with elephants. Within 15 minutes a large senior elephant walked up and stuck its head into the middle of the group, staying for 20 minutes. When they drove away, the elephant returned over a ridge with 25 other elephants that lined their exit. The friend said it changed her whole mind about who we are in this world and that we are not alone.
- **Teaching Equanimity to Women of Color** — Jack Kornfield, recounting his own experience: Jack was adopted into a group of accomplished women of color at Alice Walker's home, including a professor at Berkeley among others. He was nervous that the teaching "your happiness and suffering depend on your thoughts and actions and not my wishes for you" might sound callous. After teaching it, the response was deep appreciation. The women described being overwhelmed by community members seeking them to solve problems and said the practice became something really important and meaningful.
- **The Vice President and the Therapist** — Tara Brach, sharing a story as a pop quiz: A vice president of a large company was admired for his drive but would wet his pants each time he entered the president's office. The president sent him to a urologist at company expense. He returned the next week with wet pants again. When asked if he had seen the urologist, he said the urologist was out so he saw a therapist instead and was cured — he no longer felt embarrassed. Tara used this as a quiz to distinguish true equanimity from a near enemy.
- **The Older Woman at the Retreat** — Tara Brach, recounting her mother's response: After a talk on equanimity that included all the teachings Jack described, an older woman came up to Tara and said that if Buddhism is about equanimity it is not for her because it strips away the richness of human experience and is too even keel. Tara identified this woman as her own mother, who had attended about ten retreats, illustrating how common the misunderstanding of equanimity as blankness or flatness is.
