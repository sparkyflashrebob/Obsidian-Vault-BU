### MMTCP Mod 01 — What is Meditation
📅 October 15, 2026
👥 Main Teachers: Jack Kornfield, Tara Brach
### Outline
### Defining Meditation and Its Plural Nature

- Pausing to turn inward and pay attention is described as a radical act, one that constitutes a genuine inner pilgrimage even without physical movement.

- Adding a plural dimension to the word "meditation" is proposed as a helpful reframe, since treating it as a single unified practice leads to oversimplification, much like researching "sport" without distinguishing between mountain climbing, ping pong, and football.

- At its core, meditation is defined as the training, directing, or deliberate focusing of attention, and as a uniquely human capacity to open to perspectives larger than ordinary consciousness or the small, separate sense of self.

- Over one hundred forms of meditation exist across shamanic, Buddhist, Hindu, Kabbalistic, Jewish, Christian mystical, Sufi, Muslim, Baha'i, and Zoroastrian traditions.

### Major Categories of Meditation Practice

- Devotional practices form one primary category, focusing the practitioner on inspiration, love, and surrender, often through prayer, song, and visualization directed toward spirits or the divine in various forms.

- Contemplative practices represent another category, in which a sacred passage, prayer, theme such as gratitude or suffering, or an important spiritual question is placed in the mind and heart and reflected upon repeatedly.

- Concentration practices constitute a third major category, training the mind to attend fully and, at the deepest level, to become unified with a particular experience such as a candle, a sacred mandala, or a natural object.

- Repetition is the essential mechanism of concentration, requiring the practitioner to return to the object thousands of times until thoughts and other aspects of identity fall away, producing a mind that is focused, quiet, peaceful, and often ecstatic.

- Mantra practice is highlighted as a widely accessible form of concentration, in which the sacred repetition of a sound or name shifts consciousness from ordinary discursive thinking toward the quality of experience the sound invites.

- Zen koan practice is noted as functioning similarly to mantra, with questions such as "What is the sound of one hand clapping?" repeated silently to focus and concentrate the mind.

- Body-based concentration practices are also identified, through which deepening focus allows the practitioner to sense, and ultimately dissolve, the body into vibrations, light, or space.

- Heart-state meditations form a further category, directing attention toward compassion, joy, love, peace, gratitude, or forgiveness as the primary object of practice.

- All of these forms are understood to serve awakening by shifting the practitioner from the ordinary discursive mind and the small sense of self toward something more timeless, wiser, and interconnected.

### Mindfulness and Loving Awareness as the Core Training

- Awareness or mindfulness practice, also called insight or Vipassana practice, is identified as the specific form of meditation being trained in the program.

- A synonym offered for mindfulness is "loving attention" or "loving awareness," defined as an attention focused on present experience with the capacity to observe and be fully present to body, mind, spirit, and the vastness of life.

- This quality of presence is distinguished from reactivity and identification, offering instead a place of clarity, perspective, ease, and care.

- Mindfulness is defined as non-judgmental, caring attention to the here and now, drawing on multiple formulations including those of Jon Kabat-Zinn, Thich Nhat Hanh, and Jack Kornfield.

- Traditionally, mindfulness in the Pali and Sanskrit term "Sati Sampajanya" has two parts: a receptive dimension of spacious, kind, non-judging awareness of the present, and an active dimension of appropriate response to the present situation.

- The active dimension is emphasized as critical because mindfulness is often mistakenly understood as passive, when in fact it orients the practitioner and enables a mindful response.

- Mindfulness is described as universally applicable and liberating, beneficial in parenting, physical health, relationships, creative arts, athletics, community, education, and healthcare.

### The Four Foundations of Mindfulness

- Mindfulness is traditionally understood through four dimensions or foundations that will be explored across the training series.

- The first foundation is mindfulness of the body, encompassing the senses of seeing, hearing, smelling, and touching, as well as awareness of one's own physical body, the bodies of others, and the body of the environment.

- Mindfulness of feelings constitutes the second foundation, recognized as profoundly liberating because feelings determine a great deal of what unfolds in human experience.

- The third foundation is mindfulness of mind, primarily encompassing awareness of the thoughts, beliefs, images, and mental structures used to interpret the world.

- Mindfulness of the process of life itself forms the fourth foundation, described as a loose translation of mindfulness of the dharmas, concerned with seeing how life operates rather than focusing on its content.

- This fourth foundation represents the shift from content to the invitation of freedom, recognizing that in any moment there is a possibility to be caught or to be free.

- A moment of genuine mindfulness is described as a moment of freedom, in which a quality of witnessing, knowing, and loving awareness acknowledges what is happening without being entangled in it.

### The Relationship Between Concentration and Mindfulness

- Historically, concentration and mindfulness are treated as somewhat separate in meditation training, with some approaches beginning in focused concentration before opening to mindfulness, and others beginning with focused mindfulness before broadening.

- The Visuddhimagga, a thousand-page Theravada Buddhist commentary approximately two thousand years old, lists forty or more methods of concentration and various approaches to developing mindfulness for those interested in historical roots.

- Stabilizing attention is typically the starting point in practice, since mindfulness is difficult to sustain when attention is scattered, and anchoring to the breath, body, or sound is the most common method for arriving in the present.

- Once presence is established, the practitioner is invited to open to, feel, sense, experience, and learn from whatever is present.

- The most skillful trainings for contemporary culture are identified as those incorporating all four foundations of mindfulness, integrating awareness of body, feelings, mind, and relationships rather than focusing on only one dimension.

### The Purpose and Deeper Significance of Meditation

- The purpose of meditation is not to stop human experience but to pause within it, perceive the impermanence of changing conditions, and recognize what entangles us in suffering versus what releases us into joy.

- Mindfulness is described as an invitation to learn both how to live fully and how to die well, with the practice building a capacity for bearing witness, kindness, and trust that sustains practitioners even at the end of life.

- Beyond the well-documented neuroscientific benefits of resilience, stability, concentration, and physical healing, meditation is understood as an invitation into the sacred.

- Quieting the mind and inviting a sacred pause opens practitioners not only to wiser self-care and greater care for others, but also to vastness and mystery, described as the presence of eternity rather than a concern for the future.

- Modern consumer society is characterized as functioning like an addict, promoting speed, distraction, and numbing in order to keep people from fully confronting the insecurity of life, unfinished emotional business, and misalignment with their own values.

- Stopping and becoming present inevitably surfaces these truths, yet awareness and compassion are described as spacious enough to hold all of human experience, including grief, pain, and the unbearable beauty of life.

- Meditation teachers are framed as carriers of the sacred in a culture defined by its absence, responding to a deep longing in people to have the sacred recognized and affirmed.

### The Role of Loving Kindness and the Path with Heart

- Loving kindness, or metta, is woven into mindfulness from the beginning as a necessary counterbalance to the self-judgment, striving, and grasping that characterize contemporary culture.

- Bringing compassion and care for one's own humanity into the practice ensures that mindfulness becomes genuine presence to things as they are, rather than an attempt to change or improve experience.

- The question of whether a path has a heart is offered as the essential criterion for evaluating any practice or direction in life, drawn from the teachings of Don Juan as recorded by Carlos Castaneda.

### The Dissolution of the Meditator and the Nature of Awakening

- Tara Brach observes that practitioners frequently ask which single practice they should follow, reflecting a tendency to become attached to strategies and skillful means rather than recognizing them as tools.

- The deepest purpose of meditation is described as dissolving the meditator, releasing the sense of a separate person manipulating the levers of experience from behind a curtain.

- Genuine liberation arises not from further refinement of technique but from stopping the doing and resting in the natural, intuitive, awake openness that is always already present.

- The story of Ananda illustrates this principle, showing that vigorous striving the night before a council of enlightened ones produced only exhaustion, while releasing all effort and resting toward dawn allowed enlightenment to arise naturally.

- Skillful means and structured trainings are affirmed as necessary because they undo the conditioning in which practitioners have become caught, gradually enabling the capacity to rest back into what is already here.

- The synergy between disciplined practice and effortless resting is emphasized, with both elements understood as essential rather than contradictory.

### Remembering Fundamental Goodness and the Teacher's Role

- Buddhist texts open with the address "O nobly born," reminding practitioners not to forget who they truly are, pointing to a fundamental goodness and basic awareness that constitutes their true nature.

- This true nature is described as comprised of awareness itself and of love, accessible whenever the ordinary sense of a small, separate self is released.

- Teachers who remember this are able to perceive what Thomas Merton called the secret beauty in those who come to practice with them, a beauty untouched by struggle, pain, ignorance, or confusion.

- Seeing this goodness in others is understood as a practical act of teaching, since people often act better when they are seen in their highest nature, as expressed by Nelson Mandela.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to develop their own personal definition of mindfulness drawn from direct experience and their own heart, in preparation for answering questions from those they will teach.

- The training will proceed through all four foundations of mindfulness across the first series of sessions, with body, feelings, mind, and the process of life each receiving dedicated attention.

- Loving awareness practice is established as the core meditation approach for the program, integrating presence to life itself rather than orientation toward a particular state, visualization, or mantra.

- The group will continue exploring how to introduce the sacred dimension of practice appropriately within various teaching contexts such as schools and businesses.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**What is Meditation**
- Meditation at its core is the training, directing, or focusing of attention in a deliberate way
- It is the human capacity to open to perspectives larger than ordinary consciousness or the small separate sense of self
- There are approximately 100 forms of meditation spanning shamanic, Buddhist, Hindu, Kabbalistic, Jewish, Christian mystical, Sufi, Muslim, Baha'i, and Zoroastrian traditions
- Early research treated meditation as a single phenomenon, which Jack Kornfield compared to researching "sport" without differentiating between mountain climbing, ping pong, and football
**Categories of Meditation**
- Devotional practice: focuses on inspiration and love; includes prayer, song, and visualization; involves surrender and connection to something larger
- Contemplation: taking a sacred passage, prayer, or theme and reflecting on it repeatedly; raising important spiritual questions
- Concentration: the ability to attend fully and become unified with a particular experience; requires repetition — 100, 1,000, 10,000 times — until thoughts and other identities drop away; produces a quiet, peaceful, directed, and often ecstatic mind; Gandhi called this repetition "blessed monotony"
- Concentration forms include: visual focus on a mandala, candle, or image; mantra practice (including TM); koan practice in Zen; body-focused concentration
- Heart-state meditations: focusing on compassion, joy, love, peace, gratitude, or forgiveness
- Mindfulness/Insight practice (Vipassana): the primary focus of the training being taught
**Mindfulness Defined**
- Jon Kabat-Zinn, Thich Nhat Hanh, and Jack Kornfield each have their own definitions; teachers are encouraged to develop their own
- Jack Kornfield's description: non-judgmental, caring attention, here and now
- Traditional Pali/Sanskrit term: Sati Sampajanya — two parts:
  - Sati: spacious, kind, non-judging awareness of the present (receptive)
  - Sampajanya: appropriate response to the present situation (active)
- Mindfulness is not passive — it orients and enables mindful response
- Described as "all helpful" and liberating across all areas of life
**Four Foundations of Mindfulness**
- Body: includes the senses, physical body, and mindfulness of the environment and others' bodies
- Feelings: mindfulness of feelings, which determine much of human experience
- Mind: mindfulness of thoughts, beliefs, images, and structures used to understand the world
- Process of life itself (Dharmas): seeing how life operates; shifts from content of experience to the invitation of freedom; wherever one becomes present is a moment of freedom
**Loving Awareness**
- A synonym for mindfulness used in this training
- Defined as attention focused on present experience with the ability to observe and be fully present for body, mind, spirit, and the vastness of life
- Necessary because modern culture is full of ambition, striving, self-judgment, and grasping
- Mindfulness weaves in loving kindness (metta) and compassion from the beginning
- Described as a witnessing consciousness that acknowledges what is happening without being entangled in it
**Concentration vs. Mindfulness**
- Historically separated in training traditions
- Sometimes one begins with concentration and opens to mindfulness; sometimes the reverse
- Most trainings begin by stabilizing attention, often anchored through breath, body, or sound
- Reference to the Visuddhi Magga: a thousand-page Theravada Buddhist text listing 40+ ways to concentrate and various ways to develop mindfulness
- Jack Kornfield's book Living Dharma describes a dozen different ways to practice Vipassana
**Skillful Means and the Purpose of Meditation**
- Tara Brach noted that all the different practices are "skillful means" — strategies for collecting, quieting, and opening the mind stage by stage
- The deeper purpose of meditation is to dissolve the meditator — to stop feeling like a separate person manipulating experience
- Practitioners can become addicted to strategies and techniques
- True liberation comes from letting go into what is already and always present — a simple, wakeful resting
- The skillful means undo conditioning and help practitioners learn how to rest back into open awareness
**Meditation and the Sacred**
- Meditation is described as an invitation into that which is sacred
- Even in secular settings such as schools or businesses, teachers can hint at the sacred dimension
- Modern culture is defined in some ways by the absence of the sacred, creating a deep longing in people
- There are capacities in human beings — ways of knowing the mystery — that have been lost in modern times
**Pushback Against Meditation in Modern Society**
- Modern consumer society promotes numbing, busyness, and distraction
- The best-adjusted person in modern society is described as "not dead and not alive, just numb"
- Modern consumer society itself functions like an addict
- When people stop and become present, they face insecurity, unfinished business of the heart, and the possibility that they are not living their own values
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Radical Act of Stillness**
- To begin to quiet oneself and pay attention inwardly is described as a radical act
- The Javier quote illustrates that sitting still for three days can itself become a great pilgrimage
**Plurality of the Sacred**
- Just as there are many gods across traditions — Kali, Durga, Allah, Yahweh, Jesus — there are many meditations and many enlightenments
- Fighting over which is biggest or best misses the point of the plurality
**Awareness as Liberation**
- Mindfulness shifts from the content of experience to the invitation of freedom
- Wherever one becomes present is a moment of freedom
- The shift is from reactivity and identification to clarity, perspective, ease, and care
- The point is not to stop human experience but to pause in its midst and see the impermanence of waves and that which releases us into joy
**Basic Goodness and the Secret Beauty**
- There is a fundamental goodness and basic goodness in true nature, comprised of awareness itself and of love
- Teachers are invited to see this in those who come to practice with them — what Thomas Merton called "the secret beauty"
- Nelson Mandela's insight: it never hurts to see the good in another; they often act the better because of it
**Meditation, Living, and Dying**
- Mindfulness invites people to learn how to live fully and how to die well
- Tamara Engel's reflection before her death illustrates how every difficult sitting — every bored moment, every pain, every itch not scratched — was a training for kindness, for bearing witness, for a trusting spirit that carried her even as she faced death
**Modern Society as Addict**
- Modern consumer society promotes things that take the edge off and keep people slightly numbed out
- If fully alive, a person constantly says no to unhealthy processes: racism, polluted environment, arms race, unsafe water, carcinogenic foods
- It is in the interest of modern society to keep people zombie-like
**Consciousness and Interconnection**
- Human beings are described as consciousness itself, truly connected
- There are capacities — ways of knowing — that have been delegated to technology in modern times but remain part of what it means to be woven into the fabric of life
- The question is not the future of humanity but the presence of eternity
**The Path with Heart**
- Drawing from Carlos Castaneda and Don Juan: the essential question for any path is "Does this path have a heart?"
- If it does, the path is good
- What is being shared and practiced is an invitation to follow a path with heart

### Stories
**The Krishna Temple in Varanasi**
Jack Kornfield — describing a seven-day-and-night chanting vigil at a Krishna temple on the banks of the Ganges in Varanasi. Thousands of people walked around chanting continuously; when they could no longer chant, they lay down, fell asleep, and rose again without stopping. When Jack walked into the temple, the energy of devotion had dissolved the walls and roof and opened the vastness of the world. Illustrates the power of devotional practice.
**George Shaler and the Gorillas**
Jack Kornfield — recounting the story of George Shaler, professor of biology, who studied gorillas. When asked how he gathered so much information about their lives and relationships, he said simply: "I didn't carry a gun." He was able to sit among them without fear. Used to illustrate the essence of mindfulness as loving, non-threatening attention.
**Swami Satchitananda on a Surfboard**
Jack Kornfield — describing a poster seen in a health food store in Santa Cruz in the 1970s. Swami Satchitananda was standing on one foot in tree pose on a surfboard with a loincloth, before Photoshop. The caption read: "You can't stop the waves, but you can learn to surf." Used to illustrate the essence of mindfulness — not stopping human experience but learning to be present within it.
**Tamara Engel — Facing Death with Gratitude**
Jack Kornfield — sharing words written by Tamara Engel, one of the founders of New York Insight, shortly before she died of cancer. She expressed gratitude not only for the joy and ease of her meditation practice but for the hard parts — every bored sitting, every fearful fantasy, every pain, every itch not scratched — as training for kindness, bearing witness, and a trusting spirit that carried her as she faced death.
**The Pakistani Bus Conductor Mahmid**
Jack Kornfield — describing a bus conductor in Pakistan or India who, after letting eight people on a crowded bus and turning others away, rang the bell and said to those left behind: "So sorry, the bus is full, but plenty of room in my heart." Used to illustrate the quality of loving awareness and the heart being big enough to hold all of life.
**The Woman in Labor**
Jack Kornfield — describing a friend who had taken a few yoga classes when young and later went into labor in a hospital with little support. During labor, her body opened and she felt herself becoming part of all the mothers that ever were, part of the stream of life giving birth. Her body dissolved into light. After the baby was born, she was filled with light — and was given Valium by the hospital staff. Used to illustrate moments of expanded consciousness and the sacred breaking through ordinary experience.
**Nainoa Thompson — The Hawaiian Navigator**
Jack Kornfield — describing his friend Nainoa Thompson, navigator for the Hawaiian voyaging canoe that completed its first round-the-world voyage. The navigator sits in the back for months like a yogi, staying half awake, reading stars and ocean waves bouncing off distant islands, knowing exactly where they are without any modern instruments. Used to illustrate deep human capacities for knowing the mystery that have been lost in modern times.
**Charles and His Daughter Katie**
Jack Kornfield — describing a man he worked with who had an internet company in trouble and a difficult marriage. The man's youngest daughter Katie, age four, was hospitalized for viral meningitis and went into a coma. After five weeks with no change, Katie came to her father Charles in a dream and said, "Don't worry, Daddy. It's all okay." The next morning, he walked into her room and she opened her eyes and smiled. She grew up fine. Used to illustrate consciousness and the mysterious connections that operate beyond ordinary awareness.
**The Vision of a Death in Burma**
Jack Kornfield — describing a travel companion in Burma who somehow knew that someone in her family had died, quite unexpectedly, at a time when communication home was by slow air letters. Ten days later, a telegram arrived dated the same day as her vision, confirming that her brother had died in exactly the way she had seen. Used to illustrate that human beings are truly connected as consciousness itself.
**Ananda and the Night Before the Council**
Tara Brach — recounting a story from the Buddhist tradition about Ananda, the Buddha's cousin and most devoted disciple. After the Buddha's death, a grand council of enlightened ones (Arahats) was planned, and Ananda was not included because he had not attained enlightenment — he had been too busy helping others. Determined to attend, he practiced vigorously through the night before, succeeding only in becoming exhausted and discouraged. Towards dawn, he let go of all striving and rested. In that state, free of greed for attaining and fear of not attaining, he rested his head on the pillow and became enlightened. Used to illustrate that liberation comes from letting go into what is already and always present.
