**Summary:**

This audio is a discourse on the nature and practice of meditation, likely intended for individuals interested in or beginning a mindfulness or meditation teacher training. The speaker [[Jack Kornfiled]] emphasizes that "meditation" is not a singular activity but encompasses a wide variety of practices.

He begins by encouraging listeners to reflect on their own understanding of meditation, as they will likely be asked to explain it. He humorously mentions sometimes saying he's in "sales" or "theater" to avoid lengthy explanations.

The core of meditation, he explains, is the **training or directing of attention in a deliberate way**, allowing humans to open to perspectives larger than ordinary consciousness. He categorizes meditations, including:

1.  **Devotional practices:** Focusing on inspiration, love, prayer, worship, visualization, connecting to something bigger. He shares an anecdote about a Krishna temple chant.
2.  **Contemplations:** Reflecting on sacred texts, prayers, themes (like gratitude or suffering), or important spiritual questions.

He then focuses on **concentration practices**, which involve focusing the mind to achieve stillness, peace, and sometimes ecstatic states. This can be done through visual objects (candle flame, mandala), sound (mantra, like TM), or the body. He mentions koans in Zen as a similar focusing tool. He also highlights body-focused concentrations that can lead to experiences of dissolving into vibrations, light, or space, and heart-state meditations (compassion, joy, love, peace, gratitude, forgiveness).

The speaker asserts that all these practices ultimately serve **awakening** by shifting from the ordinary, discursive mind (the "body of fear") to something greater and more timeless.

He then specifically addresses **mindfulness practice (Vipassanā or insight meditation)**, defining it as "loving attention" or "loving awareness." This involves:

1.  A spacious, kind, non-judging awareness of the present moment.
2.  An appropriate response to the present situation.

He clarifies that mindfulness is not passive but actively orients us to the present, allowing for skillful response. The traditional four foundations of mindfulness are mentioned: body (including senses, and extending to others' bodies and the earth), feelings, mind (thoughts, beliefs, images), and the process of life itself (the "dharmas" – seeing impermanence, ungovernability, and what leads to suffering vs. freedom).

The speaker emphasizes that a moment of mindfulness is a moment of freedom. He concludes by quoting Tamara Engel, highlighting that the "hard parts" of meditation (boredom, restlessness, fear, pain) are training for kindness, bearing witness, and a trusting spirit.
