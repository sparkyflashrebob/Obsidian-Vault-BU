# MMTCP Mod 01 - The Art of Practice - Oct, 15

### The Art of Practice
📅 October 15, 2026
👥 Tara Brach (Main Teacher), Jack Kornfield (Contributing Teacher)
### Outline
### The Role of Intention and Motivation in Meditation Practice

- Whatever is practiced regularly grows stronger, meaning that cultivating judgment reinforces judgment, while cultivating awareness of what truly matters deepens that sense of care and purpose.

- Sincerity of motivation and clarity of aspiration are identified as the primary factors distinguishing practitioners who continue to deepen their practice from those who plateau or quit.

- Richard Baker's distillation of Buddhist practice as "intention and attention" captures the inseparability of why one practices and how one pays attention.

- Intention and attention operate in a virtuous cycle, where a clear sense of what matters shapes the quality of attention, and refined attention in turn deepens contact with one's intention.

- Pausing before a meditation session to genuinely reconnect with one's reason for practicing is described as a way to bring innocence and fullness to the body before beginning.

### Qualities of Presence That Support Wise Meditation

- Qualities such as curiosity, kindness, receptivity, authenticity, willingness, intimacy, wonder, and courage are identified by participants as the ways one meets arising experience when grounded in sincere intention.

- Friendliness, interest, care, and openness are described as the core dimensions of presence that teachers should help students cultivate and recognize in themselves.

- Contrasting qualities such as impatience, resistance, judgment, planning, restlessness, sleepiness, controlling, and boredom arise when practice is approached with egoic or dutiful motivations rather than genuine aspiration.

- Boredom and restlessness during meditation are understood as signs of a lack of genuine willingness or openness to present experience.

### The Problem of Unwise Effort in Meditation

- Striving, described as trying to get somewhere or achieve a particular state, is identified as one of the most prevalent forms of unwise effort, particularly within Western cultural conditioning.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's feedback to the San Francisco Zen Center, advising practitioners to get up a little later and to smile, illustrates how grimness and over-efforting undermine the spirit of practice.

- Cultural messaging around rapid results, such as promises of monk-like meditation in four weeks, reinforces unrealistic expectations that are antithetical to genuine practice.

- Fighting, or self-aversion, is a second major category of unwise effort, manifesting as self-judgment about the quality of one's meditation, comparisons with others, and attachment to how a session should feel.

- Self-judgment smothers sincere aspiration, making it impossible to feel genuine motivation when one is caught in evaluating one's performance.

- Flight, the third category, involves self-doubt, discouragement, resignation, and a sense that meaningful progress is simply not possible for oneself.

- Most practitioners and students carry a mixture of striving, fighting, and flight, and recognizing these patterns in oneself is part of developing a wiser relationship with practice.

### Communicating That Difficulty in Practice Is Not Personal

- One of the most compassionate and important messages a teacher can offer is that difficulty in meditation is not a personal failing but a universal human experience.

- Suzuki Roshi's acknowledgment that he himself finds sitting difficult is offered as an example of how normalizing struggle removes the shame that causes people to quit.

- Group sharing is highlighted as a particularly effective vehicle for helping practitioners realize that everyone faces the same challenges, transforming a private sense of inadequacy into a shared human condition.

- A leaning Buddha purchased for a community altar became an unintentional symbol of the teaching that imperfection does not negate the presence of the underlying gold, reinforcing the message that practice does not require a perfect form.

- Ruth King's framing of existence as "not perfect, not permanent, and not personal" is offered as a concise and memorable way to communicate this principle to students.

### Building Trust in the Possibility and Power of Practice

- Students need to trust that transformation through practice is genuinely possible before they will commit themselves fully to it, and teachers carry the responsibility of conveying this credibly.

- Stories of practice bearing fruit should be relatable and grounded in ordinary daily experience, rather than so extraordinary that students dismiss them as irrelevant to their own lives.

- A Tibetan practice of 100,000 prostrations illustrates how full-hearted engagement with any practice, even one that initially seems mechanical, can lead to a vivid and awakened perception of the world.

- Anything given oneself to fully has the potential to awaken, but that full giving requires trust that the practice is genuinely part of one's path.

### The Principle of Balanced Effort

- The classical Buddhist image of a musical instrument, neither too loose nor too tight, describes the quality of effort that allows the music of the spirit to flow naturally.

- Balanced effort avoids both the rigidity of over-striving and the laxity of disengagement, and teachers are encouraged to help students find and maintain this middle ground.

### Establishing a Regular and Flexible Practice

- Regular practice is compared to exercise in that consistency is the essential condition for it to produce meaningful results, regardless of the population being taught.

- A personal commitment to practice every day without conditions on duration, posture, or style is described as a highly effective approach, because it preserves rhythm while removing perfectionism as a barrier.

- Nature itself is described as loving rhythm, and the body, heart, and mind are said to respond naturally to the regularity of returning home to oneself each day.

- Rumi's question "Do you make regular visits to yourself?" is used to evoke the quality of homecoming that a consistent practice cultivates over time.

- Practical examples such as meditating on the subway, sitting by a tree, or using the time a kettle takes to boil illustrate that regular practice can be adapted to any lifestyle or schedule.

- Even a very brief session at the end of a busy day, consisting of a few breaths and a moment of presence, counts as practice and maintains the continuity of the rhythm.

### Customizing Practice to Individual Needs

- No two body-minds are alike, and every student must ultimately take responsibility for customizing their practice to what most effectively brings them into the present moment.

- Some students benefit most from a collecting or anchoring practice, while others may need loving-kindness work to soften the heart, and still others may need movement-based practice to establish connection with the day.

- Teachers are encouraged to approach guidance as an invitation to experiment, fostering in students a spirit of curiosity and inner listening rather than adherence to a fixed method.

- Gratification is identified as an essential element of sustainable practice, with early rewards such as stress relief and clarity gradually giving way to the deeper satisfaction of resting in awareness and love.

- Playing one's edge is also necessary for growth, meaning that students who find peace easily should be gently encouraged to open to difficulty, maintaining the aliveness and forward movement of practice.

### The Necessity of Feedback and Community in Practice

- Practice conducted in isolation is described as a setup for habituation and stagnation, making feedback and shared inquiry essential components of genuine development.

- Sharing practice with others, whether in classes, peer sitting groups, or phone-based meditation communities, provides the cross-fertilization of perspectives that keeps practice alive and evolving.

- Jack Kornfield emphasizes that sitting with others helps practitioners expand their window of tolerance, allowing them to be present with the full range of human experience rather than only what feels manageable alone.

- James Baldwin's observation that people cling to hatred because they cannot face their own pain is used to illustrate the cultural dimension of what practitioners are working against when they sit down to meditate.

- Alice Walker's passage from The Color Purple is invoked to remind teachers that the invitation of practice is not only to be present with suffering but also to perceive the beauty and dazzle of life.

- In the marriage of flesh and spirit, divorce is described as impossible, meaning that practice holds both the tears and the beauty of existence within the same awareness.

- Helping students remember why they want to practice, including writing it down and posting it somewhere visible, is offered as a simple and effective way to sustain motivation through periods of resistance.

### The Orientation Toward Awakening as the Ground of Practice

- The metaphor of practice making one "accident prone" to enlightenment conveys that the purpose of effort is not to manufacture awakening but to be present and available when it naturally arises.

- John O'Donohue's image of living like a river, carried by the surprise of its own unfolding, is offered as a closing evocation of the quality of openness and trust that wise practice cultivates.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to develop their capacity to listen carefully to how students are approaching practice and to offer fine-tuning guidance that improves the quality and fruitfulness of individual practice.

- Communicating the universality of difficulty, the possibility of transformation, and the importance of balanced effort are identified as foundational responsibilities for teachers working across all populations and settings.

- Creating and supporting group structures for shared practice and mutual feedback is identified as a priority, given the essential role of community in sustaining and deepening individual practice.

- Upcoming sessions will continue to explore specific methods for inviting students into presence in ways that maintain safety, aliveness, and the spirit of inquiry.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Whatever We Practice Grows Stronger**
- Tara Brach opens with the principle that whatever we practice becomes stronger — judgment, caring, awareness, or love
- The goal of practice is to manifest more fully the natural awareness and love that is our true nature
**Deliberate Practice and Mastery**
- Tara references the science of deliberate practice — the study of what enables real mastery in any field
- It is more than 10,000 hours; there are qualitative guidelines that determine whether practice truly frees us
- As teachers, the role includes listening carefully to how students are approaching practice and fine-tuning the quality of their effort
**Why Some People Wake Up and Others Plateau**
- Some practitioners work out stress and find centering but do not continue to deepen
- Others continue to unfold in wisdom and heart-opening
- The key differentiator observed is sincerity, motivation, and aspiration — a clear and conscious sense of what truly matters
**Intention and Attention**
- Richard Baker's statement at a Tricycle conference: "The essence of Buddhism is intention and attention"
- Intention and attention are inseparable — how we pay attention deepens our contact with our intention, creating a virtuous cycle
- Qualities of presence when rooted in sincere intention include: curiosity, kindness, receptivity, authenticity, willingness, intimacy, wonder, courage, and full allowing
**Egoic Patterns in Practice — Unwise Effort**
- When sitting from a place of duty, performance pressure, or agitation, the qualities of attention shift to: impatience, resistance, judging, planning, restlessness, sleepiness, controlling, boredom
- Three clusters of unwise effort identified:
  - Striving: too much efforting, trying to get somewhere, trying to achieve a state
  - Fight (self-aversion): judging one's performance, comparing to others, expecting a certain kind of meditation
  - Flight: self-doubt, discouragement, resignation, a sense that it will never be what one wishes
**Tara's Personal Experience with Striving**
- Tara spent her first decade in an ashram with wholehearted passion but also a strong self-project of striving
- She set a goal of enlightenment in seven years, got up at 3:30 AM, took cold showers, did extra practice
- Every teacher she consulted told her simply to relax
- Over forty years she learned to enjoy practice and become more relaxed, which helped her recognize these patterns in students
**It's Not Personal**
- One of the most compassionate and important messages for students: difficulty in meditation is not personal
- Suzuki Roshi told his students he had many difficulties in his own practice
- Group sharing helps people realize everyone faces the same challenges, removing the shame that causes people to quit
- The leaning Buddha story illustrates that imperfection does not negate the gold within
- Ruth King's framing: existence is not perfect, not permanent, and not personal
**Trust in the Power of Practice**
- The Buddha said he would not teach if transformation were not possible
- Students need to trust that change is possible for practice to take root
- Stories of practice should be relatable and ordinary, not so extraordinary that students feel excluded
- The Tibetan practice of 100,000 prostrations is cited as an example of how full commitment transforms perception
**Regularity and Flexibility**
- Like exercise, meditation must be regular to be effective
- Tara's personal commitment: practice every day no matter what, with a wide back door — any length, any posture, any style
- This commitment has held for over thirty-one years
- Nature loves rhythm; the body, heart, and mind respond to the rhythm of regular return to oneself
- Examples of how people find their own form: meditating on the subway, sitting by a tree, waiting for a kettle to boil
**Customizing Practice**
- No two body-minds are alike; students must be encouraged to customize their practice
- Some need more collecting and anchoring; others need loving-kindness to soften the heart; others need movement
- The spirit of experimentation and inner listening is encouraged
- Gratification is essential — initially through stress relief or clarity, ultimately through the homecoming to awareness and love
**Deliberate Practice and Feedback**
- Practice in a vacuum leads to habituation and stagnation
- Feedback from teachers, peers, and community is essential for continued growth
- Tara encourages students to share practice in groups, peer sitting circles, and formats like TeleSangha
- Cross-fertilization of experience is described as juicy and vital
**Helping Students Remember Why They Practice**
- A simple tool: writing down what matters and posting it somewhere visible
- Helps students return to practice as a gift to the soul and a new relationship with inner life and the world
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Cultural Conditioning Against Presence**
- Our cultural conditioning drives us toward the future in a chain of reactions, thinking, and planning
- Meditation runs counter to this by inviting us simply to be here
- The cultural speed and pain we carry makes sitting difficult; we project inner pain outward onto others rather than tolerating the insecurity of being human
- Jack Kornfield references James Baldwin: people cling to hate and ignorance because once it is gone they must face their own pain
- The cultural pattern of finding an "enemy du jour" — communists, immigrants, various groups — is rooted in the inability to tolerate vulnerability
**Expanding the Window of Tolerance**
- Neuroscience frames the work of meditation as expanding the window of tolerance to be with our full humanity
- This includes both the capacity to be present with pain and difficulty and the capacity to perceive beauty and wonder
**Not Being Loyal to Suffering**
- Jack Kornfield emphasizes that people do not have to remain loyal to the suffering of their lives
- Alice Walker's passage from The Color Purple is cited: noticing the color purple in a field is itself a spiritual act; the world is always trying to please us back
- The invitation of practice includes both presence with the tears of the world and the capacity to see its dazzle and beauty
**The Marriage of Flesh and Spirit**
- Jack Kornfield states: in the marriage of flesh and spirit, divorce is impossible
- Practice holds both beauty and tears within awareness
**Enlightenment as Accident**
- Enlightenment is described as an accident; practice makes you accident-prone
- The master-student dialogue: you cannot make the sun rise, but practice ensures you are there for the sunrise
- John O'Donohue's closing image: living like a river flows, carried by the surprise of its own unfolding
**The Role of Community**
- Practicing together dissolves the delusion of being a separate self trying to get somewhere
- Group sharing, classes, peer sitting groups, and TeleSangha are all vehicles for waking up together

### Stories
**The Tricycle Conference and Richard Baker**
Tara Brach shares that in 2001, the week before 9/11, she attended a conference sponsored by Tricycle at the Twin Towers. Six keynote speakers, all well-known Dharma teachers, were each asked to speak to the essence of Buddhism in five to eight minutes. Richard Baker, Shunryu Suzuki's Dharma heir, stood up, paused, bowed, said "The essence of Buddhism is intention and attention," bowed again, and walked off. Tara, speaking second, was so struck she has no memory of what she said.
**Tara's Decade in the Ashram**
Tara Brach describes her first decade in a spiritual community where she rose at 3:30 AM, took cold showers, meditated, did yoga for two and a half hours, and chanted. She set a personal goal of enlightenment within seven years. Every teacher she consulted told her simply to relax. She describes this not with pride but as an illustration of striving and self-aversion in practice.
**The Leaning Buddha**
Tara and a friend purchased a Buddha for the altar at her Wednesday night teaching community in Washington DC. The group gathered excitedly, but afterward students noticed the Buddha had been cast with a lean — it was not sitting upright. Initially Tara felt she had made a mistake, but the community reframed it: the cast is off, just as our conditioning goes all different ways, and it does not mean the gold is not there. The leaning Buddha became their Buddha.
**The Woman from the World Bank**
Jonathan tells the story of a woman from the World Bank who said she was too busy to meditate. When pressed, she eventually found her own solution: she would plug in her electric tea kettle and her sit was the time she waited for it to boil. This became her portal into practice. Shared by Tara Brach, attributed to Jonathan.
**The Tibetan Lama and 100,000 Prostrations**
Tara Brach shares the story of a lama who, after completing his first 10,000 prostrations, was told by his teacher they were no good and were thrown away. He began again, more simply and wholeheartedly. As the months continued, the trees became vibrant and the eyes of the people he met grew vivid with their story. The story illustrates how full commitment to practice transforms perception.
**Tara's Commitment After Leaving the Ashram**
After leaving her spiritual community and having her son Narayan at the same time, Tara's practice became sporadic. She made a commitment to practice every day no matter what, with the back door that length, posture, and style did not matter. This commitment has held for over thirty-one years.
**The Master and Student Dialogue**
A student asks a master: "Is there anything I can do to become enlightened?" The master replies: "No more than you could do to make the sun rise in the east each day." The student asks: "Then what is the purpose of all this practice?" The master answers: "So you will be there for the sunrise." Shared by Tara Brach.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Distracted and Busy Mind**
- Many people come to teachers confessing they have a busy mind, believing it is a personal failing
- Tara emphasizes this is universal; Suzuki Roshi himself acknowledged difficulty in his own practice
**Restlessness and Impatience**
- Named by participants as common experiences when sitting with egoic intention
- Linked to cultural conditioning toward speed and future-orientation
**Sleepiness and Boredom**
- Sleepiness and boredom identified as signs of lack of genuine interest or openness in practice
**Tension and Physical Discomfort**
- Jack Kornfield notes that practitioners face tension in the body alongside mental restlessness when beginning to sit
**Self-Judgment and Comparison**
- Students compare their experience to others on retreat, feeling cut off or disconnected while imagining others are opening
- The next person feels ripped up while imagining others are sitting with composure
- Self-judgment smothers the fires of sincere intent; when judging, one cannot feel sincere aspiration
**Discouragement and Resignation**
- Flight pattern: self-doubt, deflation, a sense of being defeated, resignation that practice will never be what one wishes
**Cultural Speed and Projected Pain**
- Jack Kornfield identifies the cultural speed and pain people carry into the cushion
- The inability to tolerate insecurity and vulnerability as human beings is a root challenge that meditation directly confronts
**Tara's Own Striving and Self-Aversion**
- Tara describes her own first decade as marked by a healthy dollop of striving and self-aversion mixed into genuine passion
- The drive to achieve certain states and judge herself when not doing it right
