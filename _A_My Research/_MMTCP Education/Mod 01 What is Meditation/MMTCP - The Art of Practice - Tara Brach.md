Okay, this is a rich and insightful talk by Tara Brach (with a contribution from Jack Kornfield at the end) on "The Art of Practice."

**Topics Covered in the Talk:**

1.  **The Necessity and Nature of Practice:** Beyond just knowing *about* meditation, the emphasis is on the consistent *doing* of practice.
2.  **Intention and Motivation:** The crucial role of sincere aspiration and understanding *why* one is practicing as a foundation for fruitful practice.
3.  **Qualities of Attention:** Exploring the different states of mind and attention (e.g., curiosity, kindness vs. impatience, judgment) that we bring to practice and how they shape it.
4.  **Deliberate Practice & Mastery:** Applying principles of deliberate practice to meditation to move beyond plateaus and achieve genuine waking up, not just hours spent.
5.  **Common Hindrances/Unwise Effort:** Identifying pitfalls like striving, self-aversion (fight), and self-doubt (flight) that can derail practice.
6.  **The Inseparability of Intention and Attention:** How our core values (intention) direct our focus (attention), and how focused attention, in turn, clarifies intention.
7.  **Personal Experience and Learning:** Tara shares her own journey with practice, including initial striving and how her approach evolved.
8.  **Guiding Principles for Fruitful Practice:**
    *   It's not personal (difficulties are common).
    *   Trust in the power of awareness (it's our nature).
    *   Tuning effort (not too loose, not too tight).
    *   Regularity with flexibility.
    *   The importance of feedback and community.
    *   Finding gratification in the practice itself.
9.  **The Role of Gratification & Beauty:** Jack Kornfield emphasizes the importance of finding joy and beauty in life and practice, not just focusing on suffering.
10. **Practical Advice for Teachers & Practitioners:** How to support oneself and others in cultivating a sustainable and transformative practice.

**Detailed Summary of Tara Brach's Talk on "The Art of Practice":**

Tara Brach begins by welcoming participants back and recapping a previous session by Jack Kornfield on the various types of meditation. She transitions to the core of her talk: moving beyond the *types* of meditation to the *art of practicing* itself. The goal is to cultivate mindfulness and loving awareness, which allow us to be truly awake in our lives.

**The Common Denominator: Practice**
Regardless of the style of meditation, the common denominator is the need for consistent practice. She shares a humorous anecdote from Robert Thurman who, upon hearing about endless "practice," asked, "When is the performance?" This highlights the Western tendency to look for an end-goal rather than embracing the ongoing process.

Tara emphasizes a core principle: **"Whatever we practice grows stronger."** If we practice judgment, judgment strengthens. If we practice remembering what we care about, that caring deepens. The aim of meditation practice is to more fully manifest our natural awareness and love, which is our true nature.

**Deliberate Practice and Mastery**
She introduces the concept of "deliberate practice," drawing parallels to achieving mastery in arts or sports. It's not just about logging hours (like the 10,000-hour rule) but involves specific guidelines that turn practice into a process that frees us. For teachers, this means not just giving instructions but also sensing how students are approaching practice and helping them fine-tune its quality to make it fruitful.

**The Key Differentiator: Sincere Motivation**
Tara observes that many people start meditating and either quit or plateau, lacking a continued sense of waking up. She posits that the key difference between those who become habituated and those who keep waking up lies in **sincerity in motivation or aspiration**—a clear, conscious sense of what truly matters.

**The Twin Towers Conference & Richard Baker's Wisdom**
She recounts a powerful story from a conference she attended in the Twin Towers the week before 9/11. Richard Baker, Shunryu Suzuki's dharma heir, was asked, "What is the core of Buddhist practice?" His concise answer was: **"The essence of Buddhism is intention and attention."** He then bowed and walked off.
This profoundly impacted Tara. She explains the inseparability of intention (our deepest values, what matters to us) and attention (how we pay attention). They form a virtuous cycle: intention guides attention, and focused attention clarifies and deepens intention.

**Exercise 1: Positive Qualities of Attention**
Tara leads a brief exercise: imagine pausing before meditation to connect with what truly matters. She then asks the audience what qualities of attention arise from this space. Answers include: *curiosity, kindness, receptivity, authenticity, willingness, intimacy, fully allowing, wonder, courage.*

**Exercise 2: Hindering Qualities of Attention**
She contrasts this with another scenario: approaching practice with a busy mind, a to-do list, or a sense of dutifulness. The audience offers qualities like: *impatience, resistance, judging, goal-seeking, planning, restlessness, (sometimes) relief, sleepiness, controlling.*

**Unwise Effort: Striving, Fight, Flight**
Tara identifies common patterns of "unwise effort":
1.  **Striving:** Too much effort, trying to "get somewhere" or achieve a specific state. She shares the "nomads" cartoon (parents telling kids to stop asking "are we there yet?" because they're nomads).
2.  **Fight (Self-Aversion):** We bring our habitual ways of judging our performance into meditation, leading to a fight against ourselves.
3.  **Flight (Self-Doubt):** Feeling defeated, deflated, discouraged, or resigned ("This will never work for me").

These are egoic patterns that often infuse our approach to meditation.

**Tara's Personal Journey**
She shares her own early experiences in an ashram: while deeply passionate and feeling a calling, she also had a "self-project" of trying to achieve enlightenment in 7 years, leading to striving and self-aversion. When her teachers advised her to "just relax," she turned relaxation into another project. Over 40 years, she learned to be more "chill" and enjoy the practice, which helped her see how we all get hooked by these patterns.

**Guiding Principles for a Fruitful Practice:**

1.  **It's Not Personal:** Difficulties in practice are universal. Tara humorously refers to us all as "leaning Buddhas" (an anecdote about a Buddha statue that wasn't perfectly upright).
2.  **Trust in the Power of Awareness:** Awakening is our true nature; it *is* possible. The Buddha wouldn't teach it if it weren't.
3.  **Tune Your Effort:** Like tuning a musical instrument, find the balance—not too loose, not too tight.
4.  **Regularity with Flexibility (The "Back Door"):** Tara committed to daily practice but allowed flexibility in duration, posture, and style. The rhythm itself is important. Rumi: "Do you make regular visits to yourself?"
5.  **Find Your "Why":** Help students remember their deepest motivation. Writing it down can be helpful.
6.  **Content of Practice:** Start with conscious collecting (an anchor like breath), then widen to include the whole play of life ("present heart"). Tailor practices to individual needs (some need collecting, others loving-kindness, some movement).
7.  **Play Your Edge (Deliberate Practice):** If one is comfortable with peace, gently encourage opening to difficulty, and vice-versa, to keep growing.
8.  **Gratification/Reward:** Practice needs to be rewarding. Initially, it might be stress reduction or clarity. Ultimately, it's the joy of "homecoming" to our true nature.
9.  **Feedback and Community:** Practice in a vacuum leads to habituation. Sharing experiences with others (teachers, peers, sangha, even "telesangha") is crucial. It counters the delusion of being a separate self struggling alone.

**Jack Kornfield's Contribution:**
Jack Kornfield joins, re-emphasizing Tara's points. He notes that sitting isn't easy; we face our own distractions and the "cultural speed and pain." He quotes James Baldwin on why people cling to hate/ignorance (to avoid their own pain) and highlights how we culturally project pain onto "enemies."
Practice, he says, involves expanding our "window of tolerance" for our full humanity. The gift of practice is not only tending to difficulties with compassion but also, as Alice Walker wrote (paraphrasing from "The Color Purple"), noticing the "color purple"—the beauty and dazzle of life. We shouldn't just be loyal to suffering. The "marriage of flesh and spirit" includes both beauty and tears.

**Closing Thoughts:**
Tara speaks to the "love-hate" relationship we have with intimacy—with practice, with others. It brings up fears and protections, but also our deep longing to belong.
She reiterates the importance of remembering *why* we practice. She shares the Zen story:
*Student: "Is there anything I can do to become enlightened?"*
*Master: "No more than you could do to make the sun rise in the east each day."*
*Student: "Then what's the purpose of all this practice?"*
*Master: "So you'll be there for the sunrise."*
Or, "Enlightenment's an accident, practice makes you accident-prone."

She concludes with a quote from Jon O'Donahue: "I would love to live like a river flows, carried by the surprise of its own unfolding." This encapsulates the spirit of a practice that is both dedicated and open to the mystery of life.
