### Outline
### The Universality of Grief and Loss in Human Experience

- Grief is described as a universal experience, with nearly every person encountered in a weekly class setting being in some stage of grieving or holding back from grief.

- Loss takes many forms beyond death, including the loss of childhood due to abuse or neglect, loss of dreams for creative expression, loss of years due to addiction, and the inability to have a child.

- Grief is observed in all social animals and is understood as a natural emotional response that allows individuals to metabolize loss and continue living.

- A story of a troubled widower and a wise woman illustrates how meaning can serve as a bridge to what has been lost, comparing the sensation of feeling water through a branch to feeling a loved one through the heart.

- The wise woman's conclusion that "no one knows how to live or how to die, we only know how to love and how to lose and how to pick up branches of meaning along the way" frames grief as an ongoing human practice rather than a problem to be solved.

### Grief as Soul Work and a Portal to Awakening

- Teaching about grief is described as necessarily rooted in personal experience, as educators must draw from their own encounters with loss to authentically guide others.

- The process of awakening through loss is referred to as "soul work," representing a deeper transformation that occurs when grief is fully engaged rather than avoided.

- Poet Naomi Nye's observation that kindness and sorrow are inseparable deepest things within a person is used to illustrate the connection between loving and grieving.

- Surrendering into reality is presented as the core practice of grief, with reality itself described as the refuge discovered through that surrender.

- Carl Jung's statement "Embrace your grief, for there the soul will grow" is cited to reinforce the idea that grief, when met openly, becomes a source of spiritual development.

### Cultural Attitudes Toward Grief and the Suppression of Loss

- Western culture is characterized as a "quick twitch" culture that encourages the suppression of grief through distraction, busyness, and the avoidance of discomfort.

- Children are often not attuned to the naturalness of death and loss, with death frequently framed as something unnatural or bad rather than an inevitable part of life.

- A subtle layer of judgment exists even within spiritual communities, where aging, illness, and death are unconsciously labeled as bad, interfering with the ability to open to reality.

- Sobonfu Somae of the Dagara tribe in West Africa is cited as an example of a contrasting cultural approach, where grieving is actively encouraged, surrounded by communal support, and considered absolutely necessary.

- Upon moving to the United States, Somae observed that grieving is discouraged and not honored publicly, and that this suppression smothers creativity, joy, and the ability to connect with others, and may even contribute to physical decline.

### Ways People Avoid and Bury Grief

- Staying busy and trying to control circumstances are identified as among the most common strategies people use to avoid confronting grief.

- Attempting to "do grief right" or perform grief well is described as a form of avoidance, illustrated by the example of a dying woman trying to manage her deeper feelings by dying in a socially acceptable way.

- Blame directed at doctors, parents, or others who have caused harm is identified as a significant barrier to grief, as it keeps individuals from entering the healing presence required to process loss.

- Turning against oneself through shame is another major obstacle, illustrated by the example of a woman in her early seventies experiencing shame around cognitive deterioration and fear of losing self-sufficiency.

- A metaphor of a tidal pool is used to describe what happens when grief is avoided, where life becomes stagnant and narrowed as a person builds protective banks to avoid the river of change.

### Consequences of Ungrieved Loss

- Emotionally, suppressed grief manifests as depression, anxiety, anger, and despair, all of which arise when vulnerability is consistently pushed away.

- Ungrieved loss creates distance between people and can generate family secrets that become unbridgeable over time.

- Partners who lose a child and fail to grieve together are described as particularly vulnerable to relational breakdown, as the buried pain fragments the connection between them.

- John O'Donohue's observation that people become so busy managing grief that life becomes boxed into "little cookie cutter days" is used to illustrate the spiritual and existential cost of avoidance.

- David Whyte's poem "The Well of Grief" is cited to convey that those who refuse to descend into grief will never find the source from which deeper meaning and renewal are drawn.

### Turning Toward Grief and the Practice of Opening

- Turning toward grief is described as a practice of mindfulness and compassion that creates a container for listening to and attuning to all waves of pain and vulnerability.

- Francis Weller's concept of becoming "an apprentice to sorrow" is introduced to convey that grief is not a one-time event but an ongoing relationship with loss across the entire landscape of life.

- John O'Donohue's approach of reverence toward loss is described as a way of remaining in relationship with grief that allows for repeated discovery of meaning and connection to a larger refuge of love and awareness.

- Titrating grief is acknowledged as a wise and compassionate approach when loss is crushing, recognizing that stepping away at times and engaging in supportive activities does not constitute avoidance if genuine pauses for connection are also maintained.

- The Tibetan teaching to "meet your edge and soften" is presented as a practical instruction for grief work, encouraging a repeated relaxing into presence and tenderness whenever resistance arises.

### Personal Experience of Grief and the Shift to Formless Awareness

- Andy McGuire describes a period of physical decline and loss of mobility that inspired the writing of "True Refuge," illustrating how the experience of waking up through loss can become a source of creative and spiritual expression.

- A hospital stay marked by total uncertainty about a diagnosis is described as a direct encounter with the inner controller, revealing strategies of self-blame, obsessive problem-solving, and busyness as defenses against vulnerability.

- Softening into the fear and grief present during that hospital experience eventually led to a sense of boundless tenderness and vast presence, described as a shift into formless awareness where there was room for loss to simply be.

- Mary Oliver's poem "Heavy" is shared to illustrate the transformation that occurs when grief is carried rather than put down, moving from a place where laughter is absent to one where it returns unexpectedly.

- The shift described in Oliver's poem from love hitched to a particular person to "a love to which there is no reply" is interpreted as the essential awakening in grief work, where awareness moves from attachment to form toward a formless refuge.

### Communal Grief and the Role of Collective Holding

- Many African cultures hold the belief that the deceased must sail on a river of tears to reach their ancestors, reflecting a deep cultural understanding that grief must be done together rather than in isolation.

- A woman in a spiritual friends group who could not mourn her narcissistic mother found that seeing the caring faces of her group members broke something open in her, allowing her to grieve the mother she had never had.

- Journaling and writing a letter to the mother of her dreams became part of her grief process, naming both the wounds she had carried and the longing for unconditional love and understanding.

- At the close of her sharing, the woman recognized that her group members were collectively channeling the mother she had longed for, marking a shift in her ability to find that loving presence in the world around her.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's account of grieving his mother for a year and then waking to the sense that she was alive in him, present in the moonlight and in the earth beneath his feet, is offered as a powerful example of the transition from grief to formless continuity.

### Practical Guidance for Teachers Holding Grief in Group Settings

- When participants arrive in the midst of great loss, acknowledging that they are in a mysterious space between the worlds is described as a helpful and grounding response for teachers to offer.

- Simple rituals such as lighting a candle, offering a prayer, or leading a brief chant are recommended as ways to hold grief as sacred when it arises in a group setting.

- Pausing the group to collectively breathe and acknowledge the mystery that has entered the room is presented as a way to create shared presence around a participant's grief.

- Physical gestures such as holding someone's hand, placing a hand on their heart, or offering a compassionate gaze are described as meaningful ways for both teachers and group members to convey care.

- Susan Griffin's poem is shared to illustrate the preciousness of the space grief opens, encouraging teachers to treat such moments as sacred opportunities for the entire group to remember something larger than themselves.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to develop their own personal rituals, prayers, or chants that feel authentic to them for holding grief in group settings.

- Francis Weller's book "The Wild Edge of Sorrow" is recommended as a resource for deepening understanding of how to work with sorrow.

- Continued exploration of how to teach the turning toward grief, including the ways grief is buried and the pathways through which it becomes a portal to loving awareness, is identified as an ongoing focus for the teaching community.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Grief as Natural and Universal**
- Grief is a natural emotion arising when something valued is lost
- It is observed in all social animals as a way to metabolize loss and continue living
- It is a depth of vulnerability requiring the capacity to stay with what is difficult
- Western culture conditions people away from the naturalness of death, dying, and loss
- Children are often not attuned to the naturalness of loss and death
**Forms of Loss**
- Death of a loved one, including spouses of long marriages
- Loss of childhood due to sexual abuse, parental mental illness, or neglect
- Loss of dreams for creative expression due to financial or circumstantial constraints
- Loss of years due to addiction
- Loss of the ability to have a child
- Loss of physical capacity and bodily health
- Loss of a marriage or partnership
- Loss of a parent, including complex or painful relationships with narcissistic parents
- Loss of the idealized future one had imagined
**How Grief Is Buried**
- Staying busy and trying to control what can be controlled
- Trying to "be good" or manage grief by doing it right
- Bargaining, as illustrated by the Mullah Nasruddin story
- Blaming others, such as doctors or abusive parents, which blocks healing presence
- Turning on oneself with shame and self-judgment
- Overlaying loss with judgments: aging is bad, sickness is bad, death is bad
- Obsessively trying to figure out and fix what is wrong
- Engaging in small activities to feel relevant and avoid vulnerability
**Consequences of Ungrieved Loss**
- Life becomes narrowed and organized around avoidance
- Emotional consequences: depression, anxiety, anger, despair
- Creates distances and unbridgeable secrets within families
- Can destroy partnerships, as seen when couples lose a child and push grief under
- Life becomes boxed into routine, stagnant days, described as a tidal pool cut off from the river of change
- Smothers creativity, joy, and the ability to connect with others
- May even contribute to physical decline or death
**Turning Toward Grief**
- Described as becoming an "apprentice to sorrow" — a relationship, not a one-time event
- Requires mindfulness and compassion as a container for waves and tsunamis of pain
- Involves continual surrendering into reality and meeting resistance with tenderness
- The practice of meeting one's edge and softening, from Tibetan teaching
- Titrating grief is acknowledged as wise when grief is crushing — stepping away, engaging with supportive people and activities, is not avoidance if there is also genuine turning toward
- The approach of reverence toward loss, as described by John O'Donohue, allows ongoing relationship with loss that wakes us up
**The Formless Dimension and Meaning**
- The branch-in-water metaphor: feeling the water through the branch, feeling a loved one in the heart without being able to touch them — this is how meaning saves us
- The shift from love hitched to a particular person to love with no particular form it is attached to
- Thich Nhat Hanh's experience of sensing his mother alive in him, in the moonlight, in the earth underfoot — the idea of loss no longer existing
- Mary Oliver's experience of her partner's death as a box full of darkness that was also a gift
- The formless refuge of love and awareness as what we are ultimately longing for
**Teaching Grief**
- Teachers teach from their own experience of loss
- Andy McGuire did not give a talk on death and dying until after his father died in June 2003
- Teaching involves naming the ways grief is buried and shining a light on consequences
- Teaching also involves exploring how to turn toward grief and how it becomes a portal to loving awareness
- The poet Naomi Nye's insight that kindness and sorrow are inseparable is used as a teaching frame
**Community and Shared Grieving**
- Many African cultures believe the deceased must sail on a river of tears to reach ancestors — grief must be done together
- The Dagara tribe, as described by Sobonfu Somae, actively encourages and surrounds grieving with family support
- Spiritual friends groups (Kalyanamitta groups in the Buddhist tradition) provide a container for shared grief work
- The story of the woman who could not mourn her narcissistic mother illustrates how community witnessing can break open grief
- Journaling and writing letters to the idealized lost person or relationship as part of grief work
- The shift from longing for a specific person to finding that quality of love present in the community around oneself
**Ritual in Teaching Settings**
- Jack Kornfield recommends acknowledging that grieving people are in a mysterious space between the worlds
- Simple rituals: lighting a candle, taking a breath, pausing together
- Offering a simple prayer or chant to hold grief in a sacred way
- Holding a person's hand, placing a hand on their heart, gazing with compassion
- Having the room pause and offer care collectively

### Stories
**The Widower and the Old Wise Woman**
Shared by Andy McGuire. A troubled widower seeks out an old wise woman. She walks with him along a stream, sees his pain, and invites him to sit on a large stone. She swirls a branch in the water and tells him he can feel the water through the branch without putting his hand in — this is what meaning feels like. She then asks him to close his eyes and feel his wife, now gone, in his heart. He begins to cry. She holds him and tells him no one knows how to live or die, only how to love, lose, and pick up branches of meaning along the way.
**The Man Who Lost His Wife of 50 Years**
Shared by Andy McGuire, referring to a man from his Wednesday night class. The man had lost his wife of 50 years some years earlier and described being in a mysterious twilight where everything felt absolutely new and different. He was both grieving and holding back from grief.
**The Four-Year-Old Boy and the Dead Seagull**
Shared by Andy McGuire, attributed to teacher Michelle McDonald. A four-year-old boy walking on the beach with his father sees a dead seagull. His father tells him the bird died and went to heaven. The boy asks, "Did God throw him back?" The story illustrates how children can be naturally open to the reality of death when not shielded from it.
**The Priest, Minister, and Rabbi Joke**
Shared by Andy McGuire. A Catholic priest, a Protestant minister, and a rabbi discuss what they would most hope people say at their open-casket funeral. The priest wants to be called righteous and generous. The minister wants to be called kind and good to his parishioners. The rabbi says he would hope someone says, "Look, he's moving." Used to illustrate the human resistance to death.
**Mullah Nasruddin and the Lost Bracelet**
Shared by Andy McGuire. Mullah Nasruddin loses his wife's bracelet and frantically bargains with God, promising to donate half his salary and devote himself fully. When he finds the bracelet behind a cushion, he immediately tells God never mind. Used to illustrate the bargaining mind as a way of avoiding grief.
**Andy McGuire's Week in the Cardiac Unit**
Shared by Andy McGuire about his own experience. During a period of serious physical decline and lost mobility, he was admitted to a cardiac unit for about a week with symptoms so severe doctors did not know what was wrong. He observed his inner controller at work — blaming himself, obsessively trying to fix things, staying busy with small activities. Eventually he began practicing more deeply, using the Tibetan teaching to meet his edge and soften. He opened to enormous fear and then a gaping hole of grief, fearing that opening to it would mean dying. He continued softening until there was a shift to a boundless, vast tenderness in which there was room for life to pass.
**Andy McGuire's Experience Writing True Refuge**
Shared by Andy McGuire about himself. He began writing True Refuge while going through a process of loss and grief around his body. He had lost mobility and had been on a downhill spiral long enough that he had no certainty he could have his life back as he had known it. The experience of waking up through losing inspired him to write the book.
**Andy McGuire's Father's Death and First Talk on Death and Dying**
Shared by Andy McGuire about himself. He did not give a talk on death and dying until June 2003, a couple of weeks after his father died. He had been superstitious that working on such a talk would cause something bad to happen. Since his father's death, he has given many talks and inquiries on the subject as losses have mounted.
**Sobonfu Somae and the Dagara Tribe**
Shared by Andy McGuire, referring to Sobonfu Somae, a teacher of African spirituality from the Dagara tribe in West Africa. She describes growing up in a culture where grieving is seen as absolutely necessary and is actively encouraged, with family surrounding the grieving person. When she moved to the United States, she found a world where grieving is discouraged, not honored in public, and pushed under — which she said smothers creativity, joy, and connection and may even kill.
**The Woman Who Could Not Mourn Her Narcissistic Mother**
Shared by Andy McGuire, referring to a student in a spiritual friends group. The woman had just lost her mother, with whom she had a very complex and painful relationship. Her mother had been narcissistic and never showed interest or care in who her daughter was. The woman felt cold, dissociated, and still angry. She said she was embarrassed because her heart felt so closed. When she looked up and saw the faces of her group members full of care and understanding, something broke open in her. She said she would never have the mother she wanted. Her grieving came — grief for never having had the mother she longed for. In the two weeks following, she journaled and wrote a letter to the mother of her dreams, naming her wounds and her longings. When she shared this with her group, many had tears. At the end she recognized that her group members were channeling the mother she had longed for, and this marked a shift in her life toward finding that loving presence around her.
