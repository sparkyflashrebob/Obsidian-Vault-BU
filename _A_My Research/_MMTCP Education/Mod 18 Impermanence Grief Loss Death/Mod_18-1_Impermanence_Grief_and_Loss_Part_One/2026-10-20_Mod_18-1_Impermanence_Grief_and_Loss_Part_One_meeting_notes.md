### Mod 18-1 Impermanence, Grief and Loss — Part One
📅 October 20, 2026
👥 Main Teachers: Jack Kornfield, Tara Brach

### Key Points, Subjects and Themes
### Outline
### Impermanence as a Fundamental Truth of Human Experience

- Ancient texts are quoted to establish that humans habitually assume permanence, settlement, and continuity, even though none of these are guaranteed.

- Participants are invited to reflect on how many of them have been affected by significant changes in the world or in their personal lives, with nearly all hands raised in acknowledgment.

- Life is described through the classical pairing of opposites — joy and sorrow, praise and blame, gain and loss, pleasure and pain — as a way of framing the universality of change.

- Master Suzuki Roshi's teaching is introduced, stating that finding composure within the fact that everything changes is the true fruition of spiritual practice.

- A poem by Mark Nepo is read aloud, expressing the simultaneous coexistence of beauty and grief as the natural condition of the human heart.

- Experience itself is compared to bubbles on the Ganges River, appearing briefly and dissolving without lasting substance, as described in ancient Buddhist texts.

- Sensations, feelings, and thoughts are each identified as arising and passing away in direct mindfulness practice, illustrating impermanence at the most immediate level of awareness.

### Teaching Impermanence Through Framing and Normalization

- A story is shared about a person whose family fell into conflict over an inheritance following the death of a patriarch, illustrating how change disrupts perceived stability.

- Rather than offering only practical solutions, the role of the teacher is described as helping people reframe difficult change as a universal human experience rather than a personal failing.

- Normalizing language is offered as a tool, such as pointing out that hundreds of thousands of other families are simultaneously going through the same transitions, including moving elderly parents into care facilities or navigating inheritance disputes.

- Bearing witness to another person's experience is presented as a form of presence that validates and normalizes change without minimizing its difficulty.

- Poetic imagery from ancient texts — a star at dawn, a flash of lightning, an echo, a rainbow — is used to illustrate the evanescent quality of life and to offer a language for impermanence.

- Ajahn Chah's teaching using a beautiful cup is recounted, in which he declared the cup already broken so that he could enjoy it fully and release it without grief when it eventually broke.

### Grief, Tenderness, and Being Present with Loss

- A story is told about Frank Ostaseski, founder of a hospice center, who was called to the home of a family whose seven-year-old son had just died from cancer.

- Upon arriving, Ostaseski observed that the parents had not touched their child in the hour since his death because they could not bear the emotional weight of doing so.

- A ritual of bathing the child's body with lavender water and herbs was introduced as a way of gently guiding the parents into presence with their grief.

- Through this process, the mother began to touch her son's feet and recall memories, moving slowly through unbearable sorrow with tenderness rather than avoidance.

- The role of the teacher or guide is described as sitting with people in their grief and helping them remain present for what is true, with profound gentleness.

- Mary Oliver's poem is cited, calling on people to love what is mortal, to hold it fully, and to release it when the time comes.

### The Illusion of Security and the Wisdom of Insecurity

- Society's widespread investment in security — cyber, financial, physical, and personal — is identified as a cultural response to the discomfort of impermanence.

- Helen Keller's observation is quoted, stating that security is mostly a superstition that does not exist in nature and that life is either a daring adventure or nothing.

- A story is shared from a men's retreat in which a participant said he did not feel safe, and the facilitator responded honestly that he was not, offering a liberating truth rather than false reassurance.

- The point is made that safety and security cannot be found in external circumstances and must instead be cultivated as an inner quality.

- Suzuki Roshi's three-word summary of Buddhist teaching — "not always so" — is presented as a profound and simple expression of the wisdom of impermanence.

- Ajahn Chah's repeated use of the phrase "it's uncertain, isn't it" is described as a teaching device used to answer questions about enlightenment, practice, and the future.

- The Korean Zen master Dae Seung-san's concept of "don't know mind" is introduced as a parallel teaching, framing uncertainty as an invitation to openness and mystery rather than anxiety.

### Relaxation, Trust, and Resilience as Teachable Skills

- The first practical teaching offered to people navigating change is to relax and recognize that it is possible to rest within change rather than resist it.

- A personal memory of learning to float in a swimming pool is used as a metaphor for discovering that one can be held by life without needing to struggle against it.

- Students and clients are described as needing to learn that awareness itself has the capacity to hold experience, to let change move through rather than be fought against.

- The biological process of a caterpillar dissolving into liquid before becoming a butterfly is used as a metaphor for transformation through apparent dissolution.

- "Imaginal cells," the biological term for the cells that carry the blueprint for the butterfly within the dissolving caterpillar, are introduced as a symbol of the innate human capacity for renewal.

- Ancestral resilience is invoked as a source of inner strength, noting that every person alive is descended from thousands of generations who survived extreme hardship, displacement, and loss.

- A story is recounted about a woman named Rosa Garcia who stood alone with a hose and a broom to defend her home against a forest fire, saving it through ordinary courage she did not know she possessed.

- This story is offered not as an example of heroism but as evidence that ordinary people carry remarkable capacities that emerge in moments of crisis, which teachers can help their students recognize in themselves.

### The Evolutionary Resistance to Change and the Path Toward Openness

- Tara Brach observes that from an evolutionary perspective, resistance to change and impermanence is entirely natural and deeply conditioned in the body and mind.

- Before guiding people toward openness, the teaching must begin with compassion for the ways in which resistance arises, honoring it rather than bypassing it.

- Abraham Maslow's reflection following a near-fatal heart attack is quoted, in which he described how the confrontation with death made everything appear more precious, sacred, and beautiful, deepening his capacity for love.

- The shift from habitual resistance to becoming the open space in which experience unfolds is described as the revolutionary transformation that mindfulness and compassion practice makes possible.

- Hafez's poem "Deepening the Wonder" is read, describing death as a favor that clarifies the miraculous and impermanent nature of existence and invites a deeper appreciation of life.

- The poem frames human existence as a brief arc of golden wine between a pitcher and a cup, emphasizing the beauty and brevity of form as a source of illumination rather than despair.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to continue developing their capacity to hold students with tenderness and presence in the face of significant life changes.

- The practice of expanding the window of tolerance through mindfulness and compassion is identified as a central skill to cultivate and transmit.

- Communicating trust, resilience, and the normalcy of change is established as a core responsibility of teachers working with people in transition.

- Guiding students from resistance toward relaxation and openness within change is affirmed as the ongoing direction of the teaching work.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Impermanence as a Core Teaching**
- Jack Kornfield opened with a verse from an ancient text: people mistakenly believe themselves to be permanent, settled, and lasting — none of which is true

**The Window of Tolerance**
- Mindfulness builds the capacity to be with a wider range of experience
- Neuroscientists refer to this as expanding the "window of tolerance"
- The first dimension of mindfulness practice is increasing the ability to be present with both joys and sorrows
**Grief and Tenderness**
- Mark Nepo's poem is used to illustrate the coexistence of beauty and sadness as the heart's natural "duet of wonder and grief"
- Frank Ostaseski's story of sitting with a family whose seven-year-old son had died illustrates the role of tender presence

- Frank guided them through bathing the child's body with lavender water, allowing them to be present with their grief incrementally
- Mary Oliver's teaching: to love what is mortal, hold it close, and when the time comes, let it go
**Normalizing Change**
- Teachers are encouraged to help people not take change personally by contextualizing it
- Example: 126,000 families per week move elderly parents into care facilities — no one is alone in these transitions
- Half of families with significant wealth go through inheritance conflicts
- The role of the teacher is to say "yes, this is life" and bear witness
**The Wisdom of Insecurity**
- Ajahn Chah's repeated teaching: "It's uncertain, isn't it?" — used as a response to nearly all questions
- Korean Zen master Dae Seung-san taught "don't know mind" — applied to questions about love, consciousness, identity, the universe
- Suzuki Roshi's three-word summary of Buddhism: "Not always so"
- Helen Keller: security is mostly a superstition; it does not exist in nature

**Relaxation and Trust**
- The swimming lesson metaphor: learning to float by relaxing rather than flailing
- People can be taught to rest in change, to let it move through them, to be the space within which life renews itself
- Loving awareness, compassion, and tenderness are the qualities that allow this resting
- Trust is communicated through the teacher's presence and the teaching itself
**Ancestral Resilience**
- A thousand generations of ancestors survived storms, famines, deserts, and oceans
- Jack Kornfield's own great-grandparents fled pogroms in Russia, Ukraine, and Turkey as teenagers in the 1880s–1890s, never to return
- His daughter works as an asylum attorney with refugees, continuing this lineage of witnessing displacement
- All people carry within them the courage and resilience of those who came before
**Imaginal Cells and Renewal**
- In the cocoon, the caterpillar does not grow wings — it dissolves into a fertile liquid and reorganizes
- Biologists call the cells that hold the blueprint for the butterfly "imaginal cells"
- These cells know what the organism can become and invite other cells to join the transformation
- This is offered as a metaphor for the built-in capacity of life to renew itself

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Spiritual Insights**
- The ancient Buddhist teaching of impermanence is not merely philosophical — it is a lived practice of finding composure within constant change
- Ajahn Chah's "It's uncertain, isn't it?" and Dae Seung-san's "don't know mind" both point to the same liberating openness — resting in mystery rather than demanding certainty
- Suzuki Roshi's broken cup teaching: by accepting that the cup is already broken, one can enjoy it fully and release it without devastation when it breaks
- The caterpillar-to-butterfly metaphor suggests that dissolution is not destruction but transformation — a spiritual truth applicable to grief, loss, and major life transitions
- Karlfried Graf Dürckheim's teaching: only by exposing oneself to annihilation can that which is indestructible be found within — this is where dignity and true awakening arise
- Dina Metzger's poem points to the alchemical spiritual act of transforming what is broken into light
- Hafez frames death as a favor that the mind, having lost its balance, cannot receive — the illumined ones laugh and sing at impermanence
- Maslow's post-heart-attack reflection suggests that proximity to death opens the heart to love and sacred perception
**Societal Insights**
- Western culture actively promotes the illusion of permanence through security systems, insurance, and anti-aging products — all of which contradict the reality of impermanence
- The NSA losing its own security tools is cited as an example of the futility of absolute security
- Society's message — that change can be insured against or prevented — is described as a trick that teachers must help people see through
- The story of Rosa Garcia standing her ground against a forest fire with only a hose and a broom illustrates that ordinary people carry extraordinary capacity they do not know about until it is needed
- Teachers and guides serve a countercultural function: offering the medicine of impermanence and resilience in a society that sells permanence and safety
- Broken hearts, divorce, business loss, and family conflict are near-universal experiences — the teacher's role is to normalize these as part of the human journey, not personal failures

### Stories
**The Broken Cup — Jack Kornfield**
Ajahn Chah held up a beautiful antique Chinese cup during a visit from a group that included Ram Dass, Mark Epstein, and Joseph Goldstein. He declared the cup already broken, explaining that this perspective allowed him to enjoy it fully and release it without grief when it inevitably broke.
**The Family Inheritance Conflict — Jack Kornfield**
A person came to Jack describing a family torn apart by conflict over money following the death of a grandfather who had founded a large company. Jack used this as an example of how teachers can normalize such experiences rather than only offering practical solutions, noting that half of families with significant wealth go through similar struggles.
**Moving Elderly Parents — Jack Kornfield**
Jack described the common experience of adult children having to move elderly parents from their family home into a care facility, noting that approximately 126,000 families go through this transition each week, as a way of illustrating how normalizing change reduces isolation.
**Michelle McDonald's Classroom — Jack Kornfield**
Meditation teacher Michelle McDonald, prior to teaching retreats, taught young children. She had her class of five-year-olds collect dead things from the woods — leaves, sticks, beetles — to discuss death. One five-year-old boy observed that death was a good thing because without it there would be more and more stuff and no room for anyone.
**Frank Ostaseski and the Seven-Year-Old Boy — Jack Kornfield**
Frank Ostaseski, founder of the Zen Hospice Project, was called to the home of a family whose seven-year-old son had just died of cancer. The parents had not touched their son in the hour since his death. Frank kissed the boy on the forehead, then sat with the family and guided them through bathing the child's body with lavender water and herbs. As they did so, the mother began to speak memories aloud — recalling how small his toes once were — pausing and breathing through the grief incrementally.
**Jack Kornfield Learning to Swim — Jack Kornfield**
At age seven, Jack was taken to a cold swimming pool and struggled to learn to swim. The instructor placed him on his back and then removed his hands. Jack discovered he could float. He uses this as a metaphor for learning to rest in change.
**Jack Kornfield's Great-Grandparents — Jack Kornfield**
Jack's great-grandparents, Eastern European and Mediterranean Jews, fled pogroms and persecution in Russia, Ukraine, and Turkey, arriving in the United States as teenagers in the 1880s and 1890s. They knew they would never see their homes, families, or grandparents again. Jack knew three of his great-grandparents into his twenties.
**Rosa Garcia and the Forest Fire — Jack Kornfield**
A journalist covering fires in Sarasota, Florida encountered Mrs. Rosa Garcia, who refused to evacuate her home during a forest fire sweeping across several hundred acres. Alone, with only a garden hose and a kitchen broom, she sprayed her house and roof with water. The fire crossed onto her property and then jumped her house because she had hosed it down. Her home was saved. Jack uses her story to illustrate that ordinary people carry remarkable strength they do not know about until it is needed.
**The Men's Retreat — Jack Kornfield**
Jack led a men's retreat with colleagues Robert Hall, Tiju, and Wes Nisker. During an evening council, a man said he did not feel safe. Robert Hall responded directly: "You're not." He then explained that safety is not the point of human interaction — it must be found within oneself. Jack describes this as a wake-up moment for the man.
**Tara Brach and the Face Cream — Tara Brach**
Standing in front of her bathroom mirror before the session, Tara noticed a face cream labeled "firm and hold," "death defying," and "age defying," made from rose stem cell therapy. She observed her own mind's quick movement from attraction to the product's promise to the recognition that it had not helped the roses. She used this as a personal illustration of how naturally and quickly the mind grasps at the promise of defying change.
**Abraham Maslow's Heart Attack — Tara Brach**
Tara cited Abraham Maslow's reflection written after a near-fatal heart attack, in which he described how the confrontation with death made everything appear precious, sacred, and beautiful, and made passionate love more possible.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- The parents of the seven-year-old boy who died were unable to touch their son's body in the hour following his death due to the emotional overwhelm they feared it would unleash — illustrating how grief can manifest as physical and emotional paralysis
- Jack Kornfield references the tendency of meditators to cling to states of peace or bliss once experienced, comparing it to holding one's breath — a form of psychological grasping that creates "rope burn" as experience inevitably changes
- Tara Brach noted her own mind's immediate grasping response upon seeing the "age-defying" face cream, using it as a live example of the conditioned resistance to impermanence that practitioners must meet with compassion
- Tara emphasized that resistance to impermanence is evolutionarily conditioned in the body and mind — it is natural and should be met with compassion before any opening is invited in teaching
- The man at the men's retreat who said "I don't feel safe" is cited as a common presentation — the psychological need for safety as a barrier to genuine presence and risk-taking in human interaction
- Dina Metzger's mastectomy and her iconic photograph with a dragon tattoo across her chest are referenced as an example of meeting physical loss and bodily change with defiance and creative transformation rather than shame or avoidance
