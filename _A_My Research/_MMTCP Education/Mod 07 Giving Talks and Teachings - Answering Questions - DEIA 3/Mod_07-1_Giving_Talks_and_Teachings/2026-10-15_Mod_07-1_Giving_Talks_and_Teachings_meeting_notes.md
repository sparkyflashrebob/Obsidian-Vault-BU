# Mod 07-1 Giving Talks and Teachings - Oct, 15

### Giving Talks and Teachings
📅 October 15, 2026
👥 Jack Kornfield, Tara Brach
Tags: Dharma teachings, public speaking, mindfulness, Buddhism, diversity and inclusion, spiritual practice, meditation, imposter syndrome, multicultural sensitivity, compassion, selfing, liberation
### Outline
### The Nature and Tradition of Offering Dharma Teachings

- Described in ancient Buddhist texts is the image of a weary traveler who finds a cool pond to cleanse, drink from, and become refreshed, which serves as a metaphor for the role of teachings of liberation.

- Offering teachings is framed as a tradition of providing spiritual nourishment to those who are tired and burdened by the difficulties of life.

- Nervousness when beginning to teach is acknowledged as a natural part of human wiring and something that diminishes over time with experience and exposure to progressively larger groups.

- Tossing one's hat over an orchard wall, as described by Irish author Frank O'Connor, is used as a metaphor for committing to the act of teaching even when the challenge feels too great, and then having no choice but to follow through.

### The Diversity of Teaching Styles and Individual Gifts

- Illustrated in an ancient text is a scene where the Buddha acknowledges that different disciples attract different followers based on their individual strengths, including ethics, wisdom, meditative power, and loving devotion.

- Each teacher is understood to have their own gifts, temperament, and natural style, and authenticity in expressing those qualities is considered essential.

- Recognized styles of teaching include prepared talks, spontaneous delivery, intellectual discourse with philosophical references, poetic expression, down-home storytelling, and scientific or naturalist approaches.

- Personal and revealing teaching can be a genuine gift to listeners, provided the teacher has sufficiently processed or framed their experience as practice rather than simply expressing unresolved distress.

- Diversity in teaching content and approach is emphasized as important because audiences carry unknown backgrounds, traumas, and identities related to class, race, caste, and sexual orientation.

### Practical Guidance on Structuring and Delivering Talks

- Keeping talks short is strongly recommended, with fifteen minutes cited as a good length, as attention tends to wane beyond that point.

- Chapters from well-known Dharma teachers such as Pema Chodron and Chogyam Trungpa are noted as examples of effective brevity in spiritual teaching.

- A journalistic structure is suggested, in which the speaker tells the audience what they will cover, delivers the content, and then summarizes what was shared, as repetition from different angles supports learning.

- Creating a simple outline is offered as a helpful tool, with numbered Buddhist lists such as the Four Noble Truths, the Five Hindrances, and the Eightfold Path serving as ready-made frameworks.

- Thematic approaches are also available, including choosing a problem such as stress or conflict, analyzing it, and offering a solution, or selecting an inspiring passage and unfolding its meaning for the audience.

- Inviting the group to share what they have learned, rather than simply asking for questions, allows the collective wisdom of the room to emerge through stories, poems, ideas, and personal reflections.

- Building a personal folder of poems, stories, scientific texts, and artistic material is encouraged as a resource for enriching talks over time.

### Overcoming the Ego and the Fear of Public Speaking

- Mark Twain's observation that there are only two types of speakers, those who get nervous and those who are liars, is cited to normalize anxiety in teaching.

- Western Dharma culture has increasingly treated teaching as a performance, which has intensified the tendency toward ego-identification and self-evaluation in the process.

- Tara Brach describes her own early experience of anxiety before giving a first talk, including the mental swings between imagining humiliation and imagining a standing ovation, as an illustration of the inflation and deflation that accompanies the ego's involvement.

- Sharing insecurities with fellow new teachers is identified as a practical and effective way to reduce the personal weight of self-doubt.

- Pausing before or during a talk to reconnect with the deep intention of waking up together, and remembering that teacher and audience are genuinely in it together, is described as the most effective antidote to the sense of a self that is performing.

- Jack Kornfield describes a personal reframe he uses when nervous, which is to remember that he is not teaching himself but rather transmitting teachings of liberation that have been offered as a gift for thousands of years, making him a conduit rather than the source.

### Cultivating Teaching Capacity Through Modeling and Inner Sourcing

- Modeling other teachers by listening carefully to their rhythm, pacing, use of voice, choice of illustrations, and authenticity is identified as a natural and encouraged way to develop one's own teaching voice.

- Sourcing from within is equally important, and involves asking oneself what about a given topic most matters personally and how it has shaped one's own life, work, relationships, and understanding.

- Grounding the audience in present-moment experience through brief reflections, such as asking them to recall a moment of judgment or consider how much of their day was spent lost in thought, helps listeners find themselves in the material.

- Anticipating questions and doubts that listeners might have, and addressing them proactively within the talk, adds depth and helps people engage more fully with the content.

### Multicultural Sensitivity and Inclusion in Dharma Teaching

- Drawing from a wide variety of sources across race, religion, and cultural background is strongly encouraged, with particular emphasis on authors and voices from non-dominant cultures, immersed in deeply rather than tokenistically included.

- Filtering stories and examples for relevance to a multicultural audience is important, and examples that assume shared economic privilege or dominant cultural experience should be reconsidered.

- When current events highlight the suffering caused by racism or other forms of oppression, including that reality in teachings is described as a deeply respectful act that honors the lived experience of those present.

- A written reflection from a woman of color co-teacher is shared, expressing the pain of silence among white Dharma community members following the Ferguson events and calling for collective hearts and wisdom to engage with the struggle against racism.

- Practitioners of color frequently do not hear Dharma stories that reflect their own experiences, do not receive guidance on identity formation in relation to practice, and do not encounter interpretations of the Dharma that reflect non-Euro-American values, which creates a serious obstacle for beginning practitioners.

- A common unconscious error is naming a person of color in a story while never identifying a comparable character as white, which implicitly centers whiteness as the unmarked default and should be actively corrected.

- Distinguishing between the universal nature of suffering and the particular call at this moment in history to raise sensitivity to the suffering caused by specific biases is framed not as a hierarchy of pain but as a responsibility of those in dominant populations.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to experiment with and develop their own individual teaching styles, drawing on the frameworks, outlines, and thematic lists provided in the course handouts.

- Ongoing attention to multicultural sensitivity in the selection of stories, examples, and sources is identified as a continuous practice to be deepened over time.

- Reconnecting with personal intention before and during talks, and cultivating the orientation of teaching as if it were the last talk of one's life, are adopted as guiding reflective practices going forward.

**Anticipating Questions**

- Tara suggested approaching each talk as if it were the last talk of one's life, not holding back on depth
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Teacher as a Vehicle for Liberation**
- The image of the cool pond from ancient Buddhist texts frames the teacher not as a performer but as a source of refreshment and liberation for those weary from the journey of life
- Jack Kornfield articulated that when nervousness arises, shifting attention from oneself to the teachings of liberation transforms the experience — the teacher becomes a conduit for something offered across thousands of years
**Dignity and Caste: The Story of Ananda and Pakati**
- The story of Ananda asking an untouchable girl for water and her subsequent encounter with the Buddha carries a direct teaching on fundamental human dignity
- The Buddha's response to Pakati — that it is not Ananda she loves but his kindness, and that she should practice that kindness for others — presents kindness as a force that transcends social hierarchy
- The teaching that a person born into the lowest caste can outshine the highest through the quality of their heart is presented as a reminder of the inherent dignity of every being
**Selfing and the Ego in Teaching**
- Tara Brach identified "selfing" — the habitual identification with a self that needs to perform well and fears failure — as the central obstacle to authentic teaching in the Western context
- Western Dharma culture is described as having aggravated this tendency, making teaching more of a performance than a transmission
- The antidote offered is remembering shared intention and the sense of being in the journey together with listeners
**Separation as the Root of Suffering**
- Most Dharma teachings are described as ultimately addressing the ways we create separation — the illusion of being a separate bubble — and the sense that others are truly other
- Racism and oppression are framed as expressions of this core delusion of separation, making their acknowledgment in Dharma talks not a political act but a spiritual one
- Making the invisible visible — acknowledging the suffering of those from non-dominant cultures — is presented as a direct expression of the teachings on interconnection
**Hierarchy of Pain and the Call to Sensitivity**
- The Dharma teaching that there is no hierarchy of pain is acknowledged, while simultaneously the call is made to raise sensitivity to particular forms of suffering — racism, marginalization of LGBTQ+ people, and other forms of oppression — that those in dominant populations can easily overlook
- This is framed not as diminishing other forms of suffering but as a specific responsibility of this historical moment
**Beginner's Mind and Presence**
- Shunryu Suzuki's teaching through Katagiri Roshi — "Today is today. The day is not yesterday. Today is not tomorrow." — is presented as a complete teaching on presence and beginner's mind, delivered in six words
- The immediacy of practice and what it means to be present exactly where one is are offered as foundational to both teaching and living
**The Universe as Friendly or Unfriendly**
- A passage attributed to Einstein is shared: if we decide the universe is unfriendly, we use technology and resources to build walls and weapons; if we decide it is friendly, we use them to understand and connect
- This is offered as a passage that can be unfolded in a teaching to explore how our fundamental orientation shapes our relationship to life and to others

### Stories
**The Weary Traveler and the Cool Pond**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, drawn from ancient Buddhist texts. A person who has traveled far, tired, thirsty, and covered with the dust of the road, comes upon a beautiful cool pond, bathes, drinks, and is refreshed. The text uses this as a metaphor for what happens when people are invited into the teachings of liberation.
**Jack Kornfield's First Teaching**
Shared by Jack Kornfield about himself. After about a year as a monk at Ajahn Chah's forest monastery, during a large holiday gathering of 500 to 800 people, Jack was called upon to give his first teaching in the Lao language in the middle of the night. The crowd's amazement that a Western monk with a long nose could speak at all meant the content mattered little. He describes this as his beginning.
**Opening for the Dalai Lama**
Shared by Jack Kornfield about himself. He was invited to be the opening teacher before the Dalai Lama at a large stadium event, describing the experience as deeply nerve-wracking before finding his footing.
**Frank O'Connor and the Orchard Wall**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, attributed to Irish author Frank O'Connor. As a boy, O'Connor and his friends would toss their hats over orchard walls that were too high and too doubtful to climb, leaving themselves no choice but to follow. Used as a metaphor for committing to teaching by throwing yourself into it.
**The Buddha and His Disciples**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, drawn from ancient Buddhist texts. The Buddha sits with his chief disciples and describes how different followers are drawn to different teachers: Upali for ethics, Sariputta for wisdom, Moggallana for Samadhi and psychic powers, and Ananda for love and devotion. Used to illustrate that each teacher has their own gifts and style.
**Ananda and Pakati the Untouchable**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, drawn from Buddhist texts. Ananda, the Buddha's attendant, becomes thirsty on a journey and asks a young girl from the untouchable caste for water. She protests that her touch would contaminate his drink. Ananda replies he asks not for caste but for water. She gives him water and, having heard he is a disciple of the Buddha, follows him and asks to serve Ananda, having fallen in love with his kindness. The Buddha tells her it is not Ananda she loves but his kindness, and instructs her to practice that kindness for others, saying she will outshine the highest caste with her heart.
**Katagiri Roshi's Six-Word Teaching**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. Katagiri Roshi, a young Zen monk who had been invited to assist Shunryu Suzuki at San Francisco Zen Center, was uncertain about teaching in English. Suzuki demonstrated by entering the Dharma hall, ringing the bell, sitting on the teaching seat, and saying: "Today is today. The day is not yesterday. Today is not tomorrow." He then bowed, rang the bell, and left, saying six words was all it took.
**Sumedha's Four-Hour Talk**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. Ajahn Chah instructed a Western monk named Sumedha to give a talk to a gathered crowd. When Sumedha finished, Ajahn Chah told him to keep going. He spoke for four hours. Ajahn Chah's purpose was to cure Sumedha of his fear of boring people by making him do it until he got over it.
**Tara Brach's First Talk**
Shared by Tara Brach about herself. Her first talk was given at Yucca Valley, in English, in a lineup of more experienced teachers. She describes her mind swinging between imagining leaving the retreat in embarrassment and receiving a standing ovation from a group that does not even clap.
**The "Just Okay" White Shark Cartoon**
Shared by Tara Brach. A cartoon depicting a large shark telling a smaller shark that the pressure to be great is too much and that he would rather be known as the "just okay" white shark. Used to illustrate the value of being at peace with how a talk goes.
**Tara Brach Described as "Jack Light"**
Shared by Tara Brach about herself and Jack Kornfield. After giving her first talk on Compassion, with Jack as her teacher trainer, his congratulations was that it sounded like "Jack Light." Used to normalize modeling other teachers
