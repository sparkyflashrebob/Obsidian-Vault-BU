# Mod 07-2 Answering Difficult Questions - Oct, 15

### MMTCP Mod 07 Answering Difficult Questions
📅 October 15, 2026
👥 Jack Kornfield, Tara Brach
Tags: Teaching, Difficult Questions, Mindfulness, Compassion, Inquiry, Bearing Witness, Don't Know Mind, Heart-to-Heart Connection, Spiritual Practice, Group Facilitation
### Outline
### The Value of Pausing and Acknowledging Questions

- Taking a breath after hearing a question allows it to be held in the space of the room without the need to immediately respond.

- Appreciating the person asking is important, as questioners are often nervous and even seemingly naive questions contain something meaningful and worth acknowledging.

- Recognizing the seed of importance in a question helps the person feel engaged and signals to the room that inquiry is safe and welcome.

- Questions are framed as a form of inquiry and discovery rather than simply requests for information.

### Naming the Emotional Content of Difficult Questions

- When a question carries intense emotion such as anger, fear, grief, or confusion, naming what is present in the question can be a powerful first response.

- Framing a difficult question as a collective struggle rather than an individual one helps the group recognize shared suffering, for example by saying "this is something we are all wrestling with as a culture."

- Pausing to acknowledge pain or grief before offering any response demonstrates that difficult experiences can be held with loving awareness and mindfulness.

- Naming what has been brought up invites presence with difficulty and models how to meet challenging material with compassion.

### Asking "What Makes You Ask" to Uncover the Real Question

- Responding to abstract or general questions with "what makes you ask" is one of the most important tools available when taking the teacher's seat.

- A general question such as one about karma may in fact be rooted in a deeply personal experience such as the loss of a loved one or a financial crisis, and knowing that context transforms how the question can be answered.

- Hearing the personal circumstance behind a question immediately clarifies whether the person is seeking intellectual understanding or emotional and existential guidance.

- More often than not, it is the heart rather than the mind that is asking, and the response should be directed heart to heart, in what the Sufi tradition calls "sobhapt," an intimate connection from one person to another.

### Speaking to the Heart Rather Than the Mind

- While some questions call for clarification of concepts, such as distinguishing healthy desire from unhealthy attachment, the deeper impulse behind most questions is an emotional and existential one.

- The underlying question beneath most inquiries is how to live in the world and how to make sense of what is happening, which calls for a heart-centered response.

- Teaching from personal experience is emphasized, as teachers are encouraged to share what has genuinely changed, touched, and inspired them rather than attempting to cover everything.

### The Power of Not Knowing and Not Answering

- Admitting not knowing the answer to a question is described as one of the most refreshing things a teacher can offer, and "Don't Know Mind" is a highly regarded state in the Zen tradition.

- Acknowledging the limits of one's knowledge and framing a response as one's own perspective rather than definitive truth is presented as a mark of integrity and mastery.

- Sometimes the most appropriate response to a question is not an answer at all, as illustrated by the story of Carl Jung's grandson asking an entire audience to rise in silence in response to a dream describing unspeakable suffering.

- There exists a category of vulnerability and suffering so extreme that it goes beyond words, and in those moments the role of the teacher is to stand in witness so that no one need bear it alone.

- Bearing witness to the life of another human being is described as a central part of responding to questions, often more important than any verbal answer.

### Framing Responses as Inquiry Rather Than Answers

- Tara Brach frames difficult questions not as problems requiring answers but as invitations into a process of inquiry, where the response keeps attention and exploration alive rather than closing down into a fixed conclusion.

- The concept of "Ehi Pasiko," a Pali phrase attributed to the Buddha meaning "come and see for yourself," is offered as a guiding principle for bringing people back to their own direct experience rather than relying on the teacher's authority.

- Keeping the process of inquiry going is described as more powerful than providing a specific answer, particularly for complex, paradoxical, or emotionally charged questions.

### Working with Anger and Hostility in Questions

- When a question is delivered with anger or hostility, the first step is to pause and become aware of any impulse to defend the teachings or one's own position.

- Behind anger or hostility in a question there is almost always suffering, and that suffering is what is truly asking for attention.

- Responding with deep respect for the person behind the suffering tends to open something useful, shifting the dynamic from confrontation to connection.

- Listening beneath the surface of a charged question for what the person is really asking is presented as an essential skill.

### Drawing on Collective Wisdom and the Questioner's Own Insight

- Checking in with the group about how many people share a particular struggle can immediately transform a personal question into a collective one, reducing isolation and expanding the response beyond what any single teacher could offer.

- Inviting the group to share what they have discovered that helps with a given challenge draws on collective wisdom in a way that can be more powerful than anything the teacher might say alone.

- Asking the questioner how they have worked with the issue so far, or what their own intuitive sense is, calls forth their own wisdom and is described as remarkably effective.

- Calling on the questioner to answer their own question honors their inner knowing and reinforces the principle that the teacher's role is to facilitate discovery rather than deliver conclusions.

### The Quality of Heart as the Foundation of Response

- Public speaking to ask a question involves significant vulnerability and self-consciousness, and this should be held with care by anyone in the teacher's role.

- More than the specific content of any response, it is the quality of heart that the questioner feels in return that determines whether the exchange is truly meaningful.

- Hafez's poem is offered as a closing reflection, pointing to the deep human pull toward connection and the invitation to become someone who consistently meets others with that quality of open, loving presence.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to integrate the practice of pausing before responding into their teaching approach as a foundational habit.

- Using the question "what makes you ask" is identified as a key technique to carry forward when fielding abstract or general questions.

- Cultivating comfort with not knowing and with bearing witness without answering is recognized as an ongoing area of development for those moving into teaching roles.

- Drawing on collective wisdom and inviting questioners to access their own insight are highlighted as practices to be actively applied in group settings.

### Detailed Subject Matter
- **Pausing Before Responding**
  - Take a breath after hearing a question before responding
  - Allow the question to be held in the space of the room
  - Everyone in the room is also digesting the question
  - There is no hurry to respond immediately
- **Appreciating the Questioner**
  - People are often nervous when asking questions
  - Even naive questions contain a seed of something important
  - Acknowledging the value in a question makes the person feel engaged
  - Creates a safe environment for inquiry in the room
- **Naming the Emotional Content**
  - When a question carries emotion such as anger, fear, confusion, or grief, name it
  - Examples of naming: "This question has a lot of suffering in it" or "There's a lot of uncertainty in this question"
  - Acknowledge collective struggle, not just the individual's
  - Pause and hold the pain with kindness or compassion before responding
  - Demonstrates that difficult content can be held with loving awareness and mindfulness
- **Using "What Makes You Ask?"**
  - Especially useful for abstract or general questions
  - Helps uncover the real personal context behind the question
  - Example: A question about karma may actually be about a personal loss or tragedy
  - Once the real context is known, the response becomes more targeted and meaningful
- **Speaking to the Heart**
  - More often than clarifying the mind, the response should speak to the heart
  - The heart is what brings people to ask questions in the first place
  - Referenced as "sobhapt" in the Sufi tradition — intimate connection from one person to another
  - Teaching is fundamentally heart to heart
- **Don't Know Mind**
  - It is acceptable and even refreshing for a teacher to say they do not know the answer
  - Don't Know Mind is highly regarded in the Zen tradition
  - Teachers teach what they know, what changed them, what touched and inspired them
  - Naming the limits of one's knowledge and identifying a response as one's own take is powerful
- **Not Answering a Question**
  - Sometimes the most appropriate response is not to answer at all
  - Bearing witness to another human being's experience is itself a form of response
  - Illustrated through the story of Jung's grandson and the dream about Nazi atrocities
- **Keeping Inquiry as a Process**
  - The concept of "answer" is smaller than the full response that is possible
  - Responses that keep the process of paying attention to experience going are more powerful
  - Referenced the Buddha's words "Ehi Pasiko" — come and see for yourself
  - Bringing people back to checking in with their own experience
- **Drawing on Collective Wisdom**
  - Ask the group how many are struggling with a particular experience
  - Invite the group to share what they have discovered that helps
  - Collective wisdom can be more powerful than anything the teacher might say alone
- **Responding to Hostile or Angry Questions**
  - Pause and be aware of what arises internally, including any impulse to defend the teachings
  - Behind anger or hostility there is suffering asking for attention
  - Responding with deep respect for who the person is behind their suffering allows something useful to unfold
  - Keep listening for what the person is really asking
- **Calling on the Questioner's Own Wisdom**
  - Ask back questions such as "How have you worked with this thus far?" or "What is your own take?"
  - Calling on the questioner's own wisdom is remarkably powerful
  - Their wisdom is already present and can be drawn forth
- **Vulnerability in Asking Questions**
  - Asking a question in a public setting is a form of public speaking
  - There can be tremendous vulnerability and self-consciousness involved
  - The quality of heart felt in the response often matters more than the specific content
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
- **Bearing Witness as a Spiritual Act**
  - Some suffering is so extreme and unspeakable that it goes beyond words
  - In the face of such suffering, standing together in witness so no one bears it alone is the most complete response
  - The role of the teacher is not only to answer questions but to bear witness to the life of another human being
- **Collective Suffering and Shared Humanity**
  - Difficult questions about racism, abortion, war, conflict, and addiction reflect collective cultural struggles
  - Naming that a question belongs to everyone, not just the individual, creates shared presence
  - Inviting the group to recognize shared experience transforms individual pain into communal holding
- **Heart-to-Heart Connection as Teaching**
  - The Sufi concept of "sobhapt" describes the intimate connection between teacher and student
  - Teaching is fundamentally about transmitting what changed and inspired the teacher
  - The quality of heart in response is often more impactful than the words spoken
- **"Ehi Pasiko" — Come and See for Yourself**
  - The Buddha's teaching to return people to their own direct experience
  - Responses that invite self-inquiry are more powerful than prescriptive answers
  - This runs counter to the natural reflex to answer specifically and definitively
- **Don't Know Mind as Spiritual Wisdom**
  - Celebrated in the Zen tradition as a highly regarded state
  - Modeling not-knowing is itself a teaching about the nature of reality and humility
  - Acknowledging uncertainty invites others into honest inquiry rather than false certainty
- **Hafez on the Human Need for Connection**
  - Every person carries a deep pull to connect and to be loved
  - The teacher's role is to embody the quality of presence that meets that need
  - Becoming one who speaks "that sweet moon language what every other eye in this world is dying to hear"

### Stories
- **The Jungian Workshop and the Dream of Nazi Atrocities** — Shared by Jack Kornfield
  - A therapist from New York attended a two-day professional workshop based on films of Carl Jung's teachings and those of his partner and pupil Maria von Franz
  - A distinguished panel from great Jungian centers attended, including Carl Jung's own grandson
  - A woman shared a recurring dream in which the dreamer was stripped of all human dignity and worth through Nazi torture and atrocities
  - The therapist in the audience began mentally formulating a dream interpretation, expecting a symbolic analysis to follow
  - Instead, Jung's grandson asked the entire large audience to rise and stand together in a moment of silence in response to the dream
  - After sitting, the audience remained quiet with no discussion following
  - Days later the therapist asked a panel member close to Jung's grandson about the response
  - The panel member said: "There is in life a vulnerability so extreme or suffering so unspeakable that it goes beyond words. In the face of such suffering, all we can do is stand together in witness so no one need bear it alone."
- **The Children's Sermon Joke** — Shared by Tara Brach
  - A priest is presenting a children's sermon before the whole congregation
  - He asks the children who knows what the resurrection was
  - A little boy raises his hand confidently and says that if you have a resurrection that lasts more than four hours, you are supposed to call the doctor
  - Used by Tara Brach to illustrate the value of having fun and lightness in teaching
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Questioners may experience nervousness and vulnerability when asking questions in a public or group setting
- People asking questions from an angry or hostile place are identified as carrying underlying suffering
- Difficult questions may arise from personal experiences of grief, loss, addiction, financial devastation, or trauma
- Teachers may feel an internal impulse to defend the teachings when faced with hostile questions, which is noted as something to be aware of and set aside
- The challenge of responding to questions about deeply charged societal topics such as racism, abortion, war, and addiction is acknowledged as part of the teacher's role
