#### Outline

#### Introduction to Compassion as a Core Practice

- Presented as one of two foundational wings of mindfulness practice, compassion is paired alongside wisdom as an essential pillar of inner development.
- Framed as the natural trembling of the heart in the face of suffering, compassion is described as an innate outpouring response rather than a learned behavior.
- Western societies are identified as having largely neglected the cultivation of compassion, particularly in its self-directed form.
- A teaching attributed to the Buddha is shared, stating that no being in the entire universe is more worthy of love and compassion than oneself.

#### The Importance of Self-Compassion

- Socialization and cultural conditioning are identified as primary forces that train individuals to be harsh, judgmental, and critical toward themselves.
- Patterns of self-criticism accumulate over time and shape a person's identity, self-worth, and habitual responses to difficulty.
- Large and small instances of self-blame are described as equally damaging, with minor incidents such as leaving water running triggering disproportionately cruel internal dialogue.
- The cumulative effect of self-criticism is compared to death by a thousand paper cuts, illustrating how repeated small acts of self-harm erode wellbeing over time.
- Three essential questions posed by journalist Krista Tippett are introduced as compassionate guides for living: what does it mean to be human, how do we want to live, and who will we be to each other.
- Personal suffering is understood to arise not only from individual life experiences but also from ancestral pain and collective societal wounds carried in the body.
- Dominant cultural standards related to race, gender, ability, and sexual orientation are described as hierarchical measures against which people unfairly judge themselves.
- Emotions such as unworthiness, shame, and feeling unlovable are identified as common outcomes of measuring oneself against impossible cultural standards.
- Imposter syndrome is highlighted as a particularly prevalent form of self-doubt, especially among those entering roles such as mindfulness teaching.

#### Self-Compassion as the Foundation for the Program

- Participants are invited to collectively commit to self-compassion as the foundational ground for engaging with the two-year training program.
- Mindfulness is described as the tool that makes self-compassion actionable, because it allows practitioners to notice when self-sabotaging thoughts arise and choose a different response.
- Specific phrases are offered as a self-compassion practice, including "May I be kind to myself," "May I give myself what I need," "May I learn to accept myself just as I am," and "May I forgive myself."
- Participants are reminded that they are not flawed, and that while behavior can change, the starting point must always be self-compassion rather than self-condemnation.
- Self-compassion is described as a path toward freedom, with the capacity to dissolve long-held anger and resentment when genuinely practiced.

#### Reflective Practice on Self-Compassion

- Participants are guided to journal individually on their personal relationship with self-compassion, exploring how easy or difficult it is to access and what tools or conditions support it.
- Shared responses from participants reveal that self-compassion becomes more accessible through mindfulness tools such as placing a hand on the heart, Metta practice, and affirming phrases like "you are fine just the way you are."
- One participant's reflection notes that including self-compassion in one's circle of awareness produces an immediate sense of being held, released, and free.
- Another response highlights the importance of recognizing shared humanity as a way of reducing self-judgment and avoiding the trap of judging one's own judgment.

#### Widening the Circle of Compassion Toward Others

- The Buddhist pairing of Sati and Sampajanya is introduced to explain that mindfulness encompasses both mindful presence and mindful response, not stillness alone.
- A Zen teaching is cited to illustrate that practice involves two complementary actions: quieting the mind and tending the heart, and then going out to tend the world.
- A systematic pedagogy of loving-kindness is described, beginning with oneself or those easiest to love, then expanding gradually to neutral people, and eventually to those who are difficult.
- An account of the Dalai Lama's visit to the Insight Meditation Society illustrates how genuine compassion manifests in small, immediate, particular acts of noticing and responding to another's suffering.
- The vows of Shantideva are referenced, expressing the aspiration to be a bridge, a boat, a lamp, and medicine for those who suffer.
- William Blake's principle that good must be done in the minute particulars is cited to ground compassion in concrete daily action rather than abstract ideals.
- A Korean Zen teaching is offered as a simple expression of compassionate action: when someone is hungry in front of you, feed them; when someone is cold, cover them.
- Teachers of mindfulness are described as holders of a tender field of care, capable of meeting students wherever they are and inviting them into their own capacity for compassion.

#### Compassion Meditation Practice

- Participants are guided into a meditation beginning with self-compassion, settling into the present moment and holding whatever arises, including tiredness or distraction, with gentleness.
- Attention is then directed toward a loved one, inviting practitioners to visualize that person and feel the natural, uncomplicated affection and connection present in the relationship.
- Awareness is expanded to include the loved one's full humanity, including their struggles, sorrows, fears, and difficulties, allowing love to naturally transform into compassion.
- Compassionate phrases are offered for extending care to the loved one, including "May you hold yourself in compassion," "May your sorrows and difficulties be eased," and "May your heart be at peace."
- The meditation closes with a sense of mutual connection, inviting practitioners to feel the shared field of tenderness between themselves and the person they have been holding in mind.

#### The Neuroscience and Evolutionary Basis of Compassion

- Research is cited showing that when compassion arises, the brain activates regions associated with positive reward, suggesting that opening the heart is part of human evolutionary design.
- Compassion and love are noted to correlate with brain regions near the motor cortex, explaining why actively expressing care through voice or action can deepen the felt experience of compassion.
- The survival brain is identified as a primary obstacle to compassion, as fear, grasping, and perceived threat automatically shut down the capacity for care toward others.
- Anthropologist Margaret Mead's discovery of an ancient healed femur bone is described as evidence that the first sign of human civilization was the act of one person caring for another through injury and recovery.

#### Widening Compassion to Difficult Relationships

- The most common suffering brought to mindfulness teachers is identified as the experience of distance, resentment, and conflict within close relationships, including family, friends, and colleagues.
- Political and ideological differences are named as a particularly prevalent source of relational separation, especially in the context of recent years.
- A three-step process for widening compassion to difficult others is outlined, beginning with honoring the anger or dislike present without making it wrong.
- Anger is described as initiatory but not transformative, meaning it signals the need for protection and care but must be deepened into vulnerability in order to become healing.
- A quote from a 1963 interview between James Baldwin and Nikki Giovanni is shared to illustrate that honestly acknowledging dislike or anger is the necessary starting point before genuine openness becomes possible.
- The second step involves making a U-turn, shifting attention inward to investigate what lies beneath the anger, typically feelings of hurt, shame, powerlessness, or unmet needs such as being seen, respected, or loved.
- Premature forgiveness or forced compassion is cautioned against as inauthentic and likely to collapse, emphasizing that genuine opening requires first bringing compassion to one's own wounded and vulnerable places.
- Once self-compassion is established, the third step involves shifting attention to the other person and genuinely wondering what they might be experiencing, what fears or unmet needs are driving their behavior.
- A detailed example of a couple navigating pandemic-era tensions over unequal domestic labor is used to illustrate how the U-turn process can shift a relationship from blame and distance to understanding and connection.
- The Hawaiian healing phrase "I'm sorry and I love you" is introduced as a tool for softening the inner landscape before attempting to understand the other person.
- Genuine compassion toward difficult others is described as not requiring a clean resolution but rather a willingness to hold imperfection with understanding, which itself opens the environment for whatever change is possible.

#### Compassion Practice for Difficult Relationships

- Participants are guided to bring to mind a relationship where there is tension, conflict, or resentment, specifically one that does not involve trauma.
- Attention is directed to the primary emotional response present, such as anger, dislike, or blame, with the instruction to allow it to be there without judgment.
- The U-turn is practiced by investigating what lies beneath the surface emotion, identifying the beliefs running in the mind and the feelings most difficult to acknowledge, such as hurt, despair, or shame.
- Participants are invited to identify the unmet need at the core of the difficulty, whether it is the need to be seen, understood, respected, safe, or loved.
- Self-compassion is then offered to that vulnerable place, using the hand on the heart, compassionate phrases, or the image of a compassionate archetype such as Kuan Yin.
- From a place of greater openness, attention is shifted to the other person, imagining their experience, their painful beliefs, their unmet needs, and how they might behave differently if those needs were met.
- Participants are invited to sense their own heart as a field of care that can include the other person with tenderness, and to reflect on what new choices might become available in future interactions.

#### The Role of Compassion in Teaching

- Mindfulness teachers are described as becoming a field of compassion and love for their students, holding whatever students carry with a tender and open heart.
- A children's book written in third-grade handwriting titled "I Wish My Teacher Knew" is referenced to illustrate the depth of unseen suffering that students carry and rarely voice aloud.
- Teachers are encouraged to draw on their own lived experience of transformation through self-compassion as the most authentic source of teaching, because real teaching comes from knowing what has shifted in oneself.
- The medical school model of "see one, do one, teach one" is applied to compassion training, framing the program as a process of practicing, embodying, and then transmitting these qualities to others.
- Participants are reminded that their students will long for refuge and belonging, and that wherever they are in the world, there will be people ready to wake up through the teachings being offered.

### Stories

**Conda Mason's Six-Week Silent Retreat and the Kuan Yin Statue**

- Shared by Conda Mason, referring to her own experience
- Conda had a painful falling out with her co-director and business partners, feeling betrayed and deeply hurt
- She sought guidance from her mentor Jack repeatedly but could not resolve the pain
- She went on a six-week silent meditation retreat at Insight Meditation Society in Massachusetts
- At the retreat, she was consumed by anger and hurt; a teacher asked her what was underneath the anger
- She discovered that beneath the anger was shame — shame that she had failed and had not done things right
- She realized she had been walking past a statue of Kuan Yin, the Bodhisattva of compassion, throughout her walking meditation without connecting it to her own need
- When she turned the lens to self-compassion, everything shifted; she returned from the retreat with the anger gone and with understanding that both she and her partners had done the best they could

**The Dalai Lama and Sharon Salzberg at IMS**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to the Dalai Lama and Sharon Salzberg
- In the late 1970s, the Dalai Lama visited Insight Meditation Society in Massachusetts
- Sharon Salzberg had broken her ankle and was on crutches, standing at the back of the group
- Upon arriving, the Dalai Lama immediately noticed Sharon and walked over to ask, "What happened? Are you okay?"
- Offered as an example of compassion expressed in the minute particulars

**Miriam and Elena: The Asylum Case**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to his daughter Caroline and her organization Oasis
- Caroline Kornfield founded Oasis to work with LGBTQ asylum seekers whose lives are in danger
- A volunteer lawyer named Miriam from Saudi Arabia was assigned to represent Elena, a transgender woman
- Miriam's cultural background made the assignment challenging, but she was respectful and courteous
- During a four-hour wait for the asylum officer, Elena showed Miriam a photo of her Pomeranian dog Lily; Miriam also had a Pomeranian named Ali
- They bonded over their dogs, their love of jeweled high heels and Louis Vuitton purses, and Miriam taught Elena about her abaya
- By the time the officer was ready, Elena's fear had receded and Miriam was whispering reassurances
- Elena was granted asylum, escaping near-certain death if returned to El Salvador
- Miriam was preparing to return to Saudi Arabia; they would likely never meet again but had profoundly impacted each other

**The Book "I Wish My Teacher Knew"**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to a children's book written in third-grade handwriting
- Children wrote what they wished their teacher knew: a father working two jobs, no pens or pencils at home, a mother who cannot read, homelessness after a parent's cancer diagnosis, feeling smarter than the teacher thinks, a little brother who gets scared at night, a dream to work with the SPCA
- Offered as an illustration of the hidden suffering students carry that teachers may never hear spoken aloud

**Trudy and the Neonatal Intensive Care Nurses**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to his wife Trudy who runs Inside LA
- Trudy began working with nurses and doctors from the neonatal intensive care unit at a children's hospital
- They brought staff together on retreats to hold the sacred work of caring for children who live and children who die
- The retreats provided a space of compassion where staff could acknowledge the weight of their work with tender hearts

**Jack Kornfield's Daughter and the Children Beggars**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to his daughter at age six
- On a visit to a country considerably poorer than the US, his daughter encountered homeless children begging on the street for the first time
- She asked for money to give them, then said: "Daddy, they don't have any place to live. We have money. Let's get them a house."
- Offered as an illustration of the natural compassion wired into human beings from childhood

**Margaret Mead and the Healed Femur**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to anthropologist Margaret Mead
- When asked about the first sign of human civilization, Mead described finding an ancient human skeleton with a broken femur that had healed
- She interpreted this as evidence that someone had cared for the injured person — providing food, water, and shelter — until they recovered
- She said this was what it means to be human

**The COVID-Era Couple**

- Shared by Tara Brach, referring to a friend and meditator in her community
- Both partners transitioned to working from home with full-time jobs and a six-year-old child
- The woman was doing significantly more childcare, homeschooling, and household management
- Her partner agreed to take on more but did not follow through, leading to growing anger and distance
- She did the U-turn practice: recognized her anger, found beneath it feelings of being devalued and disrespected, brought self-compassion using the phrase "I'm sorry and I love you"
- She then reflected on her partner and recognized he was deeply anxious about failing at work, exacerbated by pandemic uncertainty, and was doing the best he could
- She approached him with curiosity rather than confrontation; he opened up about his fear; more connection followed
- A few days later she found him listening to a guided meditation on fear
- Offered as an example of an ordinary conflict with no clean resolution but with increased understanding and compassion

**Holland and the Bedtime Forgiveness**

- Shared by Tara Brach, referring to a woman's account of her three-year-old daughter Holland
- After a difficult bedtime struggle, the mother said through clenched teeth that she loved Holland but there would be no more words that night
- Holland, standing on the bed with wild hair and tear-streaked face, said: "Mommy, I forgive you."
- When asked if she knew what forgiveness meant, Holland said: "It means you were wrong and I'm tired of being mad and now I'm going to sleep and my heart won't have a tummy ache."
- Offered as an illustration of the natural wisdom of forgiveness and the cost of holding onto anger

**Ram Dass and His Father**

- Shared by Tara Brach, referring to Ram Dass
- As Ram Dass's father approached death, Ram Dass finally allowed his father to be who he was rather than trying to change him
- His father reciprocated by no longer trying to change Ram Dass
- They became friends
- Offered as an illustration of releasing the expectation that others must change before we can fully love them
