### The Power of RAIN

📅 October 16, 2026

👥 Tara Brach (Main Teacher), Jack Kornfield (Contributing Teacher)

#### Outline

#### Introduction to RAIN as an Applied Meditation

- RAIN is described as a four-step applied meditation that brings the wings of mindfulness and compassion to difficult emotions and challenging inner experiences.
- Designed as a structured sequence, the practice is particularly valuable because it gives students a clear and easy-to-remember framework precisely when emotional hijacking makes mindfulness most difficult to access.
- The four steps of RAIN stand for Recognize, Allow, Investigate, and Nurture, each serving a distinct function in processing difficult emotional states.
- Numerous practitioners have reported that RAIN saved their lives, with its impact being especially pronounced during periods of heightened fear, loss, uncertainty, relational conflict, and loneliness such as those experienced during the pandemic.

#### Teaching RAIN Through Personal Narrative

- Illustrating the steps of RAIN through a personal story that the teacher is deeply inside is emphasized as a highly effective pedagogical approach.
- Tara Brach shares a personal account involving her 82-year-old mother coming to live with her during an intensely demanding professional period, which generated feelings of guilt and anxiety.
- The Recognize step involved mentally naming the emotions present, specifically anxiety and guilt, without elaboration or analysis.
- Allowing meant letting the emotions exist without adding judgment, trying to fix them, or making them go away, using the internal phrase "this belongs" to acknowledge the reality of the moment.
- Investigating involved asking the question "What am I believing right now?" which revealed a core belief about failing both professionally and as a daughter, and then locating that belief somatically in the body, particularly in the chest and heart.
- Nurturing began with placing a hand on the heart and sending a message of trust and self-compassion to the anxious inner state.
- After the RAIN, a noticeable shift toward greater presence and reduced identification with the anxious, guilty self was experienced, allowing for more genuine connection with her mother in the months that followed.

#### The Concept of Non-Identification and After the RAIN

- Non-identification, originally the final step in Michelle McDonald's version of RAIN coined in the early 1990s, is reframed not as a step to perform but as a realization that naturally arises when mindfulness and compassion are fully engaged.
- Tara Brach reconfigured the original acronym, which stood for Recognition, Acceptance, Interest, and Non-Identification, by replacing the N with Nurture, based on her conviction that self-compassion is essential to genuine inner freedom.
- Non-identification is explained through the metaphor of waves and ocean, where being stuck in difficult emotions means being defined by the waves, while practicing RAIN allows one to become more like the ocean in which waves arise without taking over.
- The phase after the RAIN is introduced as the blossoming or flowering that follows the four steps, analogous to how nature flourishes after actual rainfall.
- Becoming familiar with the quality of presence that emerges after RAIN is described as essential to trusting that this presence is more fundamentally true than any self-limiting story.
- Students new to meditation are noted to be not only attracted to RAIN but genuinely helped by it, and the practice can serve as an accessible entry point into a broader meditation practice.

#### Broad Applications and Accessibility of RAIN

- RAIN has been taught across a wide range of contexts including technology companies, legislative bodies, educational institutions, and therapeutic settings, demonstrating its broad applicability.
- A parent's account is shared in which a four-year-old child independently applied RAIN at school to manage frustration about wearing a mask, illustrating that the practice can be made accessible even to very young children.
- The child's self-directed application involved recognizing the feeling in her belly, sitting with it, taking a deep breath, and offering herself a self-hug with the words "I love you, Anna," demonstrating the practice's simplicity.
- One of the most pervasive and significant applications of RAIN is helping people interrupt unhealthy patterns of reactivity in interpersonal relationships.
- Practicing RAIN on the sidelines with triggering situations, as a form of rehearsal or review, builds the capacity to pause and make different choices when those situations arise in real time.
- Whatever is practiced repeatedly becomes stronger, meaning that consistent application of mindfulness and compassion through RAIN increases the likelihood of pausing and shifting behavior in live situations.

#### RAIN Applied to Anger and Interpersonal Reactivity

- An executive at an IT firm known for a volatile temper began working with RAIN specifically to address anger that was damaging his marriage.
- A simplified in-the-moment version called a light rain was introduced, consisting of naming the emotion, pausing to allow it, investigating briefly through breath and body sensation, and nurturing with the phrase "It's okay."
- After approximately one month of practice, the executive reported that roughly 25 percent of the time he no longer said something he regretted when triggered, which was described as a significant improvement for someone with a chronic anger pattern.
- During a meeting in which a project manager admitted to letting things fall through the cracks, the executive applied light rain in real time by naming his anger, pausing, breathing, and allowing the feeling rather than reacting.
- Pausing created enough space to notice that the project manager looked strained and exhausted, prompting a compassionate response rather than an aggressive one.
- The project manager then disclosed that his wife had stage four breast cancer and that he was managing two teenagers, revealing the hidden vulnerability beneath the professional failure.
- Both men ended up in tears and embraced, with the executive describing it as one of his saddest and best moments and as a return to being a real human being.
- This story is offered not as a claim that everyone carries such dramatic burdens, but as a reminder that all people live with vulnerability and fear, and that RAIN creates more opportunity to meet others with presence and compassion.

#### Jack Kornfield's Perspective on Mindful Inquiry in Teaching

- Jack Kornfield introduces a complementary skill for teachers involving pausing with students who are activated or troubled and inviting them to turn inward by closing their eyes or lowering their gaze.
- Asking students "What are you noticing just now?" and "Where do you feel what's going on in your body?" are offered as powerful in-the-moment inquiry tools that embody the essence of RAIN without necessarily naming the steps.
- Following somatic inquiry, asking "What happens if you hold that with compassion?" and "What is important for you to learn from this?" deepens the process further.
- Zen Master Dogen's framing of awakening as becoming intimate with all things is cited as a philosophical foundation for the kind of mindful loving awareness that RAIN cultivates.
- When practiced in a group setting, this approach opens the gateway to shared understanding and a felt sense of common humanity among participants.
- These skills are described as moving beyond simply answering questions or acknowledging difficulties, instead guiding students toward inner connection and freedom.

#### Guidance on the Investigate Step

- Investigating is clarified as a primarily somatic process rather than a cognitive or analytical one, aimed at contacting and experiencing what is present in the body.
- A common misunderstanding is that investigating means tracing intellectual or biographical explanations for emotional reactions, such as analyzing childhood experiences, which is explicitly identified as not the intent.
- The phrase "your issues are in your tissues" is offered as a memorable way to convey that real healing comes from bringing mindful awareness and kindness to where lived experience and wounds reside in the body.
- Asking "What am I believing right now?" can serve as a useful portal into investigation, but the inquiry must then return to the body to locate where that belief is felt somatically.
- Years can be spent circling around ideas about what is happening emotionally without achieving the genuine healing that comes from direct somatic contact with the experience.

#### Guidance on the Nurture Step

- Nurturing can arise from one's own awakened heart or from an imagined external source of compassion, and self-compassion does not require that the self be the sole origin of the care being offered.
- Simple phrases such as "It's okay," "Trust your goodness," or "Trust the gold" are offered as examples of nurturing messages that can be directed inward.
- When feeling small or regressed, visualizing a field of light, love, and presence surrounding the body and imagining it bathing the self is described as an effective alternative nurturing approach.
- Two valuable inquiries for customizing nurture with students are asking what source of compassion they most trust and what message or expression of care would feel most healing.
- Possible sources of compassion include a trusted friend, a living or deceased loved one, a spiritual figure, an animal companion, or the natural world, reflecting the need for personalization.
- Nurturing requires flexibility and attunement rather than a fixed formula, and when leading groups, offering several options and allowing people to gravitate toward what resonates is recommended.

#### Trauma-Sensitive Approaches and Nurture Seasons

- RAIN requires careful attunement because some individuals are not ready to engage directly with the full four steps, particularly when trauma is present in the body.
- In cases involving trauma or significant overwhelm, beginning with nurturing and resourcing rather than moving through the steps sequentially is recommended, and this preparatory phase may last days or months.
- At any point during a guided RAIN session, participants should be reminded that if anything feels overwhelming, they can return to nurture or resourcing as a stabilizing anchor.
- Even in the absence of trauma, many people benefit from extended periods of nurture practice either alongside RAIN or independently, as a way of building sufficient stability and resilience.
- Shauna Shapiro's experience following a painful breakup is cited as an illustration of a nurture season, in which her therapist assigned a daily practice of saying "Good morning" to herself as a starting point when "I love you" felt too confronting.
- Gradually, the practice evolved to "Good morning, I love you, Shauna," and over time the mechanical repetition gave way to a genuine felt sense of love and connection, including a sense of her grandmother's and mother's presence.
- The principle that people need to love themselves into healing underscores the importance of strengthening the nurture capacity before expecting the full RAIN process to be accessible.

#### RAIN Partners as a Co-Meditation Practice

- RAIN Partners is described as an optional but highly encouraged co-meditation practice in which two people support each other through the RAIN process.
- Practitioners consistently report that working with a partner allows the practice to go deeper than solo meditation, with several distinct mechanisms accounting for this effect.
- Hearing another person's challenges makes it easier to allow and not judge one's own life situations, reducing the sense of shame and personal isolation that often accompanies difficult emotions.
- Knowing that a debrief will follow the practice increases accountability and focus, with the structure of the partnership helping practitioners stay more committed and present.
- The container created by practicing with a partner generates a quality of safety that makes it more possible to touch into painful inner territory.
- Perhaps most significantly, co-meditation through RAIN Partners cultivates genuine intimacy and togetherness between participants.
- Graduates of previous cohorts who engaged in RAIN Partners reported that the practice supported them in processing material arising both from the training program and from their broader lives, deepened their mastery in guiding others, and in many cases led to lasting friendships.

#### Guided RAIN Meditation Practice

- Participants are invited to scan their lives and identify a situation that triggers difficult feelings such as fear, anger, shame, or relational pain, with the guidance to avoid selecting anything that feels traumatic or potentially overwhelming.
- Bringing the situation to mind in sensory detail, including the other person's words, facial expression, or the surrounding environment, is encouraged as a way of activating the emotional material more fully.
- Participants are asked to identify and briefly journal the situation in one sentence along with the primary emotions it evokes, such as anger, defensiveness, shame, or a sense of invisibility.
- The Recognize step is practiced by mentally naming the emotions present, and the Allow step is deepened through the internal phrase "This belongs" or a simple "yes" as an acknowledgment of present reality without requiring approval of it.
- Investigating is guided somatically by directing attention to the throat, chest, and belly, and by inviting participants to let their posture and facial expression physically reflect the emotions being felt.
- Placing a hand on the heart or wherever feeling is strongest is introduced as a bridge between investigating and nurturing, maintaining somatic contact while beginning to offer tenderness.
- Nurturing is guided by asking what the activated part most needs, whether understanding, acceptance, forgiveness, or love, and by offering messages of care from one's own wise heart or from an imagined source of loving presence.
- Imagining pure love flowing in from a trusted source, through the hand and into the area of greatest vulnerability, is offered as a way of allowing compassion to reach the cells and spaces of the body.
- After the RAIN, participants are invited to rest in a more spacious awareness and notice whatever shift in quality of presence has emerged compared to the beginning of the practice.
- A poem by Roshani Ray is read aloud, evoking the paradox of brokenness giving rise to the unbroken, sorrow leading to joy, and fragility yielding strength, as a poetic framing of the after-the-RAIN experience.

#### The Value of Meditating Together and Shared Awareness

- A common misconception in meditation is the idea of a self meditating itself into freedom, which is challenged by the experience of practicing RAIN with a partner.
- Meditating together, sharing what is arising, and debriefing afterward reinforces the understanding that it is awareness waking up through us rather than a self-contained individual effort.
- Human conditioning is normalized as simply part of being human rather than a deficiency, and shared practice makes working with that conditioning more viable and liberating.
- Rumi's image of night travelers who turn toward darkness and know their own fear is cited as a poetic expression of the courage and companionship involved in this kind of shared inner work.
- The phrase "night travelers are full of light and you are too, don't leave this companionship" is offered as an invitation to remain in the shared practice rather than retreating into isolation.
- Bringing hearts and minds together in practices of mindfulness and compassion is described as a deep and powerful act, and participants are encouraged to explore it in their own way.

### Detailed Subject Matter

**The RAIN Framework**

- RAIN stands for Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Nurture
- Described as an applied meditation bringing the wings of mindfulness and compassion to difficult emotions
- Originally coined by senior Buddhist teacher Michelle McDonald in the early 1990s as Recognition, Acceptance, Interest, and Non-Identification
- Tara Brach reconfigured the acronym, replacing Non-Identification with Nurture, believing self-compassion is essential to freedom
- Non-identification is reframed as a realization — the freedom that arises naturally when mindfulness and compassion are fully awake, not a step to perform
- Tara refers to the state after completing the steps as "After the RAIN," likening it to how everything flowers after real rain

**Recognize**

- Mentally naming the emotions present, such as anxiety or guilt
- Simple labeling of what is arising without elaboration

**Allow**

- Letting the emotion be present without adding judgment, trying to fix it, or making it go away
- Suggested phrases include "This belongs" or "Yes" to acknowledge the reality of the moment
- Described as recognizing the emotion as a wave that belongs in the present moment

**Investigate**

- Emphasized as primarily somatic, not cognitive
- The goal is to contact and experience what is happening in the body
- A portal in may be asking "What am I believing right now?" but the instruction is to return to the body and find where the experience lives
- Described with the phrase "Your issues are in your tissues"
- Involves locating where feelings are strongest — throat, chest, belly — and allowing posture and face to express the emotion
- Circling around ideas without touching the lived bodily experience is identified as a barrier to real healing

**Nurture**

- Can come from one's own awakened heart or from an imagined larger source
- Suggested phrases: "It's okay," "It's okay sweetheart," "Trust your goodness," "Trust the gold," "I'm here and I'm not leaving," "Thank you for trying to protect me, I'm okay right now"
- Physical gesture of placing a hand on the heart supports both investigating and nurturing
- Sources of compassion can include a trusted friend, grandparent, teacher, healer, spiritual figure, a pet, or the natural world
- When feeling small or regressed, visualizing a field of light and love surrounding the body is offered as an approach
- Two key inquiries for students: what source of compassion do you most trust, and what message or expression of care would be healing

**After the RAIN**

- The moments following the four steps where the quality of presence that has emerged is noticed
- Students are encouraged to observe the difference from when they started — more space, kindness, tenderness, openness
- Resting in a more spacious awareness, allowing whatever arises to come and go freely
- Getting familiar with this presence builds trust that it is more the truth of who we are than any story we tell ourselves

**Light RAIN**

- An abbreviated in-the-moment version for use when fully triggered
- Steps compressed into seconds: name the emotion, pause and allow, breathe and feel without acting, say "It's okay"

**RAIN on the Sidelines**

- Practicing RAIN with triggering situations outside of the live moment
- Described as rehearsing or redoing situations to build capacity to pause when in the actual situation
- Whatever is practiced gets stronger; sideline practice increases the likelihood of pausing and having more choices in real time

**Trauma-Informed Adaptations**

- When trauma is present in the body, people may not be ready to directly engage the two wings of RAIN
- In such cases, beginning with nurturing, resourcing, and grounding is recommended
- This preparatory phase may take days or months until enough stability and resilience is established
- At any moment during RAIN guidance, participants should be reminded that if anything feels overwhelming, they can return to nurture or resourcing
- Many people need intensive seasons of nurture either alongside RAIN or on their own

**Nurture Seasons**

- Extended periods focused primarily on self-compassion practice before or alongside RAIN
- Illustrated through the story of Shauna Shapiro beginning with "Good morning" and gradually building to "Good morning, I love you, Shauna"

**RAIN Partners**

- An optional but highly encouraged co-meditation practice
- Partners take turns sharing a situation and naming emotions while the other holds silent, judgment-free space
- Reported benefits include reduced shame, reduced sense of isolation, greater accountability, deeper focus, increased safety to touch painful places, and real intimacy
- Debriefing after practice deepens the experience
- Doing RAIN with a partner helps loosen identification because hearing others' challenges makes one's own feel less personal and shameful
- The container created by partnership makes it more possible to touch into painful places
- Many from previous cohorts continued with their partners beyond the program and reported deeper mastery in guiding others
- RAIN Partners protocol is available on Tara Brach's website
- Partners can be found via the MMTCP portal forum before the April matching

**Teaching RAIN to Others**

- Illustrating the steps through a personal story the teacher is genuinely inside is recommended
- RAIN can be done very simply or go very deep and nuanced
- It serves as an entry point into meditation for those who have never meditated
- The more people practice meditation, the deeper RAIN goes — described as synergistic
- RAIN has been taught at Apple, Google, and in courses on Capitol Hill in both the House and Senate
- Educators and therapists have sought RAIN training
- When guiding individuals out loud, asking questions such as "Where do you feel it in your body?" and "Is there a larger source you'd like to call on?" allows customization

**Jack Kornfield's Contribution**

- Described a skill of pausing with students when they are activated, inviting them to close their eyes or lower their gaze and turn inward
- Questions used in this approach: "What are you noticing just now?" "Where do you feel what's going on in your body?" "What happens if you hold that with compassion?" "What is important for you to learn from this?"
- Framed RAIN as carrying the essence of mindfulness, compassion, and inquiry
- Referenced Zen Master Dogen's description of awakening as becoming intimate with all things
- Noted that RAIN and related approaches drop below simply answering questions to take students to a place of connection and inner freedom
- When done in a group, RAIN opens the gateway of other people's understanding and hearts and a sense of common humanity

### Spiritual and Societal Insights

**The Two Wings of Mindfulness and Compassion**

- RAIN is described as a weaving of mindful awareness and intentional compassion, not a rigid formulaic sequence
- The steps are a structure to return to when emotionally hijacked, not a mechanical process
- Freedom arises when both wings — mindfulness and compassion — are genuinely awake

**Non-Identification as Realization**

- When stuck in waves of fear, the waves define the entire sense of self and feel very personal
- As mindfulness and compassion deepen through RAIN, one becomes more like the ocean — fear is a wave but does not define or take over
- This shift is non-identification: the place where there is freedom to be wholly who we are

**Presence as the Deeper Truth**

- After the RAIN, noticing the presence that has emerged builds familiarity with a freedom that is more the truth of who we are than any story we have been telling ourselves
- Described as homecoming — the bumper sticker referenced: "If you lived in your heart, you'd be home right now"

**Shared Practice and Common Humanity**

- Meditating together is framed not as a self waking itself up but as awareness waking up through us
- Shared practice helps people remember that conditioning is not bad, it is simply part of being human
- Doing RAIN with a partner creates real intimacy and togetherness
- Hearing others' challenges in a partner setting reduces shame and the sense that one's difficulties are uniquely personal

**Universal Vulnerability**

- Everyone is living with vulnerability and fears
- The saying referenced: "Be kind to everyone you meet, for everyone is fighting a hard battle"
- RAIN applied to interpersonal reactivity creates more chance to pause, slow down, and be present with others

**Rumi's Night Travelers**

- Willingness to turn toward darkness and know one's own fear and difficulties is the path of the night traveler
- Night travelers are described as full of light
- The invitation is to go down and in, discovering within one's essence the shared essence of boundless, timeless love

**Societal Applications**

- RAIN has been brought into corporate environments, government institutions, educational settings, and therapeutic contexts
- Even young children can learn and apply RAIN independently
- The practice builds capacity for interrupting unhealthy habits of reactivity in relationships

### Stories

**Tara Brach and Her Mother**

- Tara Brach shares a personal story about her mother coming to live with her and her husband at age 82
- Tara was in a demanding period with writing deadlines and heavy teaching commitments
- She felt guilty about not spending enough time with her mother and anxious about getting everything done
- One day while working in her office on a loving-kindness talk, her mother came in to show her an article and Tara barely looked up
- Her mother graciously put the article down and walked away
- Tara looked at her retreating form and thought, "I don't know how long I'll have her"
- She stopped and practiced RAIN: recognized anxiety and guilt, allowed them, investigated by asking what she was believing and found the belief that she would fail and fall short as both a teacher and a daughter, located the feeling in her heart, placed her hand there, and nurtured by telling herself to trust that teachings would flow and that she loved her mother
- After the RAIN there was more presence and she was no longer so identified as an anxious guilty daughter
- In the following months she found she could be fully present with her mother during meals and walks by the river without waiting to get back to her office
- Her mother died several years later and Tara felt deep sorrow but not regret
- This experience was part of her motivation for writing Radical Compassion

**The Four-Year-Old Girl in Ontario**

- A woman from Ontario wrote to Tara Brach about teaching her four-year-old daughter RAIN
- The mother had been helping her daughter use RAIN when feeling frustrated, sad, lonely, bored, or happy
- One night at bedtime the daughter reported she had done RAIN at school on her own
- She had been getting upset about having to wear a mask, went to her cubby, and practiced RAIN instead of throwing a fit
- She described sitting and feeling the frustration in her belly, and after a few minutes feeling a little better and taking a deep breath
- She would hug herself and say "I love you, Anna"

**The IT Executive and the Project Manager**

- An executive at an IT firm was known for his temper, which was causing real trouble in his marriage
- He began working with Tara Brach to learn mindfulness and apply RAIN to anger
- He practiced light RAIN on the spot: naming "anger," pausing to allow, breathing and feeling it, and saying "It's okay"
- After about a month, approximately 25% of the time he did not say something he regretted
- In one instance, a project manager admitted in a meeting that the team had fallen behind and that he personally had let things fall through the cracks
- The executive felt rising irritation but instead of pouncing, he named it, paused, breathed, allowed it, and found more presence
- He noticed the project manager looked strained and tired and recalled that this was a committed person
- He said, "I'm sure you're doing the best you can," which caught the manager off guard
- The manager, with tears in his eyes, disclosed that his wife had stage four breast cancer and he had two teenagers at home
- The two men hugged with tears
- The executive told Tara: "A month ago I would have just added to this man's burden and there we were hugging. It was one of my saddest and best moments. It was like I had found my way back to being a real human being."

**Shauna Shapiro and the Nurture Season**

- Teacher Shauna Shapiro experienced deep pain following a breakup and was locked in a sense of unworthiness and shame
- Her therapist assigned her to say "I love you" to herself every day
- Shauna said she could not do that — it was too much
- They compromised and her assignment became saying "Good morning" to herself each day
- She did this for about a month as her loving-kindness practice
- Her therapist then said it was time to graduate to "Good morning, I love you, Shauna"
- It felt mechanical at first
- One day she said it and began feeling her grandmother's and mother's presence and the love in the universe washing through her and alive in her
- She could feel the truth of it
