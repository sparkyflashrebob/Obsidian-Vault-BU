### Outline
### Overview of the MMTCP Mentor Program Structure

- The MMTCP program pairs students with a mentor and a small group of peers for the entirety of a two-year training journey.

- Mentor groups meet twice a month in a committed and conscious way, oriented toward practice and showing up fully for each other's lives.

- Groups consist of no more than six students and serve as the primary anchor and home base throughout the program.

- Additional subgroups and geography-based groups exist within the broader program, offering further opportunities to connect with other participants beyond the mentor group.

- The mentor leadership team is composed of four members, all of whom are active mentors in the program in addition to providing support to other mentors.

### The Role and Importance of Mentor Groups in the Training

- Mentor groups are described as one of the most important aspects of the entire MMTCP training, offering a rare and sustained container for growth.

- Over the course of two years, significant life events such as relationships, loss, illness, and career changes may arise for group members, all of which are held within the lens of practice.

- Students use the group to give and receive feedback, listen to each other teach, and support one another in stepping into or deepening the teacher's role.

- Peer groups provide a space to practice wise speech, offer guided meditations, and develop teaching skills in a conscious and supportive environment.

- The depth of connection formed in mentor groups has the potential to create lifelong relationships and spiritual friendships that extend beyond the program itself.

### Approaching Challenges and Imperfection Within the Group

- Mentor groups, like all aspects of life, will be imperfect, and participants should not expect their group to always be comfortable or conflict-free.

- Difficulties that arise within the group, whether relational, personal, or practice-related, are not obstacles to training but are themselves the training.

- Whatever is in the way is the way, meaning that challenges encountered in the group will mirror the challenges one will face when sitting in the teacher's seat.

- Discomfort and stretching beyond one's comfort zone are identified as the primary conditions under which deep learning takes place.

- Participants are encouraged to trust that their group, even if it does not feel ideal, is exactly what is needed for their particular growth and learning.

### Intention, Presence, and Quality of Engagement

- The quality of one's experience in the mentor group is directly related to the attitude, intention, and attention brought to each session.

- Practical expressions of care include arriving on time, logging in early to settle, preparing by reflecting on questions in advance, and honoring community agreements.

- Writing down one's intention at the outset of the program is recommended, as it serves as a reference point during moments of doubt or difficulty over the following months.

- Participants are encouraged to remain present rather than focusing on future milestones such as the practicum, which takes place in the second year.

- Even experienced teachers are invited to fully inhabit the role of student, approaching all material with curiosity and openness regardless of prior knowledge or credentials.

### Navigating Doubt and Trusting the Process

- Doubt is identified as a normal and expected experience throughout the training, particularly when encountering accomplished peers or challenging material.

- Participants are encouraged to keep moving forward and return to their original intention when doubt arises, rather than allowing it to derail their engagement.

- Trusting the process is presented as a foundational orientation, with the understanding that learning is emergent and one's intentions may evolve or expand over time.

- Mentors are described as well-qualified and thoughtfully matched, and participants are encouraged to trust that they are with the right mentor even if it does not immediately feel that way.

### The Role of Feedback in Teacher Development

- Receiving and offering feedback is a formal and central component of the mentor group experience, applied to talks, guided meditations, and responses to questions.

- This structured feedback process is described as a rare opportunity in a teacher's life to receive conscious, thoughtful, and constructive input on their skills.

- Feedback should be grounded in the principles of wise speech, meaning it is truthful, useful, and delivered with kindness.

- The energy and tone with which feedback is given matters as much as its content, and a spirit of goodwill and mutual support transforms the feedback process into a powerful learning tool.

- Participants are encouraged to lean into feedback rather than resist it, treating it as a gift and a significant dimension of their development.

### Cultivating a Growth Mindset and Shared Group Intention

- A growth mindset, as distinct from a fixed mindset, is presented as essential to the learning process, emphasizing development and skill-deepening over the need to appear competent.

- Mastery is achieved through a willingness to make mistakes, and mistakes are reframed as necessary and valuable steps in the journey toward wisdom.

- The Buddhist teaching that good friendship constitutes the whole of the spiritual life is invoked to underscore the significance of the peer relationships formed in mentor groups.

- When group members share a genuine intention to support one another's awakening, the group dynamic can become truly transformative and even magical.

- Conversely, when there is resistance, judgment, or misalignment of intention, the group experience can become difficult, making the cultivation of shared goodwill a collective responsibility.

- Groups meet twice a month for the full two-year duration of the program

### Stories
**The Wise Master and Bad Judgment**
James Baraz shares a traditional teaching story: A seeker travels many days to sit at the feet of a renowned wise master. When he arrives, he asks how the master became so wise. The master replies: good judgment. Asked how he got good judgment, the master says: experience. Asked how he got so much experience, the master answers: bad judgment. James Baraz uses this story to illustrate the growth mindset and the necessity of making mistakes on the path to mastery.
**James Baraz's First Dharma Talk**
James Baraz recounts giving his first Dharma talk to a large group in 1983 at a retreat center in Yucca Valley. There were 150 students present, along with teachers Joseph Goldstein, Jack Kornfield, and Sharon Salzberg. He was nervous speaking in front of his teachers. Afterward, he asked Joseph Goldstein for feedback. Goldstein told him kindly that what he said was good but that he might speak up, as only the first few rows had heard him due to his nervousness. Baraz recalls the kindness and generosity of that feedback as something he never forgot, using it to illustrate the power of delivering feedback with warmth.
**The Metta Queens — Conda Mason's Mentor Group**
Conda Mason shares that Shelly, who appeared on a prior panel, was in her mentor group, which called themselves the Metta Queens. The group developed a deep bond and dynamic connection during the program and continued to stay together beyond its conclusion. Conda uses this as an example of the lifelong relationships that can form within a mentor group.
**James Baraz's Previous Mentor Group**
James Baraz mentions that in the most recent cohort, Sarah Marie was in his mentor group. He describes the group as having great diversity among its members and a strong sense of mutual support and encouragement, with everyone cheering each other on and helping each other learn from mistakes.
