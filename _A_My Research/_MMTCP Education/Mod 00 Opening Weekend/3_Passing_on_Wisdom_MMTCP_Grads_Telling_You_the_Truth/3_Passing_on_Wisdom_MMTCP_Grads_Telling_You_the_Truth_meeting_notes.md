# 3 Passing on Wisdom MMTCP Grads Telling You the Truth - Oct, 16

### Passing on Wisdom: MMTCP Grads Telling You the Truth
📅 October 16, 2026
👥 **Facilitator:** Anne Cushman, Director of Mentoring, MMTCP | **Panelists:** Veronica Onassis, Shelly Harrell, Shi Ren Zhu (Renju), Sarah Marie Huff
Tags: MMTCP, Mindfulness Teacher Training, Community, Practicum, Liberation, Mindfulness and Race, Leadership, Zoom Teaching, Peer Groups, Embodiment, Organizational Development, Buddhist Practice
### Outline
### Panelist Introductions and Program Overview

- Anne Cushman, Director of Mentoring for the MMTCP program, opened the session by welcoming four recent graduates who had completed the program two years after starting in the same position as the incoming students.

- Veronica Onassis was introduced as a mindfulness teacher based in Buenos Aires, Argentina, a former school teacher who trained with Mindful Schools and created a series of Spanish-language guided meditation podcasts during the program, with her practicum focused on teaching introductory mindfulness to school teachers and staff.

- Shelly Harrell was introduced as a professor at Pepperdine University's Graduate School of Psychology and Education, a psychologist specializing in cultural and socio-political considerations in mental health, whose practicum called "A Soulful Introduction to Mindfulness" served primarily Black, Indigenous, and people of color populations.

- Shi Ren Zhu, also known as Renju, was introduced as a Buddhist monk at the Kongmeng Monastery in Singapore with a PhD in Buddhist studies, who taught introductory mindfulness and loving kindness classes in both Chinese and English for his practicum and writes a Mindfulness 365 WeChat blog.

- Sarah Marie Huff was introduced as an integral coach, systems change strategist, and entrepreneur based in Oakland, California, whose practicum focused on purpose-driven leaders, changemakers, coaches, creatives, and entrepreneurs.

### Advice on Building Community Within the Program

- Veronica emphasized that connecting with as many people as possible throughout the two years is the single most valuable piece of advice she could offer incoming students.

- Seeking out peers who share a common language, country, or application of mindfulness was highlighted as a way to enrich the learning experience.

- The program was described as containing extraordinarily rich material from which students can benefit greatly by drawing on the knowledge and perspectives of fellow participants.

- Shelly echoed this sentiment, noting that she had also planned to highlight community connection as her key piece of advice, having anticipated wanting to join a group of like-minded people on a shared path.

- The depth and richness of the connections that can form within the program were described as far exceeding initial expectations, with the capacity for expanding relationships proving to be one of the most meaningful aspects of the experience.

### The Importance of Savoring the Learning Journey

- Shelly reflected that she wished she had been more conscious from the outset of how quickly the two years would pass, as the program felt like a long trajectory at the start but moved very fast in practice.

- Savoring each teaching, module, and interaction was identified as a key mindset to cultivate, given the precious and transformative nature of the program.

- Approaching the experience with intentional presence and appreciation was recommended as a way to fully absorb the depth of what is being offered throughout the curriculum.

### Unexpected Learning About Therapeutic Presence

- Renju shared that one of the most unexpected and profound things he learned from the program was the concept of therapeutic presence, which represented a significant shift from his prior training.

- Standing alongside pain and suffering with gentleness, kindness, and compassion was described as having a profound healing power that goes beyond intellectual understanding.

- The ability to behold and remain present with suffering, rather than trying to resolve or escape it, was identified as a tremendous transformation in both personal practice and teaching approach.

### Adapting Teaching to Hold Space for Real-World Complexity

- Sarah Marie described the challenge of designing and delivering her practicum during the multiple crises of 2020 as one of the most stretching experiences of the program.

- A key learning from that process was the importance of creating a teaching space that could hold all of what students were experiencing, including joy, pain, and grief, rather than adhering rigidly to a planned curriculum.

- Allowing herself to be fully human in the teaching space, bringing her own experience into the room, and acknowledging her own growth edges were identified as more impactful than any specific content or checklist of curriculum points.

- The willingness to say "I don't know" was highlighted as an essential quality for authentic and effective teaching.

- Anne noted that the cohort had been required to pivot entirely to online practicums due to the pandemic, and praised the adaptability and resilience that the graduates demonstrated in response to that unexpected shift.

### Approaching the Curriculum as a Learner Without Getting Overwhelmed

- Veronica recommended treating each module with seriousness and giving it sufficient time and attention rather than allowing material to accumulate.

- A practical approach she used was to listen to the teachings, read the transcripts, highlight key points, and ensure thorough understanding before moving on.

- Practicing the material as soon as possible, first independently and then with others, was described as the most effective way to digest and truly understand the content.

- Following the pace set by the program and keeping up with required readings was advised as a way to avoid falling behind and facing an overwhelming catch-up workload.

- Embodying the practices and teachings, rather than simply absorbing them intellectually, was identified as the essential step that transforms theoretical knowledge into lived understanding.

### Mindfulness as a Tool for Liberation and Anti-Racism Work

- Shelly described spending her career focused on the mental health of diverse populations, particularly BIPOC communities, and coming to mindfulness through that lens.

- A key tension she identified was personally experiencing mindfulness as empowering and liberating while also observing that mainstream mindfulness spaces and retreats did not always speak to her lived experience as a person of color.

- Mindfulness-oriented therapies were noted as sometimes falling flat with people of color in therapeutic settings, prompting a desire to bridge the gap between the practice's potential and its cultural accessibility.

- Her practicum and related workshops were developed to explicitly connect mindfulness practice with the work of liberation, anti-racism, and decolonization of the self.

- A quote from Thich Nhat Hanh was referenced to illustrate the connection between inner seeing and outer action, emphasizing that mindfulness must lead beyond awareness to engaged response.

- Angela Davis's call to liberate minds as well as society was cited as a guiding framework for how mindfulness material can be presented through a liberation lens.

- The concept of detachment was identified as culturally problematic in some contexts, and an alternative emphasis on deepening connection, both to one's own inner experience and to others, was proposed as more resonant for BIPOC populations.

- Framing mindfulness consistently as being in service of personal and collective liberation was described as a central organizing principle of her teaching approach.

### The Role of Peer Groups and Mentor Groups in Deepening Practice

- Renju described the monthly peer group and mentor group meetings as a distinctly new kind of learning experience compared to the more independent study typical of monastic training.

- Rotating roles within the peer group, including teacher, student, and observer, were highlighted as a structured way to develop a range of skills across the two years.

- Receiving positive and constructive feedback in a kind, gentle, and non-judgmental manner was identified as a powerful mechanism for building the ability to communicate mindfulness concepts clearly, simply, and accessibly.

- The peer group process of leading a practice, receiving feedback, and collectively identifying areas for improvement and strength was described as directly informing how participants develop their practicum teaching.

- Shelly noted that her peer group, which named itself the "Meta Queens," continued to meet even after the program ended, illustrating the lasting depth of connection that can develop.

- Cultivating vulnerability and openness to not-knowing within the peer group was described as a key condition for meaningful growth through community.

### Leadership Qualities Cultivated Through Mindfulness and the MMTCP Program

- Sarah Marie drew on Einstein's observation that no problem can be solved from the same level of consciousness that created it to frame mindfulness as a pathway to conscious leadership.

- The capacity to pause and respond rather than react was identified as a foundational leadership quality developed through mindfulness practice.

- Greater self-awareness, including the ability to work with fear-based thoughts, biases, and blind spots, was described as enabling leaders to get out of their own way and dismantle internalized systems of oppression.

- Cultivating kindness and compassion for oneself and others, seeing the good in people, and building relationships across difference were highlighted as outcomes of sustained mindfulness practice.

- Resilience in the face of adversity, better stress management, and an expanded capacity to meet life's challenges with compassion and insight were listed as further leadership qualities the program supports.

- Maintaining perspective, staying grounded in values and intentions, and holding the ability to imagine a meaningful future were described as qualities that mindfulness helps leaders sustain.

- Balancing humility with confidence, directing energy and attention where it matters most, and experiencing greater fulfillment and aliveness in one's work were identified as additional dimensions of conscious leadership cultivated through the program.

### Strategies for Staying Organized Throughout the Program

- Renju advised against allowing module content to accumulate, warning that reviewing large amounts of material immediately before a mentor group meeting creates unnecessary stress.

- Breaking the weekly content into small daily portions, such as ten minutes of watching and note-taking per day, was recommended as a sustainable approach to keeping pace with the curriculum.

- For participants whose first language is not English, translating key points into one's native language was suggested as both a comprehension aid and a practical resource for future teaching.

- Capturing short, meaningful phrases, poems, or quotes from the teachings and translating them for use with students was described as a particularly valuable and beautiful practice.

### Finding and Designing a Practicum That Feels Authentic

- Veronica noted that rather than drawing on other people's practicum ideas, the most important approach is to reflect intuitively on how one would introduce mindfulness to someone who knows nothing about it.

- Sitting in silence and asking oneself how to take the smallest, most accessible first steps with a complete beginner was described as a reliable way to arrive at an authentic practicum design.

- Participants were reassured that students will receive what the teacher genuinely feels and embodies, and will not be aware of anything that was left out or not covered.

- Focusing on a few core ideas and transmitting them with full presence and authenticity was recommended over attempting to deliver a comprehensive or borrowed curriculum.

### Advice on What to Do Differently in Hindsight

- Sarah Marie recommended beginning from the very first module to capture stories, quotes, and poetry encountered throughout the curriculum in a dedicated database for future teaching use.

- Keeping a running log of personal stories and life experiences that could be meaningfully integrated into teaching was identified as a proactive habit that would have saved significant time later in the program.

- Being more intentional from the outset about building a personal library of teaching resources was described as the single most useful change she would make if starting the program again.

### Balancing Zoom Fatigue With Program Engagement

- Veronica encouraged incoming students to embrace Zoom as a normal part of contemporary life and professional practice rather than resisting it, noting that familiarity comes through consistent use.

- Getting comfortable with the specific device and setup being used, including ensuring good audio quality for bells and other elements, was described as a practical step toward feeling at ease in online teaching.

- Veronica also suggested that participants who share a language form small groups to collaboratively translate key terms, such as "loving kindness," into their native language to support fluency and confidence in their own teaching.

- Shelly highlighted that Zoom fatigue often stems from the narrow sensory focus of screen-based interaction and recommended introducing other sensory experiences during breaks, such as scented lotion or incense, to make the environment feel more alive and grounded.

### Time Commitment Required for the Program Each Week

- Renju estimated spending approximately one hour per day watching module content and an additional two to three hours per week on reading, alongside a daily personal meditation practice.

- Veronica described committing to the program daily without measuring time precisely, estimating roughly one hour per day inclusive of practice, listening to teachings, and reading transcripts.

- Shelly acknowledged that her weekly time investment varied significantly, ranging from one to four hours depending on workload, and encouraged students to give themselves permission for flexibility rather than striving for rigid consistency.

- Sarah Marie noted a meaningful difference between the first and second years, estimating approximately five hours per week during the curriculum year and significantly more, sometimes ten to fifteen hours per week, during the practicum year when deeper engagement with the modules was required.

- All panelists agreed that the program is designed with sufficient flexibility to accommodate varying life circumstances and that falling behind occasionally does not undermine the overall learning experience.

### Next Steps

- Incoming students were encouraged to prioritize building community connections with peers from the outset of the program as a core strategy for maximizing the learning experience.

- A dedicated session on how to make the most of peer group and mentor group opportunities was noted as forthcoming in the program.

- Participants were invited to submit questions to the panel via the chat for the remainder of the session, with the Sounds True team facilitating the flow of questions to the panelists.

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- **Zoom fatigue:** Shelly Harrell identified Zoom as presenting a challenge due to its singular sensory focus, which can lead to exhaustion. She recommended engaging additional senses — scented lotion, incense, tactile objects — to counteract this during and between sessions.
- **Falling behind in the program:** Shelly Harrell acknowledged that during particularly busy weeks at work, she fell behind on the material. She encouraged students to give themselves permission for flexibility and to return to the material when possible.
- **Teaching during crisis:** Sarah Marie Huff described the mental and logistical challenge of designing and delivering a practicum during the multiple crises of 2020, including the sudden requirement to shift to online teaching. She described learning to allow space for grief, pain, and uncertainty in the teaching environment.
- **Information overload:** Renju cautioned against accumulating unwatched modules and then facing a large backlog before mentor group meetings, describing this as potentially leading to a mental breakdown. He recommended breaking content into small daily portions.
- **Language barriers:** Renju noted that for non-native English speakers, additional time is needed to translate key concepts into one's own language. Veronica similarly noted the challenge of translating terms like "loving kindness" into Spanish and recommended collaborative translation sessions with others who share your language.
