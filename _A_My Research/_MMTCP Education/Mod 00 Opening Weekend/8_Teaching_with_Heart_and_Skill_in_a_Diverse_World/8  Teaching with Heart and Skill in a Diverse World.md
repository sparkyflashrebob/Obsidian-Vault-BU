#### Outline

#### Introduction of the DEIA Team and Program Overview

- Diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility (DEIA) has been introduced as a fully integrated and central component of the MMTCP mindfulness teacher training program, rather than a separate track.
- Kitsie, based in Berkeley, California, shared that her passion for racial justice became more effective after encountering a structured training framework led by Hilda Gutierrez-Beldequin, which gave her the understanding and skills to engage more transformatively.
- Crystal Johnson, calling in from the Mendocino Coast in Northern California, described her focus as teaching white people to develop mindfulness of their social conditioning, particularly around whiteness and the harm it causes when unexamined.
- Both Kitsie and Crystal are associated with the East Bay Meditation Center in Oakland and Spirit Rock Meditation Center in Marin, where they conduct similar trainings.
- Kate Johnson, joining briefly from Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, described her background as mixed race with a white mother from Missouri and an Afro-Honduran father, and explained that the question of how people with vastly different lived experiences can coexist brought her to the Dharma.
- Her professional work as a meditation teacher and organizational consultant focuses on strategic planning and systemic change related to equity and inclusion in education and healthcare settings.
- The program is described as innovative and breakthrough in that the DEIA training is deeply woven into the fabric of the mindfulness curriculum rather than existing as a standalone module.

#### Personal Journeys of Racial Awakening Among Program Leaders

- Tara Brach described her Washington DC meditation community as predominantly white, and recounted years of hearing from people of color about feeling excluded and experiencing microaggressions, with early diversity efforts feeling obligatory and lacking impact.
- A significant shift occurred approximately eight years ago when she participated in a year-long white awareness training conducted exclusively with other white people, which provided a safe container to unpack shame, confusion, and embarrassment around racism.
- Participating in a group setting rather than learning individually was identified as essential, as the nature of whiteness as a form of trance meant that she could not recognize what she did not know on her own.
- Specific examples shared in the group, such as a colleague unconsciously viewing a Black coworker as exceptional rather than expected, and feeling fear when passing young Black men at night, prompted recognition of internalized biases.
- Disparities in healthcare access were cited as a deeply personal example, contrasting her own ease of access with a close friend of color whose pain was dismissed by doctors.
- Retrospectively, Tara acknowledged that the early MMTCP program, while consulting teachers of color, was still hierarchically led by two white people and lacked genuine racially diverse partnership at the leadership level.
- Jack Kornfield honored the Asian roots of the mindfulness teachings, acknowledging 150 years of Asian teachers who brought these practices to the West before the white convert community became involved.
- An early attempt to offer a day of teachings for people of color at a church in Oakland revealed to Jack that the attendees were not beginners but experienced practitioners who simply felt uncomfortable in predominantly white meditation spaces.
- A Black participant at that event expressed profound relief at finding community, describing himself as the only Black vegetarian Buddhist doctor he knew, which illustrated to Jack how invisible and isolated people of color felt in mainstream Western Buddhist spaces.
- A later experience with Marlene Jones, who felt uncomfortable as the only Black person in a sea of white meditators, deepened his understanding of how structurally unwelcoming those spaces were, and she later became a teacher herself.
- Decades of leading retreats for activists of color, working with gang-involved youth in Oakland, Chicago, and Los Angeles, and conducting prison work further shaped his understanding of how race and caste produce systemic suffering through lack of education, healthcare, housing, and dignity.
- An encounter with a formerly incarcerated young man named Amos, who stood waiting for permission to cross the street after years of incarceration, illustrated for Jack the profound loss of autonomy produced by the prison system.
- Race and caste were identified as the core wound of Western culture, with the suffering they produce seen as inseparable from the Bodhisattva vow to alleviate suffering for all beings.

#### The Relationship Between Racial Awareness and Mindfulness Practice

- Conda Mason posed the question of why racial awareness cannot be separated from mindfulness, referencing scholar John Powell's framing that race is like gravity in that everyone is affected by it but few truly understand it.
- Hierarchy as a structuring principle of human societies, including the ranking of humans over other species and over the planet, was identified as a root cause of immense suffering, with race and skin color serving as primary axes of that hierarchy.
- Colorism, the privileging of lighter skin tones even within the same racial group, was described as a worldwide phenomenon that compounds racial oppression.
- Mindfulness practice, which is fundamentally oriented toward recognizing and alleviating suffering, was argued to be incomplete without an understanding of race-based suffering, which operates at an ancestral and systemic level.
- For BIPOC practitioners, knowing that white counterparts are actively engaged in this learning creates a sense of safety and trust, allowing for more authentic relationships within sanghas and teaching communities.
- The book Caste by Isabel Wilkerson was highlighted as a resource that brings the systemic nature of racial hierarchy into clear relief, demonstrating that caste is not only an Indian phenomenon but is deeply embedded in American society through race.
- Systemic caste was defined as a built-in structure within institutions that determines differential access to healthcare, education, housing, and opportunity based on racial identity.

#### Understanding Whiteness as a Constructed Social Identity

- Crystal Johnson explained that whiteness is not a skin color but a story that began gaining momentum in the 15th century when European powers sought land and wealth outside Europe, justified by the Doctrine of Discovery.
- The Doctrine of Discovery, sanctioned by papal authority, permitted Christian explorers to take land, enslave, and kill non-Christian peoples, and its ideology of white privilege was described as still very much alive today.
- Being trained into whiteness means that white people are assigned a role within a system of privilege and oppression, and without mindfulness of that conditioning, they mistake the role for their authentic identity.
- A key insight offered was that the system of white supremacy requires the cooperation of white people, and that developing awareness creates the possibility of withdrawing that cooperation.
- Mindfulness practice was framed as directly applicable to this work because it cultivates the capacity to see clearly, let go of conditioned identities, and turn toward rather than away from suffering.
- Denying or ignoring the suffering caused by white supremacy requires a willful shutting down of awareness that alienates white people from themselves and from others, making whiteness a form of self-imposed limitation.
- The potential for white people in this work is described as genuine freedom, not only personal liberation but a contribution to the liberation of others, aligned with the Bodhisattva commitment to relieve the suffering of all beings.
- Crystal described the experience of being decentered in mixed-race communities as an important and necessary practice for white teachers, requiring conscious examination of assumptions about what is good, right, or normal.
- Discomfort with not knowing, and the recognition of that discomfort as a sign of genuine learning, was identified as a marker of progress rather than failure for white practitioners and teachers.

#### The Emotional and Psychological Challenges of Racial Awareness Work for White People

- Tara Brach described a pattern of moving from guilt to heartbreak as central to her ongoing growth, in which the initial feeling of being a bad person gives way to a deeper pain of separation and grief for the suffering caused across generations.
- Resistance to this work was identified as rooted in the desire to appear good, to be seen as a dedicated ally, and to seek approval, all of which are signs of avoiding the discomfort of genuine imperfection.
- Jack Kornfield acknowledged the confusion that arises around whether living a privileged life is something to be ashamed of, ultimately concluding that the more meaningful question is how one chooses to use what has been given.
- Ongoing learning about evolving language, such as the shift from people of color to BIPOC, was described as each step opening new understanding rather than being a burden.
- Expanding awareness beyond race to include gender identity, sexual orientation, disability, and other axes of marginalization was described as part of a continuing process of waking up to the full scope of oppression.
- Fatigue was acknowledged as a real experience in this work, with the recognition that the Dharma is simultaneously difficult and profoundly awakening.
- Kitsie noted that white people often fail to notice the structural advantages they receive precisely because they do not rub against the system, making whiteness invisible to those it benefits.
- Many white people do not feel individually privileged, particularly those who grew up in poverty, and the challenge of the work is to acknowledge systemic privilege even when it does not match personal experience.
- A common and painful response for white people is being told they have caused harm, which triggers defensiveness because racism has been conflated with personal moral worth rather than understood as an inevitable product of socialization in a racist society.
- Crystal added that overwhelm is a frequent response to racial awareness training, arising from the mistaken sense that one's identity is under attack rather than the delusion being challenged.
- Racism was clarified as a multi-level system rather than a matter of personal feelings, meaning that a person can be non-racist in their intentions and still benefit from and perpetuate systemic privilege without any deliberate action.

#### The Global Relevance of Race and Hierarchy

- Crystal acknowledged that the training's focus on race emerges from the specific history of the United States, including the genocide of Indigenous peoples, the theft of land, and the enslavement of Africans, and that other countries carry different but related histories of how power and wealth became inequitably distributed.
- The principles of privilege and oppression explored through the lens of race were offered as transferable frameworks for understanding dynamics of ethnicity, gender, class, and other axes of hierarchy in different national contexts.
- Conda Mason emphasized that hierarchy has been a feature of world history from its earliest recorded periods, and that no country or culture is exempt from its dynamics.
- The designations of global north and global south were offered as an example of how racial hierarchy is embedded in everyday language, with the global north associated with lighter-skinned populations and greater wealth, and the global south with darker-skinned populations and less access.
- Colorism within racially homogeneous countries was identified as a near-universal pattern in which lighter skin correlates with higher class status and greater access to resources.
- American media was described as a powerful global export of racial stereotypes, shaping perceptions of Black and Brown Americans worldwide regardless of whether viewers are conscious of absorbing those images.
- The invitation was extended to international participants to examine how these dynamics manifest in their own cultural contexts rather than dismissing the training as irrelevant to their experience.

#### The Structure and Content of the DEIA Training Program

- The DEIA training is structured around two parallel tracks, one for white participants and one for BIPOC participants, with separate recorded monthly training sessions beginning in June and running through November.
- Each recorded training session is accompanied by homework assignments, and participants will meet in small affinity home groups to share reactions and complete specific integration activities.
- Affinity home groups are distinct from mentor groups and peer groups within the broader MMTCP program and are a required component of the DEIA training.
- Beginning in March, elective collective racial affinity groups will be offered every other week for both BIPOC and white participants, scheduled at varying times to accommodate different time zones, and these will continue throughout the full two years of the program.
- The elective groups are designed to allow participants to raise questions, share reflections, and deepen their engagement with the material on a flexible, drop-in basis.
- Three core competency areas structure the training across both tracks, covering DEIA content knowledge, relational skills for navigating harm and repair, and facilitation skills for holding diverse groups.
- The BIPOC training modules address the history of people of color, the four levels of race-based oppression, building solidarity between BIPOC communities, resilience as a teacher, navigating racial microaggressions, teacher identity, building agency, and centering one's own culture in teaching practice.
- The white participant training modules cover agreements for multicultural interactions, the invention of whiteness, the dynamics of race-based discrimination as a systemic phenomenon, the challenge of being a good white person, how to respond when called out, skills for calling other white people in, unpacking white supremacy culture, and facilitation practices for diverse communities.
- Participants who do not identify clearly within either the white or BIPOC category were acknowledged as facing a false binary, and plans were described as being developed to address their specific situations through additional outreach and support.

#### Somatic Mindfulness Exercise on Racial Conditioning

- Crystal Johnson introduced a mindfulness exercise designed to develop awareness of social and racial conditioning as it manifests in the body, based on the premise that conditioned responses register somatically even when they are not accessible to conscious thought.
- Participants were guided through a body scan and breath awareness practice to establish a relaxed and alert state before the exercise began.
- A series of fifteen words and phrases were read aloud, including friendship, immigrants, politics, love, white supremacy, peace, police brutality, Black Lives Matter, ethnic cleansing, and law and order, with the invitation to notice bodily sensations rather than verbal or conceptual reactions.
- Participants were asked to observe which words evoked the strongest somatic response, with the emphasis that there was no correct reaction and the purpose was simply to notice what arose.
- Responses shared in the chat included chest clenching at ethnic cleansing, throat tightening at law and order, grief at genocide, core shivering and retraction at white supremacy, and progressive difficulty moving into openness as the list continued.
- Participants from Durham, Denver, Santa Monica, Palm Springs, Los Angeles, and Harare, Zimbabwe contributed responses, illustrating the global reach of these conditioned reactions.
- The exercise was framed as a demonstration of how mindfulness practice provides access to embodied knowing that can serve as a foundation for engaging with racial and social conditioning.
- Staying present with the bodily sensations of contraction or discomfort, rather than moving away from them, was identified as a core mindfulness skill applicable to this domain of practice.

#### The Gifts and Transformative Potential of This Work

- Conda Mason described the ultimate gift of racial awareness work as liberation, drawing an analogy to the process of facing and working through personal trauma, which is painful in the process but freeing on the other side.
- Witnessing white friends and colleagues move through this training and develop greater ease with their own mistakes, shifting from horror to a more grounded acknowledgment of ongoing learning, was offered as evidence of its transformative effect.
- For BIPOC practitioners, being in community with white people who are actively doing this work creates a sense of safety, trust, and the possibility of authentic relationship.
- Tara Brach reflected that engaging deeply with this work has produced a greater sense of connection to all beings than she has ever felt, and that investigating the inner architecture of oppression, which mirrors the way individuals turn against themselves, leads to healing on both personal and societal levels.
- Jack Kornfield emphasized that this training will make participants more effective mindfulness teachers by equipping them to see, hold, and honor people of all backgrounds, to navigate their own mistakes, and to bring genuine warmth and understanding into their teaching.
- The integration of DEIA into teacher training was described as producing the kind of teachers the world most needs, ones who can celebrate human diversity and bring it into the heart of their practice and community.
- Kitsie noted that individual teachers modeling inclusive, compassionate, and genuinely open engagement in their communities are actively sowing new seeds within the system, even when the broader structural impact is not immediately visible.

#### Next Steps

- Elective collective racial affinity groups for both tracks will begin in March and continue biweekly throughout the full two years of the program, offered at multiple times to accommodate global time zones.

### Stories

**Tara Brach — White Awareness Training Group**

Tara described participating in a year-long white awareness training approximately eight years prior. In the group, a woman shared how she was impressed by a Black colleague's intelligence and leadership, then realized she had considered him exceptional because she had not expected a Black person to be so capable. Another participant described feeling fear when passing a group of young Black men on the subway at night. Tara described feeling horror and recognition — that these biases were present in her as well.

**Tara Brach — Mixed-Race Three-Year Group**

Tara described participating in a three-year group that was mixed in race, gender identity, and sexual orientation. As people from non-dominant groups shared their suffering, she became increasingly self-conscious and stilted in her participation. Upon reflection, she realized she was deeply uncomfortable with feeling like a bad person and was taking systemic critique personally. Staying with that discomfort, she found beneath it a pain of separation caused by whiteness, followed by grief for the suffering of generations.

**Jack Kornfield — Teaching at a Church in Oakland**

Jack described organizing a day of teaching for people of color at the church of Reverend E. in Oakland, thinking the teachings of compassion and steadiness would be helpful to a marginalized community. When he arrived, he found approximately fifty to sixty people, nearly all of whom had years of yoga or meditation practice. They told him they practiced but were not comfortable in white meditation centers. One Black man stood up and said he felt he had found his people — he was the only Black, vegetarian, Buddhist doctor he knew. Jack described realizing he had not understood the territory at all.

**Jack Kornfield — Marlene Jones**

Jack described a Black woman named Marlene Jones who came to his Monday night class of three to four hundred people. She told him she liked the teachings but felt uncomfortable as the only Black person in a sea of white practitioners. Jack arranged for her to sit near the front, next to him, so she would not have to see the crowd behind her. She later became a teacher and they remained deeply connected. Jack described this as an early lesson in how hard it was for the community to become truly inclusive.

**Jack Kornfield — Retreats for Activists of Color**

Jack described leading retreats for activists of color over decades, doing counseling at night and listening to stories of suffering. He described weeping and feeling compelled to make the teachings available to everyone.

**Jack Kornfield — Young Men from Youth Gangs in Marin**

Jack described accompanying young men he knew from gang intervention work in Oakland, Chicago, and Los Angeles on a visit to a town near Spirit Rock in Marin County. He watched shopkeepers follow the young men as if they were going to steal, and people stare and back away. He described knowing these men personally — Tito, RJ — and seeing them as beautiful men, while witnessing how they lived their lives under constant fear and hostility from others.

**Jack Kornfield — Amos and the Dalai Lama**

Jack described a conference on prison work where the Dalai Lama asked how people ended up in prison. A man named Amos responded that the Dalai Lama did not understand — that in his community, prison was where nearly everyone went. Amos described joining a gang to avoid being a victim, being sent to juvenile hall and then prison, and upon release at age 23, standing at a street corner waiting for someone to tell him it was okay to cross because he had never had the freedom to make his own decisions.

**Crystal Johnson — Swedish Immigrant Ancestors and the Price of Whiteness**

Crystal described her ancestors, mostly from Sweden and Northern Europe, arriving in America as economically desperate immigrants and being invited into the system of whiteness. She referenced James Baldwin's concept of the price of the ticket — that these immigrants gained access to better jobs, housing, and healthcare in exchange for not caring about the exclusion and violence directed at Black, Chinese, and Mexican people. She described this as a form of willful ignorance that alienated her ancestors from themselves and others.

**Kitsie — White Anti-Racist Practice Group**

Kitsie described a recent gathering of white people working on anti-racist practice where she and others, when asked to describe their identities, left out whiteness entirely. They named being women, being gay, where they grew up — but not white — because whiteness was the invisible norm they had never rubbed against.
