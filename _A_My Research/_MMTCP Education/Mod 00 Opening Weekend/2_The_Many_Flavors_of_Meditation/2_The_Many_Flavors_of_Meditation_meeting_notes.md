### Outline
### Defining Meditation and Its Many Forms

- Described as a deliberate focusing of attention, meditation is a caring way to train and direct the mind and heart, helping to steady the mind, listen deeply, and open perspective beyond separate identity.

- Just as the word "sport" covers vastly different activities, the word "meditation" is commonly used as if it refers to a single practice, when in fact it encompasses many distinct forms.

- Devotional practice focuses on inspiration, love, worship, prayer, song, movement, and ecstatic connection with the divine.

- Quiet contemplation involves engaging with passages, prayer, words, and scripture in a reflective and still manner.

- Concentration practices develop inner focus and unified attention through visual objects such as candle flames, sacred images, mandalas, nature, or deities.

- Mantra practice uses sacred repetition to concentrate the mind, with many students likely having prior experience in traditions such as Transcendental Meditation.

- Universal mindfulness practices focus on the breath, the body, and heart states including compassion, love, joy, gratitude, and forgiveness, all serving the shift from a separate self toward a more spacious connection with reality.

### The Nature and Purpose of Mindfulness

- Mindfulness, also referred to as insight meditation or Vipassana, is the most central practice to be taught throughout the program.

- Caring attention without judgment, modeled by the story of primatologist George Schaller who observed gorillas peacefully by approaching without threat and simply sitting, listening, and watching, illustrates the essence of mindful presence.

- Loving awareness, as a description of mindfulness, captures its quality of focused, non-threatening, present-moment attention that allows experience to unfold naturally.

- Mindfulness shifts practitioners from reactivity and being caught in habitual patterns toward a more spacious, vivid, and balanced presence.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's concept of the miracle of mindfulness was demonstrated powerfully when his mindful walking at Spirit Rock brought an audience of 3,000 people into a collective state of presence and stillness.

- Participants are invited to write their own definition of mindfulness and to continue refining it over the two years of the program.

- A working definition offered is "a non-judgmental caring attention here and now," described in traditional teachings as universally helpful.

- Mindfulness brings graciousness to all areas of life including parenting, business, lovemaking, gardening, community, art, education, and health, and opens practitioners to freedom.

### Neuroscience Research Supporting Mindfulness

- Over 8,000 published research papers now document the measurable effects of mindfulness practice on human wellbeing.

- Findings show that mindfulness improves the ability to focus attention and brings greater emotional regulation and inner stability.

- Research also demonstrates that practice reduces anxiety and depression, enhances concentration, and supports physical healing.

- This body of evidence is presented as a valuable resource for teachers to share with students who need to understand or build trust in the practice.

### Mindfulness as a Gateway to Freedom

- A moment of mindfulness, when genuinely understood and applied, is described as a moment of freedom.

- The example of a woman who had struggled with bulimia since adolescence illustrates how mindfulness created an attentive inner distance from compulsive patterns, allowing her to recognize she no longer had to follow the same painful path.

- Teaching typically begins by stabilizing attention through the breath, body scan, or listening to sound, before opening to feelings, sensations, emotions, and insights.

- Multiple traditions and methods of teaching Vipassana exist, including body scan, noticing impermanence, watching the movement of mind, and incorporating walking and bodily movement, all of which will be part of the training.

- The goal of practice is captured in the image of Swami Satchitananda surfing in tree pose, accompanied by the phrase "you can't stop the waves, but you can learn to surf," pointing to the capacity to be with the changing vicissitudes of life.

- A dying student's reflection that every difficult moment in practice, every boredom, fear, pain, and unscratched itch, was a training for kindness and the trusting spirit that carried her through death, illustrates the profound long-term value of sustained practice.

### The Two Wings of Practice: Mindfulness and Compassion

- Buddhist tradition likens the two essential qualities of practice, clear seeing and an open heart, to two wings of a bird, both of which are necessary for flight and freedom.

- A classical story of a monk and a nun, each seeking what the other embodied, illustrates that compassion and wisdom are inseparable and mutually necessary for awakening.

- These two wings also reflect the masculine and feminine archetypes, and the historical neglect of the feminine in early Western transplantation of meditation led to poor guidance around emotions, relationships, and the sacred feminine.

- When the feminine is suppressed, practitioners become trapped in judgment, aggression, and separation, while without the masculine, confusion, grasping, and distortion arise.

- Meditation is described as a form of spiritual reparenting, healing the wounds of not being clearly seen or loved by learning to bring clear seeing and kindness inwardly to those wounded places.

- The healing power of being truly seen is illustrated by a patient who would visit the threshold of her doctor's room on days he was absent, stepping in and out repeatedly, because the places where we are seen and cared for become holy places.

- An infant's most fundamental need, to be clearly mirrored and then loved for what is seen, reflects the core of what meditation practice offers inwardly.

- Meditation begins to repair the internalized messages of not being valued, particularly for non-dominant populations such as Black, Indigenous, and people of color who regularly receive societal messages of invisibility and inferiority.

### Skillful Means and Working with Difficulty

- A long-time meditator and therapist who froze before a presentation due to early Alzheimer's demonstrated skillful means by pausing, placing his palms together, and naming his experience aloud with bowing, which moved the audience deeply.

- Pausing before reactivity, naming what is present as the wing of mindfulness, and bowing as the wing of the heart together constitute a powerful and teachable response to difficulty.

- One Tibetan teacher's instruction to "meet your edge and soften" encapsulates the essence of what is taught, seeing what is here and letting it be here with tenderness and radical acceptance.

- Meeting one's edge requires courage, but it is not always wise or possible to go directly into strong emotions or trauma, and in such cases skillful means must first establish balance, safety, and resourcefulness.

- The principle that "it is not survival of the fittest but survival of the nurtured" underscores the importance of grounding, connecting, and offering kindness before attempting to work directly with overwhelming material.

### Non-Doing and the Deepest Dimension of Practice

- Beyond all directed techniques lies the dimension of simply being, a pure allowing presence that is the ultimate orientation of wise practice.

- Practitioners often become habituated to doing strategies, cycling through techniques such as metta, body scan, and breath focus, forgetting that the goal is not to get back to the breath but to awaken to what is already here.

- As long as there is a sense of trying to make something happen or improve the meditation, the fullness of what is always already present cannot be fully opened to.

- Ajahn Chah's instruction "do not try to become anything, do not be a meditator, do not become enlightened, when you sit let it be, when you walk let it be, grasp at nothing, resist nothing" is offered as a guiding teaching for this dimension.

- Gentle inner phrases such as "just stop," "let it drop," "surrender," and "surrender the surrendering" are used as kind reminders to release the chronic subtle doing mode.

- Nisargadatta Maharaj's description of discovering oneself as the watcher, and then as the light behind the watcher, the source of knowing and loving, points to the true nature that becomes accessible in non-doing.

- Wise effort is characterized by attention that is energetic and engaged but not tight or striving, with the pure intention of relaxing back into the awareness and love that is always already present.

### Mindfulness and the Sacred

- Mindfulness opens practitioners to vastness, mystery, and a sense of the sacred that cannot always be taught directly but arises naturally through presence.

- Lighting a candle and inviting quiet reflection on the deepest values of the heart before a business meeting with Google executives created a sense of sacred space in an unexpected setting.

- An Ojibwa saying, "sometimes I go about pitying myself when all the while I am being carried by great winds across the sky," reflects the way mindfulness reconnects people with something greater than the separate self.

- Profound moments of presence, such as being with someone dying, witnessing a birth, hearing sacred music, walking in mountains, or taking sacred medicine, point to the extraordinary nature of paying attention to this human life.

- Thomas Merton's phrase "the secret beauty behind the eyes of all beings" is offered as an expression of what loving awareness ultimately reveals.

- Meditation is described as returning practitioners to the sacredness of life and to the understanding that awakening is possible amidst suffering, and that wherever one is can become a place of freedom and ease.

### Next Steps

- Participants are to continue refining their personal definition of mindfulness over the course of the two-year program.

- A short group meditation sitting is to be followed by individual journaling on personal practice, including what skillful means are supportive and the quality of effort being brought to practice.

- Paired sharing sessions are to be conducted, with each partner holding silent, non-judgmental presence for four minutes while the other shares from their journaling, then switching roles.

- Following the break, participants are to reconvene to meet the mentor leadership team and hear from mentors and previously mentored participants from the prior program.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Many Forms of Meditation**

- Categories include: devotional practice, quiet contemplation, concentration practices, mantra, and universal mindfulness practices
- Traditions represented include Christian, Kabbalah, shamanic, indigenous, Baha'i, Sufi, and Buddhist

- Concentration practices use visual objects such as candle flames, sacred images, mandalas, or nature
- Mantra practices such as TM concentrate the mind through sacred repetition
- Mindfulness practices focus on breath, body, heart states, compassion, love, joy, gratitude, and forgiveness
**Defining Mindfulness**
- Described as a deliberate focusing of attention
- A caring way to train and direct the mind and heart
- Helps steady the mind, listen deeply, and open perspective beyond separate identity
- Defined as a non-judgmental caring attention, here and now
- Traditionally called "all helpful" because it brings presence and graciousness to all areas of life
- Vipassana, or insight meditation, is described as the most central practice
- Mindfulness shifts us from reactivity to a more spacious, vivid presence
**Neuroscience and Research**
- Over 8,000 published research papers on mindfulness

- Teaching typically begins with stabilizing attention through the breath

- Many traditions and methods exist including body scan, noticing impermanence, watching the movement of mind, and walking meditation

**The Two Wings: Mindfulness and Heartfulness**
- Tara Brach describes mindfulness and compassion as two wings of a bird, both necessary for flight and freedom
- The wings reflect masculine and feminine archetypes
- The shadow masculine manifests as judgment, aggression, and separation
- The shadow feminine manifests as confusion, grasping, and distortion
- When meditation was first transplanted to the West, loving-kindness was often compartmentalized as a side practice
- The sacred feminine was not fully present in early Western mindfulness teaching
- Both wings together bring healing and freedom
**Skillful Means and Wise Effort**
- Not always wise or possible to directly meet one's edge, especially with trauma or strong emotions
- In such cases, it is wiser to cultivate resources: grounding, deep breathing, messages of kindness
- The art of practice involves knowing when to resource and when to meet the edge
- Wise effort is energetic and engaged but not tight or striving
- The pure intention behind wise effort is to awaken awareness and love already present
**Non-Doing and Pure Presence**
- The final dimension of practice is stopping all directed attention and simply being

- In moments of non-doing, the light of awareness and true nature can shine through
**The Sacred Dimension**

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Loving Awareness as Core Teaching**
- Loving awareness is woven into practice from the beginning to counter the culture's ambition, judgment, self-judgment, and gaining mind
- Mindfulness misused as a tool to "get somewhere" increases these tendencies rather than healing them
- Loving awareness makes room for all of human experience including conflict, illness, and relational difficulty
**The Sacred Feminine in Practice**
- The suppression of the feminine in early Western mindfulness teaching led to poor guidance on working with emotions and relationships
- Restoring the feminine dimension restores the fullness of the teaching
- The two wings together represent a wholeness that neither masculine nor feminine alone can provide
**Societal Impact and Healing**
- Non-dominant populations experience compounded suffering from societal messages of invisibility, inferiority, and rejection
- Meditation begins to repair inwardly what society has damaged outwardly
- The places where we are seen and cared for are described as holy places that remind us of intrinsic goodness and can transform pain into wisdom
**Awakening Amid Suffering**
- Awakening is described as possible amidst the ocean of tears and the unbearable beauty of life
- Wherever we are can be a place of freedom and ease
- The invitation of the training is to pass this possibility on to students
**The Role of Repetition in Teaching**
- Repetition in teaching is affirmed as good pedagogy, likened to a child asking for the same story again
- Stories are used as vehicles for conveying the essence of practice
**Survival of the Nurtured**
- Luis Cozzolino's framing is cited: it is not survival of the fittest but survival of the nurtured
- Grounding, connecting, and receiving kindness are essential to wellbeing and practice

### Stories
**The Gorilla Biologist**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Professor George Schaller, a primate biologist
- Schaller approached gorillas without weapons, respectfully, and simply sat, listened, and watched
- Other scientists had approached with guns and the gorillas reacted with fear and aggression
- Schaller's unarmed, patient presence allowed the gorillas to go about their lives, enabling him to understand their social structures
- Used as an analogy for the caring, non-threatening attention of mindfulness, described as loving awareness
- Jack notes this story will be repeated in the training and affirms repetition as good pedagogy
**Thich Nhat Hanh at Spirit Rock**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Thich Nhat Hanh
- Three thousand people gathered on a hillside amphitheater at Spirit Rock in Northern California
- Thich Nhat Hanh walked mindfully up the road and his very presence brought the entire crowd to a different level of presence
- A man who had lost his home in a California fire, was dealing with insurance issues, and had moved his three children to a new school said it was the first peace he had felt in many days after witnessing Thich Nhat Hanh's walk
**Tamara Engel**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to a student of mindfulness practice named Tamara Engel
- At the very end of her life she wrote about her gratitude for meditation, including both the joyful and difficult aspects of practice
- She described every difficult sitting, fearful fantasy, pain, and unscratched itch as training for kindness and bearing witness
- Her meditation practice carried her with a trusting spirit even as she faced death
**Mahmoud the Bus Conductor**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to a Pakistani bus conductor named Mahmoud
- At a crowded bus stop he counted out exactly six passengers and let six on, then called to those left behind with warmth
- His words "Plenty of room in my heart but the bus is full" left a row of smiling faces
- Used to illustrate that it is not what you do but the way you do it, and as an example of loving awareness in action
**The Navigator Nainoa Thompson and the Hokulea**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Nainoa Thompson who led the Hokulea Polynesian voyage
- The crew navigated without instruments across the Pacific and around the world
- At one point they were lost at sea for weeks
- Thompson remembered his master's teaching to get quiet
- In the dark and quiet, he visualized pulling the island they sought out of the sea
- The clouds parted in the middle of the night and the moon revealed Rapa Nui in the distance
- Used as an illustration of how meditation allows us to listen in a deep way and return to the sacredness of life
**The Monk and the Nun**
- Shared by Tara Brach
- A monk known as a healer of the heart became exhausted and dispirited after decades of service
- He set out to find a great female teacher known for deep wisdom and investigating the living moment
- Midway on his journey he met an old nun who offered to guide him to the teacher
- Upon arrival he discovered the old nun was the very teacher he sought
- He stayed and learned from her for years; on her deathbed she revealed that on the day they met she too had lost her way and was seeking a great healer
- Used to illustrate that both heart and mind, compassion and wisdom, are needed together
**The Doctor and the Homeless Woman**
- Shared by Tara Brach, citing Rachel Remen, referring to a doctor who met monthly with a homeless woman at his clinic
- The clinic was at the top of a steep hill and the woman had to lash her two carts alternately to parking meters to get up
- Staff discovered she would sometimes come on days when the doctor was not there, go to the threshold of his room, and deliberately place her right foot in and pull it out repeatedly before leaving satisfied
- Used to illustrate that the places where we are seen and cared for are holy places that remind us of intrinsic goodness
**The Man with Early Alzheimer's**
- Shared by Tara Brach, referring to a long-time meditator and therapist she met during a retreat
- Before presenting to a group of approximately one hundred people he froze, having no idea why he was there or what he was supposed to do
- He paused, placed his palms together, and began naming what he was experiencing aloud while bowing after each word
- Many in the group had tears and one person said no one had ever taught them that way
- Used to illustrate the two wings in action: naming experience as mindfulness and bowing as heartfulness
**The Young Man with Childhood PTSD**
- Shared by Tara Brach, referring to a young man encountered during her weekly satsang
- He was the only person in his family to attend college, was struggling with fear of failing, felt guilt about not helping his family during the pandemic, and was paralyzed with racing thoughts
- As he spoke he became increasingly agitated
- Tara guided him to take five deep breaths, ground himself, and receive a message of kindness rather than going directly into the fear
- Used to illustrate the importance of resourcing before meeting the edge, especially with trauma
