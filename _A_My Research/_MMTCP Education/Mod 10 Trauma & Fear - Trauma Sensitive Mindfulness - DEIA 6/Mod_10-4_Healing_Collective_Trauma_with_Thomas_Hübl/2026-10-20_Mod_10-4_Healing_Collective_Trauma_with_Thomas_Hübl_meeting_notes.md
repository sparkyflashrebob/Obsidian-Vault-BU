### Outline
### Introduction to Trauma-Informed Meditation Practice

- Trauma is defined as an overwhelming experience that overloads the computing power of the nervous system, resulting in fragmentation and shutdown.

- Fragmentation creates a state of two-ness, manifesting as either separation, isolation, distancing, indifference, and absencing, or as heightened anxiety, stress, and restlessness.

- Absencing is identified as a particularly significant symptom for meditation practitioners, as it can lead to dissociated states during sitting practice.

- Heightened mental activity and an inability to quiet the mind are understood not as the core problem, but as symptoms of underlying chronic stress operating beneath conscious awareness.

- Stress functions as an energy that activates the mind, meaning that focusing on thinking addresses only the symptom rather than the root cause.

- Many mindfulness, Qigong, and yoga practices are understood to work by down-regulating chronic stress that has become normalized within the nervous system.

### Trauma as Postponed Experience and Its Relationship to Presence

- Trauma is described as postponed experience, an energy that has been split off and never reintegrated, functioning similarly to karma.

- History is not behind us but rather within us, with integrated history manifesting as presence and unintegrated history persisting as the past.

- A clear present moment is understood as the direct result of integrated history, while unclarity in the present moment signals unintegrated or postponed experience.

- When meditation practitioners sit down, the nervous system recognizes the created space as an opportunity to detox the psyche of all that has never been consciously processed.

- Safety and disruption are understood as directly correlated, meaning that as safety increases within a well-facilitated group or meditation practice, more incoherence and disruption will naturally arise.

- Coherence is described as the power to integrate fragmentation, and a trauma-informed facilitator who understands this dynamic is well-equipped to guide practitioners through meditation crises.

- Disruptions in a practitioner's commitment to meditation are understood to relate not to the practice itself but to the content that arises within it.

### The Intelligence of Trauma Responses and the Restoration of Movement

- Trauma responses, including fragmentation, shutdown, and numbing, are framed not as malfunctions but as highly intelligent mechanisms refined over a long period of human experience.

- Healing is defined as the restoration of original movement, with flow states representing the full expression of that original movement.

- Trauma always creates a reduction of movement, generating tension with the original flow of a person's potential.

- Absencing is identified as one of the most important mechanisms impacting meditation, described as the withdrawal of conscious awareness from parts of the nervous system due to pain.

- The human unconscious is compared to a darkened section of a city where the lights have been turned off, meaning that active processes continue in those areas without the self having any conscious reference to them.

- Meditation teachers are encouraged to develop awareness of their own absenced fields, their capacity to recognize absencing in others, and their sensitivity to cultural absencing inherited before birth.

### Collective Trauma and Its Impact on Meditation Practice

- Growing up in post-war Vienna, Thomas Hübl describes experiencing a pervasive cultural numbness and silence that was never explained as trauma, leading to a normalization of wounded states.

- All practitioners are understood to be sitting within inherited collective trauma fields, including the legacies of slavery, Native American genocide, and the Holocaust, regardless of personal history.

- As meditation increases awareness and expands the radius of conscious perception both vertically and horizontally, practitioners inevitably become more aware of these collective fields.

- Collective numbness around events such as the Holocaust or centuries of slavery serves a protective psychological function, yet simultaneously prevents conscious awareness of pain that is always present between people.

- Large-scale group processes have demonstrated that when collective trauma shadows arise in a room, participants can begin to feel what is ordinarily suppressed by collective agreement.

- Expanding meditation practice to include diversity awareness and gender awareness is described as key to increasing the capacity to be present to more of the world and to represent that world as intimacy.

- Intimacy and distance are offered as useful markers in practice, with moments of absence understood as contact with an overwhelmed part of the self rather than as failure.

- Ancestors are described as being within us rather than behind us, with unintegrated ancestry persisting as karmic waves that manifest as difficulties, health issues, and recurring life questions.

### Guided Meditation Practice on Coherence and Relational Sensing

- Participants are guided to take a screenshot of their current physical, emotional, and mental state as a starting point for the practice.

- Attention is directed toward areas of aliveness in the body, including flowing, streaming, and pulsing sensations, which are understood as participation in integrated history.

- Awareness is then extended to areas of tension, tightness, heightened activation, and stress held within the body.

- Participants are invited to notice areas of the body that feel more absent, reduced, or difficult to sense, without any agenda to change them.

- Emotional awareness is introduced by inviting participants to name even a subtle emotion present in the moment, or to notice if the emotional field feels numb or reduced.

- Mental activity is then observed, with attention given to whether the mind feels calm, spacious, busy, activated, or caught in circular thinking.

- The mechanism of switching between physical, emotional, and mental awareness is highlighted as itself an important object of observation.

- Participants are guided to rest in the awareness that is aware of all these layers, asking what is aware right now.

- Upon opening the eyes, participants are invited to extend their self-contact outward to others on screen, practicing relational sensing by allowing the whole body to perceive others' embodied and emotional experience.

- Feeling felt by another person is described as the core building block of relating and the foundation of safety, with the nervous system's capacity to sense being felt referred to in neuroscience as neuroception.

- Presence and attuned relating are identified as superpowers in healing trauma, with the facilitator's open energy field creating an interdependent whole with the group.

### The Role of the Body in Healing Individual and Collective Trauma

- The body is identified as the essential anchor of energetic transformation, with fragmentation occurring within the physical body and healing requiring its direct involvement.

- Healing in the body is described as a process of restoring flow of communication across the nervous system, hormonal system, tissues, and organs.

- From an inner science perspective, trauma healing is understood as the illumination of previously darkened parts of the nervous system, with the self moving back into those areas.

- Spiritual practitioners are encouraged to examine whether they have ever fully incarnated into the body, as full embodiment is not guaranteed simply by physical existence.

- Embodiment and transcendence are described as both required, since transcendence without embodiment can reinforce rather than resolve the symptoms of a person's life.

- When the body becomes more whole, an impulse of deep expansion and relaxation is experienced that simultaneously ripples into ancestral healing and forward into expanded future potential for subsequent generations.

- Trauma healing is framed as a significant learning harvest that benefits the individual, their ancestry, and their descendants, making it a win-win-win process.

### Responding to Personal Trauma Capacity and the Limits of Awareness

- A participant shares that engaging with the traumas of slavery and Native American genocide triggered an awareness of unresolved Holocaust trauma still carried in her body, along with anger at being asked to address additional traumas.

- Thomas Hübl acknowledges the enormity of the Holocaust as something that may never be fully healed quickly, framing the healing process as one of slowly allowing greater intimacy with the experience rather than resolving it entirely.

- Becoming an embodied witness of the Holocaust is described as an extremely difficult goal, requiring the capacity to remain fully present and feeling while confronting the full weight of those events.

- Generational time is recognized as necessary for digesting collective trauma enough to come closer to feeling its enormity, with individual life stories serving as vehicles for that gradual process.

- Moral obligation to address multiple traumas is distinguished from physical and emotional capacity, with the importance of listening to one's own limits emphasized over trying to do what is right.

- Overwhelming oneself with additional trauma is not considered a productive path, and self-regulation is offered as a tool for slowly expanding intimacy with difficult material from a place of resource.

- The humility to acknowledge the edge of one's conscious awareness is described as the beginning of an opening gate, with the prayer "I am grateful for what stays hidden" offered as an expression of that humility.

- Saying no from a grounded place is affirmed as a valid and important act, described as ground zero, provided it is accompanied by the capacity to remain in relationship rather than distancing the other.

### Collective Trauma and the COVID-19 Pandemic

- A distinction is drawn between the pandemic as an event and the crisis as the way in which people respond to it, with the response shaped heavily by pre-existing collective traumatization.

- Experience entering an open nervous system is compared to snowflakes dissolving into a flowing river, while experience landing on a traumatized nervous system accumulates like snow on ice, adding reactivity rather than integration.

- Much of the stress experienced during the pandemic is understood to originate not from COVID itself but from earlier unresolved trauma that the pandemic activates.

- Enrollment in societal fragmentation, such as taking strong political sides or insisting on being right, is identified as a sign that past trauma is disguising itself as present-moment reactivity.

- Mindfulness practice is offered as a tool for recognizing when the past is active and presenting itself as the now, allowing practitioners to return to inner connection rather than charging fragmentation with more energy.

- Relational spaces that hold shared values of presence and mutual support are described as essential for collectively digesting the past so that genuine engagement with current events becomes possible.

- The myth of workplace stress is used to illustrate the broader principle that chronic stress originates in the nervous system and is brought into external environments rather than being generated by them.

### The Nature of Meditation as Participation in Deeper Reality

- Meditation is described as an entry gate into a participation in reality that has different levels of depth, with the great mystics understood as having found access to a dimension of life beyond ordinary reach.

- Participating in that deeper reality requires becoming a different internal environment capable of receiving what that reality offers.

- Rather than framing meditation as something one does through effort alone, it is described as something that descends into a person's life through books, talks, friends, and circumstances.

- Meditation is understood as having always existed and always will exist, with practitioners preparing themselves to receive it rather than simply performing it.

- The image of a mixture between commitment and bowing down to that which already descends into one's life is offered as a way to disarm the striving meditator while maintaining dedication to practice.

- The importance of building relational spaces and social technologies capable of supporting collective trauma healing is recognized as a shared direction for the broader community of practitioners.

- Continued engagement with diversity-aware and embodiment-focused meditation practice is affirmed as a key path for expanding the conscious universe of humanity.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Trauma Defined**
- Trauma occurs when an overwhelming experience overloads the computing power of the nervous system
- This leads to fragmentation and most often a shutdown
- Trauma creates two primary symptom clusters: separation, isolation, distancing, indifference, and absencing on one side; heightened anxiety, stress, and restlessness on the other
- Trauma is described as postponed experience — energy that has been split off and never reintegrated
**History as Internal, Not Behind Us**
- Integrated history is presence; unintegrated history is what we call the past
- The past is not behind us but in us, held as postponed experience awaiting future integration
- Ancestors are not separate in space and time but are present within us; integrated ancestry creates presence, unintegrated ancestry creates karmic waves
**Trauma and Meditation Practice**
- Many meditators enter dissociated or absent states during sitting practice
- Heightened stress and restlessness make it difficult to sit; when space is created, the nervous system uses it to detox accumulated experience
- Thinking is a symptom of underlying stress, not the root issue; the stress is what activates the mind
- Meditation creates coherence, and coherence is the power to integrate fragmentation
- Disruption arising in meditation or group settings is a sign of increased safety, not regression
- Commitment issues in meditation often relate to the content arising, not the practice itself
**Absencing**
- Absencing is the pulling out of sensitivity and conscious awareness from parts of the nervous system due to pain
- The self has no reference point for what is happening in absenced parts, similar to a city district with its lights turned off — life continues there but without awareness
- Meditation teachers need capacity to recognize absencing in themselves and in students
**Collective and Ancestral Trauma**
- All individuals are born into pre-existing collective trauma fields — post-Holocaust society, slavery, Native American genocide
- These fields contribute to collective numbness and suppressed pain that is present between people even when not consciously felt
- Thomas Hübl grew up in Vienna approximately 30 years after World War II, surrounded by trauma symptoms that were normalized and unexplained
- Large-scale group processes have demonstrated that collective trauma shadows can surface in rooms of hundreds or thousands of people
- There is a collective agreement to keep certain pain suppressed; meditation expands awareness into these fields
**Coherence and Safety in Group Healing**
- In large-scale group processes addressing the Holocaust, racism, and genocide, increased group safety consistently produced increased disruption
- A trauma-informed facilitator understands this dynamic and does not interpret disruption as failure
- The facilitator's role is to guide people through meditation crises with this understanding
**The Body and Embodiment**
- Trauma fragmentation happens in the physical body; healing requires the body as anchor
- Healing involves new flow of communication in the nervous system, hormonal system, tissues, and organs
- Full incarnation into the body is not guaranteed simply by being alive; hurt attachment processes can prevent it
- When the body becomes more whole, one impulse heals ancestry and another expands future potential for children
- Embodiment and transcendence are both required; transcendence alone can reinforce symptoms
**Relational Healing and Feeling Felt**
- The core building block of relating is: I feel you, and I feel you feeling me
- Neuroception — feeling felt by another — is the foundation of safety
- Parental sensing of a child is the original model of this safety
- Restoring relational health and the experience of feeling felt is described as one of the superpowers of healing trauma
- A facilitator's open energy field creates an interdependent whole with the group; a defensive or scared facilitator transfers that into the group
**COVID-19 and Pre-Existing Trauma**
- The COVID-19 pandemic is not itself the crisis; the crisis is how pre-existing trauma shapes the response to it
- Experience entering an open nervous system integrates; experience landing on pre-existing trauma compounds it
- Much of the stress experienced during the pandemic is rooted in earlier personal and collective trauma, with COVID activating those older layers
- Societal fragmentation and political reactivity during the pandemic reflect enrollment in collective unconscious dynamics
- Mindfulness practices help practitioners recognize when past trauma is disguising itself as present-moment reactivity
**Meditation as Participation in Deeper Reality**

- Meditation always was and always will be; it descends into people's lives rather than being purely self-generated

### Stories
**Flying into Kathmandu — The Dark City as the Human Unconscious**
- Shared by Thomas Hübl, describing his own experience
- On a flight to Kathmandu bringing a group from a three-year training to the Himalayan mountains for a meditation retreat, Thomas looked down at the city at night and noticed dark patches where the city rotates power outages every four hours
- He could not tell whether a dark spot was a forest, a city, a lake, or a mountain
- This became his explanation for the human unconscious: the absenced parts of the nervous system where conscious awareness has been withdrawn due to pain — the lights are off, but life and process continue there without the self's awareness
**Growing Up in Post-Holocaust Vienna**
- Shared by Thomas Hübl, referring to his own childhood
- Thomas grew up in Vienna approximately 30 years after the Second World War, surrounded by numbness, silence, and strange phenomena
- No one explained to him as a boy that what he was observing were trauma symptoms
- He normalized what he saw in teachers, parents, and grandparents who survived the war
- Looking back, he identifies this as the normalization of collective trauma and the absence of a framework to understand it as woundedness rather than simply how the world is
**Large-Scale Group Processes on Collective Trauma**
- Shared by Thomas Hübl, referring to his own facilitation work
- Thomas describes taking large-scale groups through processes integrating the Holocaust, working on racism, genocide, and large-scale traumatizations
- In every instance, as the group developed more safety, more disruption arose
- He observed rooms of hundreds and thousands of people where the Holocaust shadow surfaced collectively, allowing participants to feel what is ordinarily suppressed
- This demonstrated that increased safety produces increased disruption, and that a facilitator must understand this as correct rather than as failure
**Liz Wiener's Discovery of Unresolved Holocaust Trauma**
- Shared by Liz Wiener, referring to her own experience
- Liz, in her early eighties and Jewish with family who emigrated from Poland before the Holocaust, began working on racism and Native American genocide as part of the course
- In doing so, she discovered that her own unresolved trauma around the Holocaust was still present within her, which she had not previously recognized
- She expressed anger at being asked to address other collective traumas before resolving her own, and questioned whether the wound of the Holocaust can ever be healed
- She expressed gratitude for the space to name this and for the community of approximately 2,500 participants worldwide
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Dissociation and Absent States in Meditation**
- Many meditators enter dissociated or absent states during sitting practice without recognizing them as trauma responses
- Absenced fields in the nervous system mean the meditator has no internal reference for what is occurring in those parts of themselves
**Inability to Sit Still**
- Heightened anxiety, stress, and restlessness create difficulty sitting down for meditation
- When space is created through sitting, the nervous system begins detoxing accumulated experience, which can feel destabilizing
**Meditation Crisis**
- Practitioners who are in good states can suddenly experience disruption that appears to be regression
- This is reframed as the nervous system feeling safe enough to surface fragmentation for integration
- Commitment to practice can waver not because of the practice itself but because of the content arising within it
**Overthinking and Mental Activation**
- Difficulty quieting the mind is identified as a common challenge for meditation practitioners
- Thinking is described as a symptom of underlying chronic stress, not the root issue
- Focusing on thinking rather than its source is identified as missing the point of regulation
**Incomplete Incarnation**
- Some spiritual practitioners have not fully incarnated into the body due to hurt attachment processes
- This creates struggles in real life challenges and can mean that transcendent spiritual practice reinforces rather than resolves symptoms
**Overwhelm When Facing Collective Trauma**
- Liz Wiener described the experience of anger and overwhelm when asked to engage with multiple collective traumas simultaneously while her own Holocaust-related trauma remained unresolved
- Thomas Hübl noted that thinking about the Holocaust can be so overwhelming that sense-making becomes purely cognitive, disconnected from embodied feeling
- Moral obligation to address collective wounds cannot override physical and emotional capacity; overwhelming oneself with more trauma does not improve healing
**Enrollment in Collective Fragmentation**
- Practitioners can become enrolled in societal fragmentation — taking sides, arguing about politics, experiencing reactivity — without recognizing it as trauma activation
- Recognizing enrollment and finding a way back to inner connection is identified as a key practice challenge
