### Outline
### Introduction to Antisemitism and Its Definition

- Antisemitism is described as a polite term for Jew hatred, encompassing discrimination and prejudice directed at Jewish people with deep historical roots.

- Religious antisemitism formally began when the Catholic Church became the official religion of the Roman Empire in the fourth and fifth centuries, with the foundational doctrine that Jews were responsible for the death of Jesus.

- The Catholic Church became the most powerful political entity in Europe during the Middle Ages, wielding significant influence over royalty across the continent.

### Historical Restrictions and Economic Scapegoating of Jews in Medieval Europe

- Jews across Europe were subjected to numerous restrictions, including prohibitions on owning land, limitations on certain professions, and bans on carrying weapons for self-defense.

- Because Christians were forbidden by the Church from lending money to other Christians at interest, a small number of Jews became moneylenders, primarily serving the nobility.

- Nobility across Western Europe exploited this arrangement by expelling Jews when debts became too large, effectively erasing financial obligations while reinforcing negative stereotypes.

- Over approximately five centuries, Jews were repeatedly invited into and then expelled from countries such as France, England, and the various provinces of Germany and Italy.

- This cycle of expulsion forced Jewish communities to remain mobile and maintain liquid assets, as owning real estate was both impractical and often legally prohibited.

### Jewish Life Under Islamic Rule Compared to Christian Europe

- Beginning in the seventh and eighth centuries, Jews living in Islamic-ruled countries were granted "dhimmi" status, which guaranteed protection of their lives, property, and freedom of religion.

- Life for Jews under Islamic governance was considerably more stable and protected than under Catholic or Eastern Orthodox Christian rule, where antisemitism was widespread.

- When Jews were expelled from Spain and Portugal in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, many relocated to the Ottoman Empire and other Islamic countries.

- A strong Jewish presence in Islamic countries persisted until the creation of the State of Israel in 1948, after which most Jews departed, some voluntarily and others due to persecution.

### The Origins of Racial Antisemitism and the Concept of Blood Purity

- Forced conversions of Jews and Muslims in fifteenth-century Spain gave rise to the concept of "limpieza de sangre," or purity of blood, representing one of the earliest forms of biological racism.

- Distrust of converted Jews, known as "New Christians," contributed to the eventual expulsion of Jews from Spain in 1492 and later from Portugal.

- The Doctrine of Discovery, established through papal bulls in the fifteenth century, authorized the King of Spain to reduce Muslims, indigenous peoples, and Jews to perpetual slavery and to seize their possessions.

- These papal bulls served as the foundational justification for colonialism and imperialism worldwide, placing Jews, Muslims, and indigenous peoples in the same category of those vulnerable to European domination.

### Jews as Visible Agents of Oppression and the Scapegoating Model

- Eastern European nobility in the 1700s invited expelled Jews to serve as tax collectors and innkeepers, deliberately positioning them as visible intermediaries between the ruling class and the peasantry.

- This arrangement allowed the nobility to redirect popular anger about taxes and poor living conditions toward Jews rather than toward those actually holding power.

- The vast majority of Jews during this period were poor farmers, while only a small number held the roles of tax collector or innkeeper that were used to justify scapegoating.

- Centuries of Church-based religious antisemitism made this scapegoating strategy highly effective, as deep-seated prejudice had already been cultivated across European societies.

### The Emergence of Modern Racial Antisemitism and the Pogroms

- In the late nineteenth century, the rise of so-called scientific racism led to Jews being classified as a separate, inferior, and dangerous race.

- Wilhelm Marr coined the term "antisemitism" during this period, drawing on the linguistic classification of Hebrew as a Semitic language, though the term was always intended to apply exclusively to Jews.

- Beginning in the 1880s, the Russian Tsar sponsored pogroms, or mass killings of Jews, as a deliberate strategy to deflect popular unrest and anger away from the Russian government.

- The majority of Jewish Americans can trace their ancestry to immigration between 1880 and 1920, driven largely by the violence of these pogroms across Eastern Europe.

- The Dreyfus Affair in France at the end of the nineteenth century exemplified the rise of political antisemitism in Western Europe, where political parties began organizing around explicitly antisemitic platforms.

### The Holocaust and Its Lasting Trauma on Jewish Communities

- Hitler rose to power in Germany partly by blaming Jews for the failures of the Weimar Republic, ultimately orchestrating the systematic murder of six million Jews alongside millions of others including Roma, gay people, disabled people, and socialists.

- Nearly no country in the world was willing to accept Jewish refugees before or during the Holocaust, a fact that significantly increased the death toll and deepened Jewish feelings of isolation.

- Beyond the loss of life, the Holocaust destroyed centuries of Jewish culture and vibrant Jewish communities in countries such as Poland, Germany, and Czechoslovakia.

- The Holocaust reinforced intergenerational trauma that had already accumulated over millennia of antisemitism, leaving lasting psychological effects on Jewish communities worldwide.

- The global Jewish population stands at only 14.8 million, representing just 0.2% of the world's population, and has not returned to its 1939 peak despite decades passing since the Holocaust.

### The Function of Antisemitism as a Political Tool

- Jean-Paul Sartre observed after the Holocaust that antisemitism in France served to unite the most reactionary political forces, with Jews becoming a projection screen for anxieties about modernity.

- The Protocols of the Elders of Zion, a book commissioned by the Russian Tsar around 1905, falsely portrayed Jews as a secret cabal controlling world banking and global events.

- Despite being a complete forgery, the Protocols have been translated into over 30 languages and remain in print today, serving as the foundational text for many contemporary conspiracy theories including QAnon.

- Antisemitism functions consistently as a tool to preserve the political power of the wealthy by diverting the anger of oppressed or economically marginalized people away from those actually in power and onto Jews.

- Unlike racism, whose primary historical function has been the economic exploitation of Black, Brown, and Indigenous people through the theft of land and labor, antisemitism's primary function is to divide people and prevent solidarity among oppressed groups.

- Because antisemitism operates differently from racism, which serves as the dominant template for understanding oppression in Western countries, many people fail to recognize or understand antisemitic dynamics when they encounter them.

### The Rise of White Nationalism and Its Relationship to Antisemitism

- White nationalism emerged as a social and political movement in the United States following the civil rights movement and in Europe following the expansion of rights for marginalized groups.

- Extreme economic concentration and a widening wealth gap in the United States and globally have created conditions in which right-wing politicians exploit fear and anger among middle-class and poor white Christians.

- Politicians such as Donald Trump and Jair Bolsonaro have stoked racism, homophobia, and transphobia to gain political power, with antisemitism forming a central part of that ideological framework.

- White nationalists hold the core belief that Jews are puppet masters secretly responsible for the civil rights movement, marriage equality, and other expansions of rights for marginalized groups.

- Scholar Eric Ward identifies antisemitism as the bedrock of white nationalism, noting that it provides the explanatory framework through which white nationalists interpret all social and political change.

- White nationalists are significantly more likely than left-wing actors to commit violent acts against Jews, as evidenced by patterns of hate crimes and mass violence targeting Jewish communities.

### Jewish Identity, Whiteness, and Diversity Within the Jewish Community

- Jews who present as white have benefited from white privilege in societies where whiteness confers social and economic advantages, and many have assimilated into dominant white culture in pursuit of safety and opportunity.

- These privileges are considered provisional, as they can be revoked by the Christian majority, particularly under Christian nationalist governance where Jews would likely lose their current social standing.

- Approximately 15 to 20 percent of people who identify as Jewish in the United States identify as non-white, reflecting significant racial and ethnic diversity within the Jewish community.

- No assumptions can be made about a Jewish person's appearance, political beliefs, religious practice, cultural background, or level of Jewish knowledge, as the community encompasses enormous diversity.

### Distinguishing Left-Wing from Right-Wing Antisemitism

- Antisemitism exists across the political spectrum because people of all backgrounds are raised in societies that have historically taught antisemitic ideas, but its role and function differ significantly between left and right.

- Antisemitism is a core and necessary ideological component of white nationalism on the right, whereas on the left it contradicts the stated values of progressive organizations and can be challenged on those grounds.

- Progressive organizations can be held accountable to their own values when antisemitism arises within them, and members can often be persuaded that antisemitism undermines their own interests by dividing potential allies.

- Attempts to equate left-wing and right-wing antisemitism are described as a dangerous conflation that obscures the far greater threat posed by organized white nationalist movements.

### The Distinction Between Antisemitism and Anti-Zionism

- Criticism of the Israeli government is not inherently antisemitic, as any government's policies can and should be subject to scrutiny and critique.

- Zionism is a relatively modern political ideology dating to the late nineteenth century, whereas Judaism is a religion with roots spanning 2,500 to 3,000 years, and the two must not be conflated.

- Anti-Zionism crosses into antisemitism when it relies on conspiracy theories portraying Jews as secret controllers of world events, when the word "Zionist" is used as a coded substitute for "Jew," or when Jewish historical experience including the Holocaust is dismissed.

- Treating support for or opposition to Israel as a litmus test for participation in progressive organizations constitutes a form of antisemitism, as no other group is subjected to such a prerequisite.

- A wide range of opinion exists within the Jewish community regarding Israel and Zionism, and no assumptions should be made about any individual Jewish person's political position on these matters.

### Creating Welcoming and Inclusive Spaces for Jewish People in Mindfulness Communities

- Mindfulness teachers are encouraged not to assume that everyone in their group shares the dominant religion of the surrounding culture, and to remain open to the diverse backgrounds participants may bring.

- Intergenerational trauma is a significant reality for many Jewish practitioners, and may manifest during meditation as fear, anxiety, or a sense of isolation that the individual may not consciously connect to their Jewish heritage.

- Fear arising during extended sitting practice among Jewish participants may operate at a cellular or somatic level, rooted in ancestral experiences of persecution, violence, and displacement.

- Teachers are advised to hold space patiently for fear and anxiety as it arises, encouraging students to observe these states without being overtaken by them and to explore what lies beneath.

- Meditation has a long history within Judaism, including a rich Jewish mystical tradition, and insight meditation has been incorporated into Jewish practice by hundreds of rabbis and many congregations.

- The practice of mindfulness can be presented to Jewish students as something that integrates naturally with Jewish spiritual life rather than as something foreign or in conflict with their identity.

### Addressing Internalized Oppression and Identity Concealment

- Internalized oppression occurs when members of an oppressed group absorb the negative messages of the dominant society and turn them against themselves, such as when Jewish people measure themselves against Eurocentric standards of appearance.

- Individuals who conceal aspects of their Jewish identity, such as shortening or changing their names to ease social passage, are encouraged to examine whether this reflects a free and conscious choice or an internalized response to external pressure.

- Honest self-reflection about the motivations behind identity concealment is presented as an important step toward understanding the difference between genuine personal agency and the internalization of oppressive norms.

### Responding to the Rise of Antisemitic Violence and the Path Toward Jewish Safety

- Feelings of isolation in response to antisemitic violence are described as a deeply conditioned Jewish response rooted in centuries of historical abandonment, including the refusal of nations to accept Jewish refugees during the Holocaust.

- Recognizing isolation as a triggered historical pattern rather than an accurate reflection of current reality is presented as a necessary first step toward effective response.

- Jews are described as safest in multiracial, pluralist democracies, and building alliances with other marginalized communities including people of color and LGBTQ people is identified as the most effective path toward collective safety.

- Those who target Jews also target other marginalized groups, making solidarity across communities both a moral imperative and a practical strategy for mutual protection.

- Speaking openly about antisemitism, connecting with other Jewish people, and actively seeking out allies committed to pluralist democracy are presented as the key steps for responding to rising antisemitic violence.

### Detailed Subject Matter

- Antisemitism is described as a polite term for Jew hatred
