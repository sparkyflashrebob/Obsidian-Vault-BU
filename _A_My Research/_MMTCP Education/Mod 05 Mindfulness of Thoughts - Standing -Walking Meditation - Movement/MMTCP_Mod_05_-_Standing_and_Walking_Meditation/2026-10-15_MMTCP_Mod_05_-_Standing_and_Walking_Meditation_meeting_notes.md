### Outline
### Standing Meditation as a Foundational Practice

- Standing meditation is introduced as a core posture for mindfulness training alongside walking, lying down, and transitional postures in between.

- Practitioners are invited to stand still with eyes closed for approximately 30 seconds, during which it becomes apparent that the body naturally sways and cannot remain completely motionless.

- The purpose of the exercise is not to stop movement or force the body into a fixed state, but to bring loving awareness to the natural rhythm of life present within the body.

- Attention is directed downward to the feet, encouraging a sense of grounding and rootedness, as if the body were a great tree with deep roots extending through the earth.

- Adding a half-smile, as recommended by Thich Nhat Hanh, is suggested as a way to introduce a quality of brightness or joy into the standing practice.

- Awareness of the breath while standing is incorporated as an extension of seated mindfulness of breath practice, with attention given to wherever the breath is most apparent in the body.

- Thoughts, moods, and feelings that arise during standing are acknowledged as natural occurrences, and standing awareness functions as a mirror to illuminate the waves of experience just as sitting meditation does.

### Practical Applications of Standing Meditation for Teachers

- Standing meditation is well suited to everyday situations such as waiting in line at a supermarket or in an airport security queue.

- One particularly valuable application for teachers is offering standing as an option to students who feel sleepy during a class, as standing generates more energy and makes it significantly harder to fall asleep.

- Familiarity with standing meditation is presented as essential for teachers, as it helps students understand that their entire life can serve as their practice rather than only formal seated sessions.

- Regular personal practice of standing meditation is encouraged, particularly during moments of waiting or as part of a home meditation routine, to build genuine embodied understanding of the posture.

### Lying Down Meditation Guidance

- Lying down meditation is recognized as a necessary and valid posture for individuals who cannot sit upright due to physical pain or disability, and this needs to be fully respected by teachers.

- Additional contexts where lying down meditation is appropriate include meditating before sleep or returning to mindfulness after waking in the middle of the night.

- Keeping the eyes open during lying down practice is recommended to counteract the body's conditioned association between lying down and falling asleep.

- Drawing the knees up so the body is not lying completely flat is offered as a helpful adjustment to maintain alertness during the practice.

- Raising one or both hands upright while lying down provides a physical cue for drowsiness, as the hands will fall if the practitioner begins to drift toward sleep.

- Teachers are encouraged to practice lying down meditation themselves so they can guide students with genuine understanding and without privileging any one posture over another.

- The principle that the Dharma gates welcome everyone where they are is emphasized as the underlying reason for offering lying down as a fully legitimate meditative posture.

### Walking Meditation as a Core Mindfulness Practice

- Walking meditation is described as one of the most powerful and important components of mindfulness training and is not to be considered a secondary or lesser practice compared to sitting.

- Deep insights and understandings arise during walking meditation with the same frequency and depth as in seated practice when full attention is given to it.

- Walking meditation serves to balance energy by providing wakefulness and alertness for those who are tired or restless and unable to settle into sitting.

- Students who arrive home wired and restless are advised to begin with walking meditation and transition to sitting only once they feel sufficiently settled.

- The practice makes it evident that the goal is not to arrive anywhere, which directly challenges the Western tendency toward goal-oriented movement and reinforces the principle of being present where one is.

- Walking at whatever speed best supports presence is the most important instruction to convey, as some practitioners benefit from slowing down while others need a faster pace to remain attentive.

### Step-by-Step Instructions for Teaching Walking Meditation

- Teaching walking meditation begins with having students stand still, feel their feet on the floor, and open their senses to the surrounding environment before taking any steps.

- Orientation to the present moment and to one's physical location is established first through open awareness before attention is narrowed down to the feet and legs and their connection with the earth.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's image of the empress or emperor taking a dignified stroll through a garden is offered as a way to cultivate a sense of well-being and graciousness in the walking.

- Another image from Thich Nhat Hanh is that of the soles of the feet kissing the earth with each step, which introduces a quality of loving-kindness into the physical movement.

- Pausing frequently is highlighted as an important instruction, particularly when thoughts become dense, allowing the practitioner to stand still, acknowledge the mental activity, feel the feet on the ground, and then resume walking.

- Naming techniques such as silently noting "lifting" and "placing" with each step are offered as concentration aids, similar to using "in" and "out" for the breath.

- Counting steps in small groups of three followed by a pause is suggested as a helpful structure for those who find it difficult to maintain continuous attention.

- Linking the breath to the steps is presented as a secondary instruction to be introduced only after the practitioner has established basic familiarity with feeling the steps themselves.

- Varying the rhythm of walking, including alternating between faster and slower paces, is recommended as a way to sustain interest and presence during extended periods of walking practice.

- Bringing a spirit of play into walking, such as imagining walking along a dramatic edge or recalling childhood movement games, is offered as a legitimate and effective way to stay present.

### Handling Difficulties in Walking Meditation

- Balance difficulties, particularly among older practitioners, often arise when attempting to walk more slowly than the body can comfortably support, and adjusting the pace can resolve this.

- Strong emotions such as frustration or anger may surface during walking meditation, and these are to be acknowledged through noting practice rather than suppressed or acted upon.

- Noting emotional states by name while continuing to walk allows the intensity of those states to naturally diminish over time, as illustrated by the experience of a practitioner who worked through anger by simply labeling it with each step.

- Insight and unexpected lessons emerge naturally from sustained attentive walking, and teachers are encouraged to invite students to remain open and curious about what arises rather than directing them toward a specific outcome.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to deepen their personal familiarity with standing, lying down, and walking meditation through regular practice in everyday contexts.

- Walking meditation will be practiced as a group in the session immediately following this instructional segment.

- Teachers are expected to integrate the systematic instructional approach outlined for walking meditation into their own teaching, with a primary emphasis on inviting students to discover what they learn from the practice themselves.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Standing Meditation**
- Jack invited participants to stand and attempt to remain completely still for 30 seconds
- The exercise reveals that the body naturally sways and moves — the point is not to stop movement but to observe it
- Standing illustrates the rhythm of life present in the body
- Attention is directed to the feet on the floor, the weight and gravity of the body, and a sense of grounding deep into the earth
- The image of a great tree with deep roots is offered — slight swaying is natural while roots remain firm
- A half-smile, as recommended by Thich Nhat Hanh, is suggested to bring brightness or joy to the standing posture
- Practitioners are invited to feel the breath while standing, noticing wherever it is most apparent
- Standing is described as being "halfway between heaven and earth"
- Thoughts, moods, and feelings arise during standing just as in sitting — standing becomes a mirror for experience
- Practical applications: standing in line at the supermarket or airport security
- In a teaching context, standing can be offered to sleepy students as an energizing alternative to sitting
**Lying Down Meditation**
- Appropriate for people with physical pain, disability, or those meditating before sleep or in the middle of the night
- Not an invitation to sleep — it is a legitimate mindfulness posture
- Keeping eyes open is recommended to signal to the body that sleep is not the intention
- Drawing knees up rather than lying completely flat can help maintain alertness
- Raising one or both hands while lying down provides a physical cue — if drowsiness comes, the hands fall
- Teachers are encouraged to practice lying down meditation themselves so they can guide students who need it
- The reclining Buddha image is referenced; Trudy (Jack's wife) adopted sleeping on her right side after seeing these images
- The Dharma gates welcome everyone where they are — no posture should be privileged over another
**Walking Meditation**
- Described as one of the most powerful and important parts of mindfulness training — not a second-class practice
- Deep insights and understandings arise during walking just as in sitting
- Balances energy: helpful when restless or when sitting leads to drowsiness
- Teaching sequence: begin by standing, feel feet on the floor, open senses to surroundings, orient to where you are, then drop attention to feet and legs before beginning to walk
- Walk at whatever speed keeps you most present — sometimes faster walking is more grounding for restless practitioners
- Thich Nhat Hanh's images: walking as the empress or emperor in the garden; letting the bottom of the feet kiss the earth
- The walking path goes nowhere — this counters the Western drive to always be going somewhere
- Pausing frequently is encouraged: stop when thoughts are heavy, acknowledge them, feel feet on earth, begin again
- Naming steps — "lifting and placing" — is offered as a concentration aid, similar to noting the breath
- Counting steps in groups of three with pauses is suggested for those who find it difficult
- Linking breath to steps is an option but not the first instruction
- Play is encouraged: varying pace, imagining walking on the edge of the Grand Canyon, childhood games like banana steps
- Thich Nhat Hanh's walking at Spirit Rock is described as a living demonstration of mindfulness — 2,000–3,000 people witnessed it
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Embodied Presence as the Foundation of Practice**
- Sitting with eyes closed is described as "training wheels" — the deeper aim is to embody loving awareness in all postures and all of life
- The body in standing, walking, and lying down is not an obstacle but the very field of practice
- Mindfulness of posture teaches that life itself is the practice — not a separate activity reserved for the cushion
**Grounding and Rootedness**
- The image of roots going through rock down to the center of the earth conveys a spiritual sense of belonging to the earth
- Standing meditation cultivates a felt sense of one's right to be present — "taking a stand, your right to stand on the earth"
- The practitioner is described as standing "halfway between heaven and earth" — a poetic framing of the human condition
**Non-Goal-Oriented Presence**
- Walking meditation makes obvious that you are not going anywhere — a direct counter to the Western cultural drive toward destination and achievement
- The poem by Barbara Ruth reframes "best time" not as speed but as depth of presence — resting with lizards, staring at mountains
- The story of Robert walking slowly along the garden wall illustrates how unhurried presence opens unexpected paths
**Inclusivity and the Dharma**
- Physical limitations, pain, and disability are explicitly acknowledged — no posture is privileged
- Teachers are called to ensure every student feels welcomed and accommodated: "The Dharma gates welcome everyone where they are"
- This is framed as both a responsibility and a gift that teachers offer
**Transformation Through Sustained Practice**
- Jack's account of standing for 18 hours describes a progression from pain to fire, to water, to rapture, to dissolution into a field of energy — illustrating the transformative potential of sustained embodied practice
- The point is not to replicate the extreme but to understand that commitment to a posture can yield profound states
**Emotions as Teachers**
- Emotions arising during walking — such as frustration or anger — are not obstacles but material for practice
- Noting emotions ("hatred, hatred, anger, anger") and continuing to walk allows them to naturally subside and yield insight

### Stories
**The 30-Second Stillness Exercise**
Jack Kornfield, recounting a teaching from his own teacher, describes being asked to stand completely still for 30 seconds. The teacher would laugh afterward and say, "You couldn't do it, could you?" The point is that the body naturally moves and the practice is to observe that movement with loving awareness, not to stop it.
**Jack's 18-Hour Standing Practice**
Jack Kornfield shares a personal story from his time in a training monastery. As a young man drawn to difficult challenges, he was told by a teacher that he could learn to break through the pain of any posture. He stood for 18 hours. His feet swelled and felt like hot coals. Over the subsequent hours, the pain transformed — energy released, his body filled with fire, then the fire turned to water, then waves of energy, then rapture, and finally his body dissolved into a field of energy. When the bell rang at 18 hours, he said, "Okay, I know how to stand."
**Trudy and the Reclining Buddha**
Jack Kornfield shares that his wife Trudy read about the Buddha sleeping on his right side and saw images of the reclining Buddha. She decided she should do the same and learned to sleep on her right side.
**Thich Nhat Hanh Walking at Spirit Rock**
Jack Kornfield recounts a visit by Thich Nhat Hanh to Spirit Rock. Two to three thousand people were seated on a hillside, having just done a mindful eating practice with apples. When Thich Nhat Hanh walked up the road toward the stage, his walking was so mindful and present that all three thousand people collectively felt the meaning of mindfulness. Jack describes it as like "a clipper ship with the sails all trimmed perfectly in a breeze."
**Barbara Ruth's Poem on Walking**
Jack Kornfield quotes a poem by a friend named Barbara Ruth. She describes walking six miles from her house to Kent Lake. Her fastest time was under four hours, but her personal best was eight hours and fifteen minutes — time that included resting with lizards, writing down a remembered dream, and staring at Mount Barnaby.
**Robert and the Locked Garden Gate**
Jack Kornfield shares a story written by a friend about a trip to Montreal with his husband Robert to visit a Chinese temple garden with the largest bonsai collection outside Asia. When they arrived, the massive gate was locked. The friend panicked, ready to demand entry after a 400-mile drive. Robert, described as sage-like, treated it as a koan and began walking slowly back and forth in front of the gate, then along the outer wall. The friend grew cranky imagining walking the entire perimeter. But as they continued, the wall simply disappeared — the garden had no walls except the front facade. An open grass path welcomed them in.
**The Woman and the Noisy Walker**
Jack Kornfield recounts a story from a student at his center in Massachusetts. She was walking in a quiet meditation room when a man entered without removing his boots and paced loudly back and forth. She felt intense hatred toward him. She began noting — "hatred, hatred, anger, anger" — and continued walking. After a time, the hatred became boring. She noticed her steps change. She realized the man was just sound. When he finally left, the room was quieter, but not as much better as she had expected. She said she learned a great deal from simply paying attention in that way.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Balance Difficulties in Walking Meditation**
- Some practitioners, particularly older individuals, report losing their balance when attempting to walk slowly
- This is attributed to walking slower than the body can physically support
- The suggested response is to walk at a pace that maintains stability and presence
**Sleepiness During Practice**
- Sleepiness is identified as a common challenge in both sitting and lying down meditation
- Standing is offered as an antidote — it is harder to fall asleep while standing, though Jack notes he has seen it done
- For lying down meditation, keeping eyes open, drawing knees up, and raising hands are offered as practical countermeasures to drowsiness
**Physical Pain and Disability**
- Physical pain and disability are explicitly acknowledged as reasons some practitioners cannot sit upright
- Lying down meditation is presented as a fully valid alternative, not a lesser option
- Teachers are instructed not to privilege any posture and to ensure all students feel welcomed
**Restlessness and Inability to Sit**
- Some practitioners arrive home wired and restless from the day and cannot settle into sitting
- Walking meditation is offered as the appropriate starting point — walk until settled, then sit if ready
**Emotions Arising During Walking**
- Strong emotions such as frustration and anger can surface during walking meditation
- These are treated as objects of mindfulness rather than obstacles — noting them and continuing to walk allows them to naturally dissolve
**Extreme Physical Endurance Practice**
- Jack's 18-hour standing practice involved significant physical challenge: feet swelling, a sensation of standing on hot coals, and the body feeling on fire
- This is explicitly not recommended as a general practice but is shared to illustrate the depth of transformation possible through sustained embodied practice
