### Outline
### The Third Foundation of Mindfulness and the Nature of Thought

- Described by Ajahn Buddhadasa as "lost in thought," the modern world is seen from the perspective of the forest tradition as fundamentally disconnected from present-moment awareness.

- Dimensions of human experience such as love, connection, presence, and mystery exist entirely outside the realm of thought, becoming accessible only when the thinking mind quiets down.

- Quieting the mind allows individuals to attune themselves to the world in a fundamentally different and more open way.

- Central to mindfulness teaching is the miraculous capacity of consciousness to observe itself, described as "the mind who observes the mind" or loving awareness witnessing the workings of thought.

- This capacity is described as both revelatory and revolutionary for students encountering it for the first time.

### The Creative and Destructive Power of the Mind

- Secreted continuously like saliva from a gland, thoughts take the form of words, images, ideas, visions, memories, and plans that collectively construct much of the human world.

- All human-made structures, systems, and inventions — from roads and bridges to governments and medicine — originated as ideas in someone's mind, demonstrating the enormous creative power of thought.

- Mind is equally capable of destruction, and understanding its workings is described as critical to the happiness and suffering of students in their relationships and communities.

- The Buddha's teaching is cited to illustrate that an untrained mind is one's greatest enemy, while a well-trained mind is one's greatest friend, surpassing even the care of the most loving parent.

- Khyentse Rinpoche's teaching that "mind creates both samsara and nirvana, yet there's nothing much to it — it's just thoughts" is offered as a foundational insight into the nature of mental experience.

### Seeing the Waterfall and the Moment of Clarity

- A common and significant moment for new meditators occurs when they first sit quietly and realize the sheer volume of mental activity — planning, remembering, judging, imagining, and analyzing — that had previously gone unnoticed.

- For many people, this recognition of how much is happening in the mind is an enormous moment of clarity, precisely because they have been so identified with their thoughts.

- A profound teaching offered to students is that the thought of their mother is not their mother, illustrating how people can spend years following loyalty to misguided mental representations rather than reality.

- Nations and societies are noted as equally susceptible to carrying and believing misguided thoughts across generations, particularly in the domain of child-rearing and cultural conditioning.

### Cultural Conditioning and the Limiting of Human Potential

- A poem is read depicting a four-year-old boy who delights in wearing dresses, embodying innocent beauty and joy entirely free from cultural judgment.

- Cultural conditioning is shown to impose narrow expectations on children — such as the idea that boys should only dream of baseball, trucks, and trains — leaving no room for those who fall outside these norms.

- Each child is described as being born with a beautiful and innocent spirit that can be shrunk, diminished, judged, and limited by the thought structures, prejudices, and misguided understandings of the surrounding culture.

- Tenderness is expressed for every child whose natural expression is constrained by the conditioning of the world around them, with the reminder that such conditioning does not define who a person truly is.

### Mindfulness of Thought as a Gateway to Liberation

- Teaching others to become aware of the mind opens a doorway of liberation, allowing students to see that their beliefs and the ways they structure reality are not necessarily accurate representations of how things are.

- An illustration from Ajahn Chah uses the image of a heavy boulder to demonstrate that suffering arises not from things themselves but from how the mind holds, imagines, and engages with them.

- Thoughts can serve people in beautiful and meaningful ways, but they can also imprison individuals for long periods, much like Japanese soldiers who continued holding their posts decades after a war had ended because they had not received word that it was over.

- A compassionate approach to releasing outdated thought patterns is suggested — not punishing the thoughts, but acknowledging them with gratitude and recognizing that the war they were protecting against is now over.

- Carlos Castaneda's observation that people maintain their entire world through inner dialogue is cited, with the insight that a person of knowledge recognizes the world will change completely once they stop talking to themselves.

### Guided Practice for Counting Thoughts

- A short practice of approximately thirty seconds to one minute is introduced in which participants sit quietly and count each thought as it arises, treating each thought as a discrete event to be numbered.

- Participants are cautioned that the act of counting is itself a thought, and that the mind will attempt to trick the observer with meta-thoughts such as "there haven't been many thoughts yet."

- Following the practice, participants report varying counts ranging from three to eleven thoughts, prompting a discussion about whether higher counts reflect more thoughts or simply shorter ones.

- Differences in thinking style are explored, including whether thoughts are primarily visual, verbal, or bodily in nature, highlighting the diversity of inner experience across individuals.

- A key observation from sustained practice is that approximately ninety percent of thoughts are reruns from past mental content, and that much of this repeated inner dialogue constitutes what is described as "fake news."

### Recognizing Patterns and Styles of Thought

- One of the gifts of mindfulness practice is the growing ability to notice one's personal style of thinking and the recurring patterns that shape inner experience.

- Thoughts oriented toward the future are often accompanied by anxiety and a compulsive need to manage or control outcomes, while thoughts oriented toward the past tend to carry themes of guilt, love, or nostalgia.

- Turning loving awareness toward the mind itself produces a shift in which the heart is able to hold thoughts with compassion, tenderness, joy, or spaciousness rather than being swept away by them.

- Nisargadatta's teaching that "the mind creates the abyss and the heart crosses it" is offered as a central principle, with Thomas Merton's parallel reflection on the inability to cross the abyss separating people from themselves and one another reinforcing the same insight.

### The Inner Judge and the Capacity for Love

- A near-universal feature of the Western mind is the presence of an inner judge and jury whose standards are so harsh that Jack Kornfield humorously suggests they would not be hired in any civilized judicial system.

- Judging thoughts are understood not as inherently malicious but as learned protective mechanisms, often originating from parents, teachers, or cultural authorities who instilled beliefs intended to create safety.

- These same judging thoughts, however, prevent people from experiencing the beauty of the natural world, the eyes of their children, and the simple joy of being present.

- A story from Frank Ostaseski's "Five Invitations" is recounted in which a dying gay man named Matthew, raised in a fundamentalist Christian household, was tormented by the belief that God would condemn him to hell for his sexual orientation.

- A small plastic figurine of Jesus placed on Matthew's altar, combined with the spontaneous and faith-filled words of a cleaning woman named Dina, transformed his image of God from a punishing figure into a merciful and all-accepting one.

- Matthew's entire body relaxed in that moment as the ruthless inner critic was replaced by the experience of unconditional divine love, illustrating the power of shifting from the mind to the heart.

### Practical Techniques for Teaching Mindfulness of Thought

- Naming thoughts lightly — using words such as "planning," "remembering," "judging," or "doubting" — is introduced as a skillful means that shifts identity from believing a thought to simply acknowledging it without struggle.

- Thanking the judging mind with phrases such as "thank you for your opinion, I'm okay for now" is offered as a way to disengage from difficult thoughts without suppressing or fighting them.

- Students can be invited to identify their top ten recurring thought patterns and assign them numbers, so that when a familiar thought arises again it can simply be acknowledged by number rather than re-engaged with fully.

- Turning attention directly toward a thought often causes it to dissolve like a cloud under sunlight, a phenomenon that becomes increasingly apparent with sustained practice.

- Joshu Sasaki Roshi's response of "that's an interesting thought" to students presenting elaborate answers is cited as a powerful model for helping others recognize their stories without judgment or reaction.

- Normalizing the thought stream is essential, as many students mistakenly believe that the goal of meditation is to stop thinking entirely, and reassurance that all minds think helps remove the sense of failure.

### Three Levels of Awareness in Mindfulness of Thought

- The first level of awareness involves noticing the content of experience — identifying whether thoughts are planning, worrying, self-judging, self-aggrandizing, or creative in nature, thereby mapping the inner landscape of the mind.

- At the second level, attention shifts from content to process, and the practitioner begins to observe that thoughts are insubstantial — arising and passing like stars at dawn, flashes of lightning, echoes, or dreams as described in the Dhammapada and the Diamond Sutra.

- The third level involves shifting attention to awareness itself — becoming "the one who knows," the clear witnessing space of consciousness that observes all thought without being identified with it.

- Each of these three levels represents a progressively deeper form of letting go, moving from engagement with thought content to recognition of its impermanence to resting in the open space of loving awareness.

- Ultimately, the heart rather than the mind becomes the place from which one lives, and this shift is described as both healing and a tremendous gift to those one works with.

### Working with Thoughts Through Replacement and Spaciousness

- Once a practitioner has developed familiarity with the content and process of thought and has settled into witnessing awareness, it becomes possible to work with thoughts playfully rather than struggling against them.

- The Dalai Lama's teaching that "some of your thoughts do not have your best interest in mind" is cited as a basis for intentionally replacing unhelpful thoughts with those of loving-kindness, self-compassion, gratitude, joy, or peace.

- Zhuangzi's parable of the empty boat is used to illustrate the value of holding thoughts lightly — when one crosses the river of the world without the weight of fixed identity, no one opposes or seeks to harm them.

- A spaciousness described as "the laughter of the wise" becomes available as practitioners stop clinging to opinions, echoing the Buddha's teaching that those who grasp after views wander about the world causing unnecessary friction.

- The inner and outer dimensions of awareness are described as ultimately inseparable, with the space of the mind mirroring the vastness of the cosmos itself.

### Tara Brach's Emphasis on a Non-Adversarial Relationship with Thoughts

- Recognizing "I am not my thoughts and I don't have to believe them" is described by Tara Brach as one of the most profound signs of genuine awakening in a student.

- A subtle but pervasive subtext in most meditation books, teachings, and teachers is identified — the implicit message that a quieter mind is better and a busier mind represents failure, leading many practitioners to feel they are falling short.

- Being at war with thoughts is described as a losing strategy, since such a war would last for the rest of one's life, making the quality of one's relationship to thoughts far more important than their quantity or content.

- A poem by Kaveri Patel is read in which the "monkey mind" swinging between past and future is ultimately thanked rather than killed, after which it jumps down into the speaker's lap and they sit still together.

- Thoughts are understood as part of evolution's mechanism for survival and flourishing — often misguided and often inaccurate, but not enemies to be defeated.

- The attitude recommended for guiding students in working with thoughts is one of interest, humor, and lightness rather than resistance or suppression.

### Stories
**The Boulder in the Field**
Jack Kornfield, recounting a teaching moment with Ajahn Chah — walking in the countryside, Ajahn Chah pointed to a large boulder and asked if it was heavy. When students said yes, he replied: "Not if you don't pick it up." The story illustrates how we hold thoughts and whether we choose to pick them up.
**Japanese Soldiers After World War II**
Jack Kornfield — soldiers left in jungles and on islands continued holding their posts 10 to 20 years after the war ended because they had not heard it was over. When discovered, the Japanese military sent an officer in the old uniform with a sword to thank them for their service. Used as a metaphor for how old thought patterns continue to protect us long after the original threat has passed — and how they can be acknowledged rather than punished.
**The Woman in the Bank Robbery**
Jack Kornfield — a woman who had been on retreat was present during a bank robbery in St. Louis. Initially her thoughts raced with fear. She returned to her breath, began to see the robbers as young, desperate men, filled herself with metta, and spoke to them calmly. The situation de-escalated. Used to illustrate the practical power of tending the mind.
**Matthew at the Hospice**
Jack Kornfield, recounting a story from Frank Ostaseski's Five Invitations — Matthew, a gay man raised in a fundamentalist Christian family, was dying and terrified of God's condemnation. Frank Ostaseski tried Buddhist altars, thangkas, chanting, and music without success. In the early hours of the morning, he retrieved a small plastic figurine of Jesus from his memory box. A cleaning woman named Dina entered, saw the figurine, and said: "Merciful Jesus, when his kindness is with us, everything is all right." Matthew's entire body relaxed. The punishing God of his childhood was transformed into the merciful, all-accepting God he knew and loved. Used to illustrate the thousand skillful means available to teachers and the power of shifting from the mind to the heart.
**The Tooth Fairy**
Jack Kornfield, recounting a personal story about his daughter Cindy — after being advised by his mother that indulging the tooth fairy fantasy would teach Cindy not to trust adults, Jack told his seven-and-a-half-year-old the truth. Cindy cried hard. After being comforted, she had one last question: "What do you wear?" Used to illustrate the innocence and playfulness that exists just beneath thought, and how quickly a child can step out of one story and into another.
**The Child Who Prefers Dresses**
Jack Kornfield, reading a poem — a parent describes their four-year-old son who prefers to wear dresses, calls himself gorgeous, and dances in sparkly red slippers. The parent affirms the child's beauty while internally dreading the name-calling he will face. Used to illustrate how cultural conditioning and thought structures impose suffering on innocent human beings.
**The Sixth-Grade Letter**
Jack Kornfield, reading a letter from a child — a student who visited a meditation center with their school initially did not take meditation seriously. After getting into big fights with parents, they recalled the meditation experience, went into the yard after a fight with their mother, meditated, and returned to the house less angry. The child wrote: "I don't know what it is about meditating that helps me, but I do know it helps me let go of my thoughts and anger." Used to illustrate that even a small amount of practice can be transformative.
**The Mandolin and Come Back to Sorrento**
Tara Brach, recounting a story from psychologist Len Bergentino — after a series of frustrating therapy sessions with a patient who was either disconnected or trying to please him excessively, Bergentino took out his mandolin and played Come Back to Sorrento. The patient wept throughout the session and said: "Bergentino, today you really earned your money." Bergentino replied: "To think I wasted all these years talking to people." Used to illustrate the longing for deep connection that exists beneath thought.
**The Monkey in My Mind**
Tara Brach, reading a poem by Kaviri Patel — a monkey in the mind swings on a trapeze, reaching to the past or leaning into the future. The speaker sometimes wants to kill the monkey, but one day thanks her instead, and the monkey jumps down into the speaker's lap. Used to illustrate the shift from being at war with thoughts to relating to them with acceptance and lightness.
**Joshu Sasaki Roshi and the Koan**
Jack Kornfield, recounting personal experience studying with Rinzai Zen master Joshu Sasaki Roshi, who died in Los Angeles at age 106 and taught until age 103. Students sat for long hours and visited the teacher four times a day to answer koans. When Jack gave an answer, the Roshi would smile and say: "That's an interesting thought," then ring his bell. Used to illustrate the power of meeting a student's story with loving non-identification rather than judgment.
