### Money, Sex, and Power — Reflections for Meditation Teachers

### Outline
### The Purpose and Foundation of Teaching Ethics in Mindfulness Training

- Happiness in the context of mindfulness teacher training is defined not merely as pleasure, but as fulfillment, presence, compassion, and overall well-being of the heart.

- Ethics and the commitment to non-harming form the foundational basis upon which all other spiritual practices rest.

- Without an ethical foundation, meditation and other contemplative practices lose their effectiveness, as illustrated by the metaphor of a rowboat still tied to the shore when trying to cross a stream of confusion.

- Establishing a basis of non-harming through speech and action is presented as the prerequisite for the path of well-being to unfold naturally.

### Why Money, Sex, and Power Must Be Addressed by Meditation Teachers

- Money, sex, and power are identified as charged and often avoided topics in both personal life and broader society, yet they will inevitably arise in the experience of a teacher.

- Including these themes in one's own consciousness and teaching practice is framed not merely as addressing problem areas, but as making them conscious and practical.

- Four specific areas of reflection are proposed for teachers to engage with these themes, covering personal relationships to each topic, how to avoid harm, how to develop these areas positively, and how to respond when problems arise.

### The History and Impact of Teacher Misconduct in Spiritual Communities

- Numerous examples of teachers abusing their roles have emerged across decades, spanning spiritual teachers, lamas, swamis, Catholic priests, rabbis, coaches, and politicians.

- Sexual misconduct by teachers toward students is identified as a widespread issue that occurs in spiritual communities as frequently as in the worlds of power and politics.

- The great majority of those who suffer from sexual misconduct by teachers are women, a fact Andy McGuire states plainly and directly.

- Spiritual injury resulting from betrayal by a teacher is described as being as profound as the ending of a deeply intimate relationship, and recovery can take years or even decades.

- The depth of this harm reflects both the importance of the teacher role and the degree of love, trust, and vulnerability that students bring to their spiritual teachers.

- Teachers are therefore called to be stewards of that trust and to carry it with respect and care.

### Patriarchy and Structural Imbalance in Spiritual Traditions

- A meeting arranged with the Dalai Lama and prominent teachers from Tibetan, Zen, and Theravada traditions was convened to openly address the growing awareness of teacher misconduct.

- A Western Tibetan nun who had practiced for twelve years in the Himalayas described the poor treatment of women in Tibetan monasteries, where men received preferential access to food and teachings.

- Upon hearing this account, the Dalai Lama wept and expressed that he had not known the situation was so severe.

- German meditation teacher Silvia Wetzel then offered the Dalai Lama a guided reflection in which he was invited to imagine being the 14th Dakini Dalai Lama, a figure who always incarnates as a woman.

- Within this imagined parallel universe, all the enlightened beings depicted in paintings, all the statues, and all advisors were female, and men were asked to sit at the back and help in the kitchen after sessions.

- This exercise caused widespread realization among those present of how deeply out of balance patriarchal structures have been across spiritual traditions and society at large.

- The point of this reflection is not to assign right or wrong, but to illuminate that patriarchy, like racism and homophobia, is the water in which society swims, and teachers will feel its effects.

### The Dynamics of Projection and Idealization in the Teacher-Student Relationship

- Meditation teachers occupy an archetypal role onto which students project their ideals, expectations, imagination, and desires for wisdom and answers.

- Students frequently bring their unresolved questions about money, power, sex, and relationships to teachers, expecting guidance and wisdom.

- One significant risk for teachers is beginning to believe their own elevated image as reflected back by students, a process that can lead to misuse of the role.

- Teachers from other cultural backgrounds may also misunderstand how to hold the teacher role within Western cultural contexts around sexuality, contributing to further confusion and harm.

- The MMTCP code of ethics is presented as a key reference point, emphasizing equal respect for all persons regardless of background, religion, caste, race, or ability, and the avoidance of all forms of harassment and harm.

- When difficulties arise, teachers are encouraged to consult mentors, and the MMTCP alumni association is in the process of establishing an ethics council to support graduates with these challenges.

- Many spiritual communities including Spirit Rock, IMCW, and Insight Meditation Society now have ethics bodies that students and teachers can turn to when needed.

### Sexuality as a Dimension of Human Experience and Spiritual Practice

- Sexuality is described as a profound mystery built into human incarnation, serving not only reproduction but also as one of the deepest drives for pleasure, beauty, and connection beyond the small sense of self.

- Sexual experience is often deeply connected to love and to the fundamental human longing to love and be loved.

- Western culture is identified as holding contradictory attitudes toward sexuality, simultaneously puritanical and highly sexualized, with pornography being the most frequent internet search globally.

- Advertising frequently exploits sexuality to sell products, further contributing to cultural confusion around the topic.

- A survey conducted in the 1970s and early 1980s of 52 meditation masters and teachers revealed an enormous diversity of sexual orientations and practices, demonstrating that there is no single correct relationship between spirituality and sexuality.

- Awareness, rather than adherence to a prescribed model, is presented as the appropriate starting point for engaging with sexuality in a spiritual context.

- Teachers are encouraged to reflect on their own family stories around sex, as these shape comfort levels and unconscious judgments that will affect their ability to hold space for students.

- Students will bring a wide range of sexual identities and experiences to teachers, including those outside heteronormative frameworks, and teachers are called to respond without nervousness, judgment, or shame.

- Sexual imagery and fantasy arising during meditation practice can often be traced to deeper emotional states such as loneliness, making sexuality a gateway for mindful inquiry into underlying needs.

- Students can learn through this inquiry to recognize where they are caught, what they judge, and how the energy of life can move through them in healthy ways, ultimately becoming a gateway toward freedom.

### Boundaries Around Sexual Conduct in the Teacher Role

- Flirting with students or signaling sexual availability is identified as clearly problematic due to the transference dynamics already present in the teacher-student relationship.

- Students frequently hold teachers in a special, idealized light, making them particularly vulnerable to harm if a teacher engages sexually with them.

- Andy McGuire shares a personal account of falling in love with a student and choosing to disclose this openly to colleagues and the training group, which dissolved the charge and prevented harm.

- Disclosure to colleagues and mentors is recommended as a key strategy when a teacher feels caught in attraction toward a student, as carrying it privately is unhealthy for both parties.

- When students bring accounts of sexual harassment or misconduct by others, teachers are called to listen with clear compassion and refer them to the ethics statement or resources such as the Buddhist Peace Fellowship publication Safe Harbor.

### Money as Energy and the Spiritual Confusion Surrounding Wealth

- Monastic and renunciate traditions have contributed to a widespread cultural message that money is dirty or unspiritual, despite the fact that sacred imagery appears on currency itself.

- Like sex and power, money is described as a form of human energy that is fundamentally neutral and can be used in either beneficial or harmful ways.

- Teachers who misuse money often do so from a sense of deficiency and a belief that they deserve compensation for what they have given, a dynamic that can escalate into serious misconduct.

- Many people carry shame or guilt about their relationship to money regardless of whether they have too little or too much, and this discomfort is compounded by spiritual teachings on renunciation.

- The broader societal context of economic disparity and systemic injustice adds further complexity to how teachers and students relate to money, raising questions about participation in unjust systems.

- A teaching attributed to the Buddha advises that it is not wealth and power that enslave people, but the clinging to and misuse of them, and that the path of unselfishness is available to anyone regardless of material circumstances.

- Teachers are encouraged to become comfortable with money as a topic because students will bring questions about inheritance disputes, career ethics, and financial decisions to them.

- An example from Sylvia Boorstein illustrates that charging a modest fee for classes resulted in significantly higher student retention compared to a donation-only model, suggesting that financial investment can reflect and reinforce personal commitment.

- Counting attendance and calculating income during teaching is identified as a sign of unhealthy entanglement with money, and teachers are encouraged to approach it with mindfulness, playfulness, and conscious stewardship rather than fear or grasping.

### Power, Leadership, and the Teacher Role

- Research is cited suggesting that excessive power can have measurable negative effects on brain function, though power itself is not characterized as inherently good or bad.

- Three distinct forms of power are identified, namely power over others, power with others, and the power of empowerment, each carrying different implications for the teacher role.

- Power in the teacher role derives from institutional authority, personal charisma, or the role itself, and all three forms require conscious attention.

- Charisma and wisdom are identified as entirely separate dimensions, with some charismatic leaders being selfish and some deeply wise teachers lacking charisma altogether.

- The greatest joy of teaching is described not as transmitting knowledge but as recognizing the inherent beauty and potential in students and helping it blossom from within them.

- Students will either idealize teachers beyond their actual qualities or project negative associations onto them based on past relationships, and teachers must hold both forms of projection with loving awareness without reacting.

- Bringing a needy ego, a desire for admiration, or a need to control into the teacher role creates suffering, and even mild reliance on student approval for self-worth is identified as an impurity in the teaching relationship.

- The true source of joy in teaching is described as the teacher's own inner freedom and love, communicated in a way that allows students to recognize the same capacity within themselves.

- Humility in the early stages of building a teaching community is encouraged, with examples of prominent teachers who began with only one or two students and found deep fulfillment in that.

- True leadership in the teacher role is framed as servant leadership, in which the teacher leads not through power over others but through empowerment and service to the well-being of those they teach.

- The mantle of the teacher role is to be held lightly, with humor and graciousness, and the foundational ethics remain consistent, avoiding harm, not placing oneself above others, and speaking truth when it prevents harm.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to engage in personal journaling and reflection on their own family stories and relationships to sex, money, and power as an ongoing practice.

- Consulting mentors and colleagues whenever situations involving sexual dynamics, financial confusion, or power imbalances arise is established as a standard practice going forward.

- Reviewing the MMTCP code of ethics and making it available to students when relevant questions or concerns emerge is recommended as a practical resource.

- The MMTCP alumni association is in the process of establishing an ethics council to provide structured support for graduates navigating these challenges.

- Teachers are invited to approach money, sexuality, and power not as problem areas to be avoided but as active domains of mindful inquiry that can contribute to their own growth and to the well-being of a culturally confused world.

- A wealthy merchant asked the Buddha whether he should give up everything and become a homeless wanderer

- It is not wealth and power that enslave us but the clinging and misuse of them
- The teaching does not require homelessness but asks everyone to free themselves from the illusion of separation from the rest of the world

### Stories
**The Dalai Lama Meeting and Silvia Wetzel's Guided Reflection**
Shared by Andy McGuire. Approximately 30 years before the session, Andy helped arrange a series of meetings with the Dalai Lama and teachers from Tibetan, Zen, and Theravada traditions to discuss teacher misconduct. A Western Tibetan nun who had spent 12 years in practice in the Himalayas described the poor treatment of women in Tibetan monasteries. The Dalai Lama wept. German teacher Silvia Wetzel then asked permission to teach the Dalai Lama a meditation, which Andy facilitated. She guided him to close his eyes and envision a parallel universe in which he was the 14th Dakini Dalai Lama — always incarnating as a woman — surrounded by female enlightened beings in paintings, female statues, and female advisors, with men asked to sit at the back and help in the kitchen. The room was stunned into recognition of the depth of patriarchal imbalance.
**Andy's Personal Experience with Romantic Transference**
Shared by Andy McGuire about himself. During his decades of teaching, Andy fell in love with a beautiful woman student who reciprocated. When she asked what they should do about it, Andy said they should tell everyone. He disclosed the situation to his training group and colleagues. After the disclosure and open discussion, the charge dissipated entirely.
**The Survey of 52 Meditation Teachers**
Shared by Andy McGuire. In the 1970s and early 1980s, Andy conducted a survey of 52 meditation masters, swamis, lamas, and teachers about their sexuality, later published in Yoga Journal. The results showed every variety of sexual expression — happy and unhappy celibates, monogamous and non-monogamous practitioners, polyamorous teachers, gay teachers, and others. The conclusion drawn was that there is no single correct model for spirituality and sexuality.
**Sylvia Boorstein's Class Experiment**
Shared by Andy McGuire about colleague Sylvia Boorstein. Sylvia taught a free 10-week course to 100 students at Spirit Rock. By the end, only 43 remained. Disappointed, she ran the same course again at $15 per class paid upfront. Of 100 students, 96 were still attending at the end of 10 weeks. The story illustrates that charging for teaching can increase student investment and commitment.
**Trudy Goodman's Beach Sits**
Shared by Andy McGuire about Trudy Goodman. When Trudy Goodman began what became Insight LA, she held meditation sits on the beach in Santa Monica. Sometimes only one person would come. She described feeling happy that someone saw her. The community eventually grew to spread across the city.
**Suzuki Roshi's Early Teaching**
Shared by Andy McGuire about Zen Master Suzuki Roshi. When Suzuki Roshi first began teaching in San Francisco, sometimes only one or two people would attend his classes. He expressed deep gratitude that anyone would sit with him and spoke of his love for practice itself.
**The Balinese Shaman**
Shared by Andy McGuire. While traveling in Bali, Andy sought out a famous shaman known for channeling remarkable beings. He was told the shaman no longer did that work. The shaman explained that the gods had come to him for a period, people benefited greatly, and then the gods indicated it was enough. The shaman returned to being a rice farmer. Andy expressed admiration for this humility — the willingness to acknowledge when a channel is no longer open.
**Andy's Own Experience of Sexual Imagery in Long Retreat**
Shared by Andy McGuire about himself. During long retreats as a young monk, Andy experienced persistent sexual imagery. Attempts to stop it or apply mindfulness did not resolve it. A teacher directed him to listen more deeply. Underneath the erotic content he found loneliness — being far away and feeling alone. The sexual fantasies were an unconscious response to that loneliness rather than purely sexual in nature.
**The Wealthy Man Who Died**
Shared by Andy McGuire. A brief story about a wealthy man in a neighborhood who died. When people asked how much he left, the answer was: everything. Used to illustrate the point that money is ultimately not something we keep, and therefore need not be feared or grasped.

- The psychological impact of spiritual betrayal by a teacher is described as profound — comparable to the traumatic ending of a close love relationship — and potentially requiring years or decades to heal
- Carrying unresolved transference or romantic feelings toward a student alone is described as unhealthy for the teacher and damaging to the student
- The ego's need for admiration, accolades, and control is identified as a psychological challenge particularly present in early-stage teachers
- Shame and guilt around money — whether from having too little or too much — are described as near-universal psychological burdens that affect practitioners and teachers alike
