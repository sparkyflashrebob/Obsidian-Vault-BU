### Outline
### The Role of Inquiry and Investigation in Mindfulness Practice

- Inquiry and investigation are considered key ingredients in awakening to full awareness across Buddhist teachings and most spiritual paths.

- Fundamental human curiosity, present from childhood, reflects an innate interest in understanding the nature of reality and a love for truth.

- The Pali phrase "Ehi Pasiko," meaning "come and see for yourself," encapsulates the principle that real liberation comes only from direct experience rather than belief in what one is told.

- Investigation is described metaphorically as shining a light in a dark room, where moving through life without awareness leads to repeated patterns of confusion and suffering.

- Unconscious patterns, including judgment, guilt, and limiting beliefs, are sustained precisely because the habit is to avoid looking at what is unpleasant.

- Carl Jung's view is referenced to support the idea that suffering and neurosis arise from unseen and unfelt parts of the psyche.

### Four Levels of Inquiry in Mindfulness Teaching

- The first level of inquiry focuses on the content of individual experience, including sensations, feelings, thoughts, emotions, and beliefs.

- A second level addresses relational content, investigating the interacting field between self and others, including qualities such as trust, attraction, understanding, and care as they arise within oneself, within another, and in the space between.

- The third level examines the process of experience through the lens of the three characteristics, namely the impermanence of all experience, its ultimately unsatisfactory nature, and the absence of a static or substantial self.

- A fourth level turns inquiry toward the nature of awareness itself, asking who or what is aware, and looking back at the very awareness that is doing the observing.

- Tara Brach notes that the session focuses primarily on the first two levels, with the latter two to be explored in upcoming sessions.

### What Investigation Is Not

- Investigation in mindfulness is not any mental process that takes attention away from the present moment.

- Analysis, abstract problem-solving, mental digging, or tracing current feelings back to past experiences are distinguished from genuine mindful inquiry.

- Such retrospective reasoning may have value in therapeutic contexts but does not cultivate a liberating presence.

- Answering the question "why" is not the aim of mindful investigation, as illustrated by the story of a novice monk asking an elder about death, where the elder's honest "I don't know" demonstrates the spirit of open inquiry.

- True investigation is directed toward what is present right now, not toward abstract or philosophical explanations.

### Attitudes That Support Wise Investigation

- A willingness to be uncertain is identified as one of the foundational attitudes enabling deeper truths to emerge during investigation.

- Arrogance or the need to present oneself as an authority closes off availability to mystery and genuine discovery.

- "Don't know mind" requires real courage, as there is vulnerability in entering a moment open-handed, open-minded, and receptive.

- A quality of heart, described as gentleness and care toward vulnerability, is equally essential before any meaningful investigation can begin.

- The image of a child returning home after being bullied is used to illustrate the need to create safety and warmth before inviting exploration of difficult inner experiences.

- Frightened inner creatures are described as needing a welcoming and warm atmosphere before they can emerge into open awareness.

### Investigating the Content of Personal Experience

- Intentional investigation is most necessary when a person is caught in a tangle, such as recurring patterns of anger, defensiveness, fear, hurt, or insecurity.

- The "I" in the RAIN acronym, standing for Investigate, is highlighted as central to the practice of mindfulness because it actively deconditions resistance.

- Physical sensations that have been dissociated from are included in the scope of investigation, with the suggestion to scan the body and even place a hand on the chest or belly to deepen contact.

- Buried emotions can be accessed by gently turning attention inward and asking what one is unwilling to feel.

- Beliefs operating outside conscious awareness are identified as capable of running a person's life, keeping them in a limited reality and reinforcing feelings that something is deeply wrong with themselves or others.

- Asking "what am I believing right now?" and bringing that belief above the line of awareness begins to dissolve its power.

- The underlying discovery is that suffering is often tied to believing something that is not true, described as arguing with reality.

### Applying RAIN and Inquiry Through a Case Example

- A young man new to a professional environment felt tongue-tied and embarrassed during team meetings despite being normally extroverted and verbal.

- Recognition involved naming the feelings of insecurity and fear, while allowing involved simply letting those feelings be present.

- Investigation began with the question of what he was believing, which surfaced the belief that others would see him as inferior and reject him.

- Directing attention to the body revealed a tight pressure in the chest and throat, followed by a hollow sense of shame in the gut.

- Asking what it felt like to live with this experience brought forward a sense of disconnection, smallness, and an old feeling of not being okay.

- Going directly to the young, frightened part and asking what it most needed produced a surprising answer, that it wanted to be accepted and not made wrong for being present.

- This shifted the process toward the nurturing phase of RAIN, where he gently sent the message that the fear was okay, that it belonged, and that it was part of him.

- Repeating this process over time gave him more equanimity and space, allowing his frontal cortex to reactivate and enabling wiser perspective, including the recognition that he was on a learning track.

- A shift in identity occurred through this process, moving from being identified with the scared self to becoming the mindful presence that could observe what was happening.

- Post-RAIN questions such as "if I didn't believe I was falling short, who would I be?" and "if there is no problem, what is here?" began to open inquiry toward the level of awareness itself.

### Investigating the Relational Field

- Relational inquiry involves bringing genuine interest and attention to what another person might be experiencing, including their suffering, needs, and what matters most to them.

- Teaching compassion and loving-kindness involves guiding students to investigate vulnerability in others as well as the goodness and what another person loves.

- Pausing to genuinely wonder "what are you experiencing, what do you need, and what are you longing for?" is described as a radical act given how self-referential human attention tends to be.

- Assumptions and interpretations typically replace real attention to others, even in close relationships, making intentional relational inquiry rare and transformative.

- At a societal level, bringing investigation and a willingness to be uncertain into dialogue across diverse identities and cycles of misunderstanding has the potential to undo deeply held biases.

- The example of the Dalai Lama meeting with a delegation from the San Francisco gay community illustrates how deep listening and genuine inquiry led him to publicly revise his position on homosexuality.

- Pema Chodron's words are cited to anchor this teaching, noting that the aim is not to save the world but to wonder how other people are doing and to reflect on how one's actions affect others' hearts.

### Teaching Investigation to Different Populations

- When working with young adults who may not be drawn to self-introspection, engaging them through small experiential exercises followed by peer sharing is suggested as an effective entry point.

- Posing a question such as imagining a stressful situation and identifying what might help in that moment begins to cultivate attention to inner experience without requiring explicit framing as meditation.

- Mindfulness programs in schools have found success by weaving together introspection and the natural interest young people have in each other's experiences, using circle formats to make the process enlivening.

- In secular or nontraditional settings, using the language of "interest" and "asking good questions" rather than terms like "inquiry" can make the practice more accessible and less charged.

### Guiding Group Investigation and Responding to Tangles

- When leading a group meditation focused on investigation, participants can be invited to bring to mind a challenging situation in their own life as their portal into the practice.

- RAIN provides a clean and straightforward structure for guiding group inquiry, moving from recognition through allowing, investigating beliefs and body sensations, and arriving at nurturing.

- Feeding participants possible beliefs, such as fear of failure or feelings of unworthiness, can help them begin to identify what is operating beneath the surface.

- Directing inquiry into the body after surfacing a belief ensures that investigation leads to full mindful contact with the felt sense rather than remaining abstract.

- Asking what the vulnerable place most needs allows the meditating person to rest in a larger, more tender space and naturally transitions into the nurturing phase.

- Post-RAIN inquiries pointing toward freedom from old identity, such as "who am I if I'm not believing something is wrong with me," can be introduced as comfort with the process grows.

### Managing Trauma Responses During Inquiry Practice

- Investigation practices carry the potential to trigger trauma, and it is important to explicitly give participants permission to stop at any moment if they feel overwhelmed.

- Visible signs of overwhelm such as uncontrolled shaking or sobbing indicate the need to gently interrupt the process and offer grounding options.

- Regrounding techniques include inviting participants to feel the weight of their body on the seat, feel their feet on the floor, open their eyes, look around, and mentally note objects in their environment.

- In one-on-one settings, the process can be more customized and responsive, whereas group settings require more generic guidance with built-in off-ramps.

- Participants generally self-select the depth of their attention, which means severe triggering is less common in shorter sessions but remains a possibility to prepare for.

### Working with Resistance to Uncertainty

- When a student clings to certainty and resists the openness required for investigation, the approach is to respect rather than challenge that resistance.

- Byron Katie's question "are you sure this is true?" is offered as a gentle way to introduce a small opening without forcing a shift.

- Mirroring back a person's certainty and asking what it feels like to be so certain allows the process of paying attention itself to unfold naturally.

- There is no need to shake up a person's certainty; acknowledging it and following where it leads is sufficient for the process to move as far as it can in that moment.

### The Relationship Between Investigation, Compassion, and Non-Identification

- Moving directly from investigation to non-identification is described as premature transcendence that skips essential steps in the process.

- The full RAIN sequence, ending in nurturing, creates an organic unfolding where unwinding emotional pain and beliefs naturally gives rise to tenderness and compassion.

- Completing the RAIN process shifts identity from the small self identified with beliefs and feelings to a more spacious, tender presence that has been holding awareness throughout.

- Resting in that spacious awareness after RAIN is strongly encouraged, as repeated immersion in that state builds familiarity and confidence that one is not the small self bumping against furniture but rather the light of awareness itself.

- Authentic non-identification is only realized by going through the full process of investigation and compassion rather than bypassing it.

### The Role of Referral and Complementary Modalities

- Meditation teachers are encouraged to recognize the limits of their training and to maintain a network of healers and therapists for appropriate referral.

- Body psychotherapy, movement, sound, visualization, and psychodrama are acknowledged as valuable modalities that can complement meditation practice.

- Deep inquiry work inevitably touches the domain where trauma lives and where deep healing happens, making collaboration across modalities both natural and beneficial.

- The current era is described as less rigid, with people increasingly benefiting from weaving together multiple healing approaches rather than relying on a single path.

- Meditation teachers are encouraged to trust that the tools they offer can open significant healing on their own, while also being honest about what falls outside their scope of training.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to experiment with investigation practices on themselves first, as personal experience will reveal which questions feel alive and will therefore be most effective when teaching others.

- Building on the foundational inquiry of "what is happening" and "can I be with this" by gradually introducing questions about beliefs, body sensations, and needs is recommended as a developmental path for new teachers.

- Future sessions in the training program are planned to explore the third and fourth levels of inquiry, focusing on the process of experience and the nature of awareness itself.

- Combining mindfulness tools with referrals to complementary therapeutic modalities is identified as a sound approach for supporting students whose needs extend beyond the scope of meditation teaching.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Nature and Purpose of Inquiry**
- Inquiry and investigation are considered a key ingredient in awakening to full awareness in Buddhist teachings and most spiritual paths
- In the Buddhist psychology, the Abhidharma, investigation is listed as a factor of awakening
- The Pali phrase "Ehi Pasiko" means "Come and see for yourself," reflecting that meditation is a path of direct experience, not belief
- Investigation is likened to shining a light in a dark room: without it, one bumps into furniture and repeats patterns
- Carl Jung's view is referenced: suffering and neurosis arise from unseen, unfelt parts of the psyche
- Trance is sustained and patterns continue when we do not look; the habit of not looking stems from wanting to avoid the unpleasant and grasp after pleasure
**The Four Levels of Inquiry**
- Level 1: Inquiry into content — investigating sensations, feelings, thoughts, emotions, and beliefs within oneself
- Level 2: Relational content — investigating the experience of others and the interacting field between people, including trust, attraction, understanding, and care
- Level 3: Process of experience — investigating the three characteristics: impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and no-self
- Level 4: The nature of awareness itself — looking back at who is looking, investigating what awareness is
**What Investigation Is Not**
- Not any mental process that takes attention away from the present moment
- Not analysis, abstract thinking, or problem-solving
- Not mental digging into historical causes such as past experiences
- Not answering the question "why" in an abstract sense
- Therapeutic processes of tracing causes can be valuable but do not cultivate liberating presence
**Attitudes Supporting Wise Investigation**
- Willingness to be uncertain: allows deeper truths to shine through; requires courage and vulnerability
- "Don't know mind": putting down the need to present oneself as someone who knows
- Gentleness of heart: creating a safe, welcoming atmosphere before investigating, likened to making it safe for a bullied child to open up
- Interest and curiosity as the underlying orientation
**Investigation and the RAIN Framework**
- RAIN stands for Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Nourish/Nurture
- The "I" of RAIN speaks to the centrality of investigation
- Recognizing begins with the question "What is happening here?" and allowing asks "Can I be with this?"
- Investigation deepens by asking: What are you believing? How does that feel in your body? Where do you feel it? What does that place most need?
- Nourishing follows naturally once vulnerability and need are contacted
- After-RAIN inquiries include: "If I didn't believe I was falling short, who would I be?" and "If there is no problem, what is here?"
**Investigating the Body and Beliefs**
- Investigation includes physical sensations that have been dissociated from, emotions that have been buried, and beliefs outside conscious awareness
- Scanning the body — throat, chest, belly — and bringing a hand there makes investigation more pointed
- Beliefs outside conscious awareness can run one's life, create a sense of limited options, and reinforce feelings that something is deeply wrong
- Asking "What am I believing right now?" and bringing the belief above the line causes it to begin losing its power
- Whenever there is a belief, there is a corresponding felt sense in the body
- Directing attention to the body prevents belief investigation from spinning into abstraction
**Relational Inquiry**
- Investigating others involves asking: What are you experiencing? What do you need? What most matters to you? What are you longing for?
- Central to teaching compassion and loving-kindness: investigating vulnerability and goodness in another
- People tend to be self-referential, assuming and interpreting rather than genuinely paying attention to others
- Pausing to wonder "What is it like for you?" is described as rare and radical
**Teaching Inquiry in Groups**
- Guided meditations can invite participants to bring a challenging situation to mind as their portal
- RAIN can be used as a clean, straightforward guide for group investigation
- Feeding possibilities for beliefs (e.g., "Are you believing you're going to fail?") can help participants engage
- Inquiry into the body follows investigation of beliefs: "How do you feel that in your body? Where is it? What is it like?"
- The sequence moves toward contacting the felt sense and then identifying the need
**Trauma Awareness and Grounding**
- Participants can be given the option at any moment to open their eyes and discontinue the process if overwhelmed
- Signs of overwhelm include shaking, sobbing that feels out of control, and other visible signs of trauma
- Grounding techniques include feeling the weight of the body on the seat, feet on the floor, looking around the room, and mentally noting objects in view
- Generally, people self-select what they pay attention to and are not frequently triggered in group settings unless in a longer workshop
**Working with Resistance to Uncertainty**
- Personal style is not to poke at resistance but to respect it
- Byron Katie's question "Are you sure this is true?" can open a small space for wavering
- If a person remains certain, that certainty itself can be mirrored back and investigated: "How does it feel to be so certain?"
- The process of paying attention to whatever a person puts forward will unfold as much as is possible for that time
**Non-Identification and the Completion of RAIN**
- The shift from identified self to spacious presence is not a jump but an organic unfolding through the RAIN process
- Completing RAIN — especially the nurturing step — naturally produces non-identification
- After RAIN, resting in the resulting awareness allows familiarity with who one is beyond stories
- Rick Hanson's teaching on real learning is referenced: after investigating and nurturing, staying with and getting saturated in the realization of awareness that cares
- Jumping to non-identification without going through investigation and compassion is described as premature transcendence
**Teaching Inquiry to Young Adults**
- Young people living with reactivity, peer pressure, and the drive to conform may not be drawn to self-introspection
- Entry points include stories, small experiential tastes, and group sharing of what was noticed
- Posing a question for participants to share on engages attention
- Example prompt: imagining a stressful situation and asking what one could remember or do to feel better
- In Washington, mindfulness is taught in schools to over 15,000 children, woven together with circle sharing and peer interest
**Language in Secular Settings**
- "Investigation" is described as broad enough not to offend in nontraditional settings
- "Inquiry" may feel more charged in some contexts
- Using the word "interest" and framing it as "bring your interest to this and ask some good questions" can serve as a secular equivalent
**Referral to Other Modalities**
- Body psychotherapy, movement, sound, visualization, psychodrama, and other modalities are acknowledged as valuable
- Meditation teachers are encouraged to know the limits of their training and refer students to therapists and healers as appropriate
- Building a network of healers and therapists for referral is encouraged
- Combining modalities is described as increasingly common and beneficial
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Investigation as a Path to Liberation**
- The only real liberation comes from direct experience, which arises through investigating rather than through belief or tradition
- Inquiry deconditions resistance by bringing into full awareness the life that is present
- When investigation and nurturing are complete, there is a shift in identity from the scared, reactive self to the mindful presence that can witness what is happening
- The meditator moves from bumping around in the dark room to being the light shining on the room
**The Collective and Societal Dimension of Inquiry**
- Investigating the inner experience of those with diverse identities and those caught in cycles of misunderstanding and violence can undo bias
- Mutual inquiry and willingness to be uncertain are presented as foundations for societal transformation
- The Dalai Lama's example illustrates how genuine inquiry and deep listening can lead to a public change of position on a significant social issue
- Globally, ongoing dialogues with mutual inquiry and willingness to be uncertain are offered as a model for resolving conflict
**Shared Awareness and Collective Heart**
- The session closes with a collective meditation sensing shared awareness and extending goodwill to all living beings
- The sense of a collective or shared heart space is described as including all living beings and as the ground of the practice
**The Dalai Lama and Homosexuality**
- When visiting San Francisco, the Dalai Lama initially stated that his religion did not approve of homosexuality

### Stories
**The Father, Son, and the Dead Seagull**
- Shared by Tara Brach
- A father and son are walking on a beach. The son sees a dead seagull and asks what happened. The father says the seagull died and went to heaven. The son's follow-up question: "Did God throw him back?" Used to illustrate the innate curiosity and interest in how things are that underlies all learning.
**The Novice and the Elder Monk**
- Shared by Tara Brach
- A novice asks an elder monk what happens after we die. The elder monk says he does not know. The novice is upset and says he thought the monk was a monk. The elder monk replies he is, but not a dead one. Used to illustrate that investigation is not about abstract or metaphysical "why" questions but about what is present.
**The Young Man in the Environmental Nonprofit**
- Shared by Tara Brach, referring to a young man from her meditation community in Washington
- Fresh out of grad school, the young man joined a team at an environmental nonprofit. Normally extroverted and verbal, he became tongue-tied and inarticulate during weekly team meetings, feeling out of his league among bright colleagues. Tara worked through RAIN with him: recognizing fear and insecurity, allowing it, investigating the belief that others would see him as inferior and reject him, locating the fear as tight pressure in his chest and throat and shame as a hollow sense in his gut. The vulnerable part expressed a need to be accepted and to belong. He sent the message "It's okay, you can be here." Over time, repeating this process gave him more space, reactivated his frontal cortex, and allowed him to remember he was on a learning track. He also began relational inquiry, sensing that others too dealt with desires to succeed and fears of failing. Used to illustrate the full arc of mindful investigation using RAIN.
**The Dalai Lama and the San Francisco Gay Community**
- Shared by Tara Brach
- The Dalai Lama visited San Francisco and was asked his position on homosexuality. He stated his religion did not approve. The San Francisco gay community requested a meeting to discuss his position. He met with a delegation, asked questions, and listened deeply as they spoke about the suffering and violence of discrimination. After the long meeting, he publicly announced he had changed his position. Used to illustrate the power of genuine inquiry, deep listening, and willingness to be uncertain in transforming understanding at a societal level.
**The Bullied Child**
- Shared by Tara Brach
- A child comes home from school after being bullied. Before finding out what happened, a parent must first make it safe enough, bringing gentleness and care to the vulnerability present. Used as a metaphor for the quality of heart needed before investigating difficult inner experience.
**The Frightened Creatures in the Dark**
- Shared by Tara Brach
- Frightened creatures take refuge in the darkness. To invite them into an open, sun-filled field, one must create a welcoming, warm, safe atmosphere. Used as a metaphor for the inner parts of oneself that need a gentle, safe invitation before they can be investigated.

- Physical sensations that have been dissociated from — in the throat, chest, and belly — are part of what investigation brings back into awareness
