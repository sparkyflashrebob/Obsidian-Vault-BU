### Outline
### Introduction to Reward-Based Learning and Habit Formation

- Reward-based learning is illustrated through the example of visitors at the Louvre taking selfies and posting them on Facebook, demonstrating how the brain redirects attention from the present experience toward seeking social validation.

- A Harvard study conducted in an fMRI scanner found that people preferred self-disclosure over earning money, activating the nucleus accumbens, which is the downstream dopamine pathway associated with every known drug of abuse including alcohol, cocaine, heroin, and social media.

- Activation levels in the nucleus accumbens were found to predict the amount of time people spent on Facebook, indicating an inherently rewarding quality to self-referential online behavior.

- A separate study using adolescents' Instagram feeds showed that the number of likes received activated both the nucleus accumbens and the posterior cingulate cortex, a self-referential brain region that would later prove significant in meditation research.

- Ancient Buddhist psychology described this process 2,500 years ago through the concept of dependent origination, in which a trigger is interpreted as pleasant or unpleasant, leading to craving or aversion, a resulting behavior, and a memory that reinforces repetition of that behavior.

- This loop was classically termed samsara, meaning endless wandering, reflecting how the pursuit of pleasure and avoidance of discomfort perpetuates habitual behavior indefinitely.

- Ignorance, described as the first link in dependent origination, corresponds to the modern concept of subjective bias, whereby past actions distort perception and increase the likelihood of repeating the same behaviors in the future.

- Positive and negative reinforcement, as described by B.F. Skinner and confirmed at the evolutionary level by Eric Kandel's Nobel Prize-winning research on sea slugs, align directly with the dependent origination framework and confirm that this learning process is deeply conserved across species.

- The habit loop is summarized as a trigger, a behavior, and a reward, with the example of stress leading to eating cupcakes and experiencing temporary relief reinforcing the cycle.

### Limitations of Conventional Addiction Treatments

- Avoidance of people, places, and things is a common strategy for alcohol addiction but becomes impractical for behaviors like smoking, which occur across many environments throughout the day.

- Substitute behaviors such as eating candy to replace cigarette cravings offer partial relief but carry their own consequences, with smokers who quit gaining an average of fifteen pounds through substitution strategies.

- Neither avoidance nor substitution strategies address the habit loop at its core, meaning the underlying mechanism of craving driving behavior remains intact.

- The Dhammapada is cited to reinforce this point, noting that just as a tree grows back if its roots are undamaged, sorrows will return if the roots of craving are not fully uprooted.

- Engineered junk foods, developed using techniques originally employed by the tobacco industry, are deliberately designed to maximize addictive qualities through optimized shape, smell, color, taste, and texture, exploiting the reward-based learning system.

### Mindfulness as a Mechanism for Breaking Habit Loops

- Mindfulness is defined as paying attention in the present moment, on purpose and non-judgmentally, with an attitudinal quality of curiosity and openness that resists being pulled toward pleasant experiences or pushed away from unpleasant ones.

- A clinical smoking cessation study randomized participants to receive mindfulness training or a gold standard treatment, with the mindfulness group instructed to smoke while paying close attention to the actual sensory experience.

- Participants who smoked mindfully reported that cigarettes smelled like stinky cheese and tasted like chemicals, demonstrating that direct attention to the reward naturally produces disenchantment without requiring willpower or avoidance.

- This approach is grounded in a passage from the Majjhima Nikaya in which the Buddha describes exploring gratification to its end, suggesting that clearly seeing the actual reward of a behavior is sufficient to reduce its pull.

- Disenchantment with a behavior reduces its reward value, and since reward drives behavior rather than the behavior itself, the habit loop begins to weaken naturally once the reward is seen clearly.

- The RAIN practice, which involves recognizing a craving, allowing it to be present, and getting curious about its physical sensations, is used to help individuals ride out cravings by observing them as transient bodily experiences rather than overwhelming imperatives.

- Mindfulness training was found to be twice as effective as gold standard treatment at the end of the smoking cessation program and five times more effective at the four-month follow-up.

- Rather than providing avoidance or substitution, mindfulness inserts a wedge of awareness between craving and behavior, dismantling the habit loop at its core by breaking the link that drives the behavior.

### Digital Therapeutics for Habit Change and Addiction

- Digital therapeutics were developed to disseminate evidence-based mindfulness training more broadly, with programs including Craving to Quit for smoking, Eat Right Now for emotional eating, and Unwinding Anxiety for anxiety-related habit loops.

- These programs deliver daily modules in bite-sized ten-minute sessions, allow users to revisit earlier lessons, and include in-the-moment exercises such as RAIN that can be practiced at the exact time a craving or anxious feeling arises.

- Embedded experience sampling within the apps allows both participants and researchers to track progress and test clinical efficacy in real time.

- A study by Ashley Mason at UCSF involving 104 participants found a significant thirty-six percent reduction in craving-related eating among users of the Eat Right Now program, demonstrating that the app could decouple negative emotions from eating behavior.

- Participant journals from the eating program revealed genuine conceptual shifts, with one participant articulating the full habit loop of using food to escape uncomfortable feelings and recognizing that the strategy ultimately fails.

- Another participant described the paradox that relaxing into a craving rather than fighting it caused the craving to pass, and reported feeling empowered by having survived the urge without acting on it.

- A participant in the anxiety program described a complete transformation in her relationship with anxiety over four weeks, shifting from identifying with anxiety as part of herself to experiencing it as a passing bodily sensation.

- One participant with panic attacks reported that turning attention inward during a full-blown panic attack caused it to melt away, illustrating the power of observing sensations rather than ruminating on their cause.

### The Neuroscience of Getting Caught Up and the Role of the Posterior Cingulate Cortex

- The concept of being caught up is presented as a continuum ranging from daydreaming, which occurs during approximately fifty percent of waking life and resolves quickly, to stress, which is harder to release, to addiction, defined as continued use despite adverse consequences.

- Research conducted in Europe mapped emotional body sensations using body silhouettes and found that fear, anger, and anxiety all produce a characteristic hot spot in the chest, reflecting the contracted physical quality of being caught up.

- A neuroimaging study comparing experienced and novice meditators found no brain regions with increased activity in experienced meditators across breath awareness, loving kindness, and choiceless awareness practices.

- Decreased activity was found in only four brain regions in experienced meditators, with the posterior cingulate cortex showing the most notable deactivation, the same region activated by Instagram likes and self-referential processing.

- Real-time neurofeedback using fMRI was employed to confirm these findings and to cross the gap between first-person subjective experience and third-person brain data, allowing participants to observe their own posterior cingulate activity while meditating.

- Participants reported high correspondence scores of approximately eight out of ten between their subjective experience of being caught up or on task and the red or blue fluctuations shown in the neurofeedback graph.

- Experienced meditators showed substantially more sustained deactivation of the posterior cingulate compared to novices, with their brain activity patterns appearing markedly different even during the same meditation tasks.

- Participant reports from the neurofeedback sessions confirmed that trying harder to meditate caused the brain activity to spike upward, while relaxing and stopping effortful control caused it to decrease, aligning with the Yoda principle of doing rather than trying.

- EEG source estimation neurofeedback was subsequently developed as a more accessible tool, demonstrated with Anderson Cooper whose brain activity shifted dramatically from a red spike during anxiety induction to a sustained decrease upon returning attention to the breath.

### Intrinsic Rewards and the Three Gears of Awareness

- Mindfulness can be understood as hacking the reward-based learning system by replacing the behavior of distraction or substance use with the behavior of curious, kind awareness, which is always internally available and does not require an external substance or action.

- Fear and stress tend to produce a contracted quality of experience, while joy and curiosity tend to produce an expanded quality, and this distinction can itself become an intrinsic reward that drives continued mindful behavior.

- The three gears of awareness provide a practical framework in which first gear involves recognizing being caught up in a habit loop by observing the trigger, craving, behavior, and reward.

- Second gear involves zooming in on the reward by asking the simple question of what one gets from a given behavior, allowing direct experiential disenchantment rather than cognitive reasoning about why the behavior is harmful.

- Third gear involves stepping out of the habit loop using practices such as RAIN, breath awareness, or loving kindness, all of which bring attention to the immediate moment and support a balanced, equanimous relationship with experience.

- Generosity, kindness, and gratitude are identified as behaviors that tend to produce an expansive quality of experience, making them naturally self-reinforcing once a person begins to distinguish contraction from expansion in their own body.

- A participant in the smoking cessation program described forming a new habit loop in which the craving for a cigarette automatically triggered recall of the unpleasant sensations of smoking, demonstrating internalized disenchantment.

- Another participant in the eating program described a shift in which weight loss became a side effect rather than the reward, with the actual reward being a more balanced life and inner peace.

### Research on Meditation Quality and Contraction Versus Expansion

- Studies examining Christian contemplative, Zen, and Tibetan practitioners across practices including gratitude, curiosity, breath awareness, and loving kindness all showed similar patterns of posterior cingulate deactivation, suggesting that contraction versus expansion is a universal element across traditions.

- This finding does not privilege any single meditation practice over another but instead points to a common underlying quality of effortless, open awareness as the active ingredient across diverse methods.

- The seven factors of awakening as taught in the Anapanasati Sutta are described as arising sequentially from the combination of awareness and curiosity, with energy, joy, tranquility, concentration, and equanimity emerging naturally from those two foundational conditions.

- Informal meditation practices were found in clinical studies to drive the majority of outcomes more than formal sitting practice, because applying mindfulness in the exact moment of a craving directly interrupts the habit loop in context.

- The Tibetan teaching of short moments many times was incorporated into the app-based training design based on this finding, with informal in-the-moment practices introduced before formal sitting practices for beginners.

- Quality of practice is emphasized over quantity, with the analogy that practicing scales out of tune on a violin is worse than not practicing at all, and that even a few moments of genuine present-moment awareness outweigh extended sessions of distracted sitting.

### Next Steps

- Ongoing NIH-funded research aims to test whether EEG neurofeedback can accelerate the calibration of meditative experience for beginners in MBSR programs, with the goal of helping practitioners develop their own internal feedback capacity more quickly.

- Further development of digital therapeutics is planned to continue expanding access to evidence-based mindfulness training for smoking, emotional eating, and anxiety outside of clinical settings.

- A head-to-head comparison of mindfulness training and twelve-step programs for addiction recovery is identified as an important open research question that has not yet been formally studied.

- Continued exploration of how informal mindfulness practices can be integrated into daily life is prioritized as a key direction, given the evidence that in-context practice drives stronger outcomes than formal meditation alone.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Reward-Based Learning and Habit Formation**
- A trigger enters the mind and is interpreted as pleasant or unpleasant
- Pleasant triggers create craving for continuation; unpleasant triggers create aversion
- A behavior follows, a reward is received, and memory lays down the instruction to repeat
- This loop is described in Buddhist psychology as dependent origination and in modern science as positive and negative reinforcement
- B.F. Skinner demonstrated this in animal behavior experiments; Eric Kandel received the Nobel Prize for showing it is evolutionarily conserved back to the sea slug
- The process was likely set up evolutionarily to remember food sources and avoid danger
- Alan Watts described the ego as nothing but a pattern of habits
- The Dhammapada states: just as a tree though cut down can grow again if its roots are undamaged, sorrows will come again if the roots of craving are not wholly uprooted
**The Brain and Social Media**
- A Harvard study placed participants in an fMRI scanner and offered them a choice between earning money and talking about themselves; self-disclosure was more frequently chosen
- Self-disclosure activated the nucleus accumbens, the downstream dopamine pathway from the ventral tegmental area
- This reward pathway is activated by every known drug of abuse including alcohol, cigarettes, cocaine, heroin, and Facebook
- A study with adolescents using Instagram found that receiving more likes activated both the nucleus accumbens and the posterior cingulate cortex
- The amount of time people spent on Facebook could be predicted by the level of nucleus accumbens activation
**Avoidance and Substitution Strategies and Their Limits**
- Avoiding people, places, and things reduces drinking cues but is impractical for smoking due to frequency and variety of contexts
- Substitute behaviors such as eating candy instead of smoking can help but carry downsides; average weight gain when quitting smoking is 15 pounds
- Neither avoidance nor substitution dismantles the habit loop at its core
**Mindfulness as a Behavioral Intervention**
- In a smoking cessation study, participants were told to smoke mindfully and pay attention to what they actually experienced
- One participant reported that mindful smoking smelled like stinky cheese and tasted like chemicals
- This approach is grounded in the Buddha's teaching about exploring gratification to its end
- Reward drives behavior, not the behavior itself; if the reward is seen clearly as unrewarding, behavior naturally shifts
- Mindfulness training was twice as good as gold standard treatment at end of treatment and five times better at four-month follow-up
- A wedge of awareness breaks the link between craving and behavior each time it is applied
**The RAIN Practice**
- Recognize the craving
- Allow it to be present rather than pushing it away
- get curious about what it feels like in the body
- A modified version incorporates Mahasi-style noting of moment-to-moment sensations such as tightness, tension, or burning
- Participants reported that relaxing into cravings made them less powerful and that surviving an urge without acting on it felt empowering
**The Posterior Cingulate Cortex and Meditation**
- No brain region showed increased activity when comparing experienced to novice meditators across breath awareness, loving kindness, and choiceless awareness practices
- Only four brain regions showed decreased activity in experienced meditators, with the posterior cingulate cortex being the key region
- The same region activated by Instagram likes is deactivated during meditation
- Real-time fMRI neurofeedback confirmed this finding by linking subjective experience to brain activity in real time
- Participants reported high correspondence between their subjective sense of being caught up or on task and the red or blue feedback on the graph
- Experienced meditators showed vastly more blue deactivation patterns compared to novices
**The Caught-Up Continuum**
- Daydreaming represents a mild level of being caught up, occurring approximately 50% of waking life, and is relatively easy to exit
- Stress represents a deeper level of being caught up that is harder to release
- Addiction represents the far end of the spectrum: continued use despite adverse consequences with no sense of control
**Digital Therapeutics**
- Craving to Quit: app-based program for smoking cessation
- Eat Right Now: app-based program for mindful eating
- Unwinding Anxiety: app-based program for anxiety habit loops
- Programs deliver daily modules in bite-sized pieces, allow revisiting of lessons, include animations, provide in-the-moment exercises, and embed experience sampling for tracking progress
- A study by Ashley Mason at UCSF with 104 participants found a 36% reduction in craving-related eating using the Eat Right Now program
**The Three Gears of Awareness**
- First gear: awareness of being caught up in a habit loop, including the trigger, craving, behavior, and reward
- Second gear: exploring the results of behavior by asking the question "What do I get from this?" to build disenchantment
- Third gear: stepping out of the habit loop using practices such as RAIN, breath awareness, or loving kindness
**Formal Versus Informal Practice**
- Research found that informal practices drove the majority of outcomes more than formal sitting practice
- In-context mindfulness, applied at the moment of craving, prevented perpetuation of the habit loop in real time
- The Tibetan teaching of short moments, many times informed the design of app-based training
- App-based programs begin with informal practices and introduce formal practices as participants build momentum
**Contraction Versus Expansion**

- Fear, anger, and anxiety all produce a hot, contracted sensation in the chest
- Joy and curiosity feel more expanded

**The Seven Factors of Awakening**
- Taught sequentially in the Anapanasati Sutta
- Awareness and curiosity are brought together and create friction that generates energy, then joy, then tranquility, then concentration, then equanimity

**Humility and Relational Habits**

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Dependent Origination as a Map of the Mind**
- The Buddhist concept of dependent origination, described 2,500 years ago, maps directly onto modern reward-based learning and positive and negative reinforcement
- The first link in the chain is ignorance, described in modern terms as subjective bias: our future behavior is biased by our past actions, meaning we do not see the world clearly
- The sense of self or self-identity arises through this loop, which is described classically as samsara or endless wandering
- The Buddha's contemplation of this process on the night of his enlightenment signals its central importance
**Disenchantment as a Spiritual and Neurological Mechanism**
- The Majjhima Nikaya describes seeking gratification in the world and seeing clearly how far that gratification extends
- Paying careful attention to rewards, and seeing them clearly as unrewarding, naturally produces disenchantment
- Disenchantment builds momentum and energy to ride out cravings without acting on them
- This is not a cognitive or willpower-based process but an experiential one rooted in direct observation
**Intrinsic Versus Extrinsic Rewards**
- Extrinsic rewards such as likes, food, or substances produce contracted, temporary relief
- Intrinsic rewards such as curiosity, kindness, generosity, and gratitude produce an expanded quality of experience
- One participant described the reward of mindful eating as a more balanced life or inner peace rather than weight loss
- Another described anxiety transforming from something identified as self to a body sensation that passes
**Societal Implications of Engineered Addiction**
- Big tobacco, after being exposed by Congress for claiming tobacco was not addictive, shifted strategies and purchased large food companies
- Food engineers were employed to design maximally addictive products engineered for shape, smell, color, taste, melting sensation, and crunch
- This represents a deliberate application of reward-based learning principles against consumer wellbeing
**Mindfulness as a Universal and Non-Sectarian Tool**
- Mindfulness practices across traditions all show similar neural signatures around contraction and expansion
- The approach does not privilege any one tradition or technique
- Digital therapeutics make evidence-based mindfulness training accessible outside clinical settings and at the moment of need
**Working with Family and Generational Patterns**
- The same habit loops that operate in individuals operate in family and societal systems
- Beginning with one's own patterns rather than trying to change others is the recommended starting point
- Noticing when one is leaning in, waiting to argue, or trying to force a point of view reveals the habit loop in relationship
- Humility and asking what one gets from a relational behavior opens the same disenchantment process available in individual practice

- Participants in smoking and eating programs initially experienced cravings as overwhelming, with some reporting feeling like their head was going to explode

**Anxiety and Panic Attacks**
- One participant described anxiety as so deeply identified with the self that it felt etched in their bones
- Another participant reported that during a full-blown panic attack, bringing awareness inward caused it to melt away
- The shift from identifying anxiety as self to experiencing it as a body sensation was described as transformative
**Stress and the Caught-Up Continuum**
- Stress is described as a deeper level of being caught up than daydreaming, making it harder to simply snap out of
- The physical quality of stress, fear, and anger was mapped in a European study showing hot spots in the chest, hands, and face
**Forcing Meditation as a Counterproductive Pattern**
- Multiple neurofeedback participants showed increased posterior cingulate activity when they tried harder to concentrate or pay attention more
- One participant noted that trying to look at the feedback graph harder caused it to go red
- Brewer notes that gritting one's teeth and forcing meditation is a common but counterproductive approach
**Daydreaming and Loss of Presence**
- Research shows that approximately 50% of waking life is spent lost in the past or future through daydreaming
- This represents a baseline level of being caught up that affects the continuity of mindfulness practice
**Old Habits and Waning Practice**
- Brewer acknowledges that old habits die hard and that awareness, like physical fitness, requires ongoing engagement
- He notes that intrinsic rewards of presence can become self-perpetuating, reducing the effort required to maintain practice over time
