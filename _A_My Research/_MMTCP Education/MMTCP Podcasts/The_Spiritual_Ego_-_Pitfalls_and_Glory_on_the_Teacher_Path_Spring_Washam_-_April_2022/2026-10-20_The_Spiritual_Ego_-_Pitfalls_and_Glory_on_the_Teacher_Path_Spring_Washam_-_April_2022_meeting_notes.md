### Outline
### The Role of Intention and Self-Reflection in Teaching

- Constant reflection on one's core motivation for teaching is identified as a foundational practice for anyone stepping into the teacher role.

- Motivations will never be entirely pure until full enlightenment is reached, meaning a mix of altruistic and ego-driven intentions is natural and expected.

- Maintaining awareness of the ego-driven portion of one's motivation, even if it is only 25%, is presented as the key to staying humble.

- A Tibetan teaching is referenced describing two streams on the spiritual path, one leading to Buddhahood and one to egohood, which look similar for a long time but have fundamentally different roots.

- Radical honesty about one's mind states and motivations at any given moment is described as central to navigating the pitfalls of the spiritual ego.

### Teaching as a Spiritual Practice and the Importance of Authenticity

- Approaching teaching as a spiritual practice rather than a performance is described as the foundation of a sustainable and meaningful teaching career.

- Every workshop, retreat, and teaching dynamic is framed as an opportunity for the teacher to learn and grow, with students serving as mirrors.

- Authenticity requires teachers to share their genuine struggles with students rather than projecting an image of effortless calm or perfect practice.

- A balance must be struck between appropriate self-disclosure and oversharing, as too much personal detail can overwhelm students and undermine the teacher's role.

- Teaching beyond one's actual level of understanding is identified as a danger, as it can lead to a self-deception where the teacher begins to believe they have attained more than they have.

### The Danger of Neglecting Personal Practice

- One of the most significant pitfalls for teachers is the belief that they no longer need a formal daily practice once they have reached a certain level.

- Gradually reducing or abandoning personal practice leads to a slow degradation of clarity and an increase in unconscious action.

- Spending time on retreat regularly and maintaining daily reflection and study are described as essential for keeping one's teaching channel alive and heart-centered.

- The Dalai Lama's four-hour daily practice beginning at three in the morning is cited as a powerful example of the ongoing commitment required even at the highest levels of teaching.

- When teaching begins to feel painful or joyless, it is presented as a signal to pull back, reconnect with personal practice, and reevaluate one's motivations.

### Navigating Jealousy and Comparative Mind Among Teachers

- Jealousy is identified as a widely present but rarely discussed challenge among teachers and spiritual practitioners, often dismissed as incompatible with a spiritual identity.

- Comparing oneself to colleagues who receive more opportunities, book deals, or public attention can lead to resentment and even hateful thinking toward people one genuinely cares about.

- Underlying jealousy is typically a poverty consciousness, a belief that there is not enough success, attention, or opportunity to go around.

- Awareness of jealousy as it arises is the first step, followed by using it as a conscious practice to examine one's own self-worth and beliefs about capability.

- Mudita, or sympathetic joy, is presented as the most powerful antidote to jealousy, involving the active cultivation of happiness for another person's happiness and success.

- Mudita is described as the hardest of the four Brahma Viharas to generate, precisely because the comparing mind operates so quickly and automatically.

- Visualizing the person one feels jealous of thriving, laughing, and being supported is offered as a practical method for shifting from poverty consciousness to genuine joy for others.

- Jealousy left unexamined can spiral into aggressive action and negativity, while jealousy used consciously becomes a path toward greater self-esteem and abundance thinking.

### Responsibility, Renunciation, and Boundaries in the Teacher Role

- Taking on the title of teacher carries a level of responsibility that requires a higher degree of awareness in all interactions with students.

- Renunciation is described as an inherent aspect of the teacher role, meaning that certain casual social interactions and personal freedoms must be consciously set aside.

- Students hold their teacher as a spiritual example and a figure of transference, which means the teacher's behavior outside formal settings carries significant weight.

- Attempting to socialize with students as peers after a retreat or class creates confusion and can lead to ethical violations, as the power dynamic does not disappear when the formal session ends.

- Seeking social or emotional nourishment from students is identified as a sign that the ego is looking for something it should not be receiving from that relationship.

- Having a separate peer group and maintaining clear personal boundaries are presented as essential safeguards against the erosion of the teacher-student relationship.

- Teachers who stop recognizing their leadership role and begin wanting to simply hang out with students are described as being at risk of causing harm and ethical confusion within their communities.

### The Spiritual Ego in the Context of Social Media and Public Visibility

- Teachers today operate in a glass house environment where their words, actions, and teachings are immediately recorded, shared, and scrutinized by a wide audience.

- Getting lost in social media metrics such as follower counts and platform growth is identified as a subtle but real threat to a teacher's authenticity and sense of purpose.

- Posting content should be driven by genuine intention rather than external pressure from publishers or comparisons to other teachers' online presence.

- Engaging with social media is acknowledged as a practical necessity for building a teaching community, but it must be approached with ongoing mindfulness about motivation and messaging.

- The current cultural moment is noted as one that has expanded the definition of what a meditation teacher looks like, making space for teachers of all backgrounds, body types, and identities to find their audience.

- Teachers are encouraged to trust that their particular frequency and style will attract the students who resonate with them, rather than trying to conform to an idealized image.

### Stepping Into the Teacher Identity and Overcoming Fear

- Fear of being seen, making mistakes, and feeling unprepared is described as a normal and even healthy part of entering the teacher role.

- Feeling completely ready and put-together before beginning to teach is itself identified as a form of delusion, given how much growth happens through the act of teaching itself.

- The ego will generate many reasons to delay teaching, including self-criticism about appearance, readiness, or qualifications, and these must be recognized as obstacles rather than truths.

- Owning mistakes openly and remaining curious about feedback allows new teachers to grow rapidly and build genuine credibility with their students.

- Having a cohort of peers going through the training together is presented as a vital source of support for navigating questions of identity, boundaries, and the challenges of holding the teacher seat.

### Building Community and Starting a Teaching Practice

- Beginning a teaching practice can start very simply, such as hosting a small group at home, before expanding to yoga studios, community clinics, libraries, or online platforms.

- Thinking of oneself as a teacher tends to attract opportunities to teach, as a shift in self-perception opens the door to invitations and connections that were not previously visible.

- Starting with a low-budget website and making oneself available to the local community is described as a practical and effective early strategy.

- Choosing a specific focus or niche, whether based on gender, community, relaxation practices, or mindfulness, helps new teachers identify where to begin and who they are serving.

- Committing to a regular sitting group, even weekly, is described as one of the most formative and growth-producing structures a new teacher can establish.

- Events with low attendance are reframed not as failures but as learning opportunities, and the act of showing up consistently for the heart of the work is what builds lasting community.

### The Value of Mentorship and Ongoing Learning

- Finding true mentorship is described as difficult but transformative, and teachers are encouraged to be specific about what kind of support they are seeking from a mentor.

- Having a tapestry of mentors across different domains, such as teaching, meditation, writing, and somatic practices, is more sustainable than expecting one person to fulfill all mentorship needs.

- Consistently showing up at a respected teacher's events is identified as one of the most effective ways to get on their radar and eventually develop a mentoring relationship.

- Putting out a clear intention to find mentorship is described as a practice in itself, with the expectation that the right support will be drawn in when the intention is genuine.

- Continuing to learn through new traditions, trainings, and areas of study is presented as essential to staying relevant, humble, and connected to the living practice of teaching.

- Remaining open to feedback through evaluations, recordings of one's own talks, and input from trusted peers is described as a critical feedback loop that keeps teachers grounded and growing.

### Next Steps

- Teachers in training are encouraged to establish or deepen a formal daily practice and commit to regular retreat time as a non-negotiable foundation of their teaching path.

- Developing clear personal ethics and boundaries before stepping fully into the teacher role is identified as a priority to prevent future harm to students and to oneself.

- Cultivating mudita as an ongoing practice is recommended as a way to address jealousy and comparative mind, particularly within peer groups and online environments.

- Building community through accessible local offerings and a simple online presence is presented as the practical starting point for new teachers.

- Seeking out mentors and peer support structures is identified as a key next step to ensure accountability, growth, and a healthy feedback loop throughout the teaching journey.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Intention and Motivation**
- Spring emphasizes the importance of constantly reflecting on why one wants to teach
- Motivations are never 100% pure until full enlightenment; a mix of altruistic and ego-driven motivations is normal
- Keeping an eye on the ego-driven portion of motivation is itself a practice that sustains humility
- A Tibetan teacher's teaching is referenced: two streams on the spiritual path — one toward Buddhahood, one toward egohood — look similar for a long time but have different roots
**Teaching as Spiritual Practice**
- Spring views teaching as a humbling, ongoing spiritual practice
- Every workshop, retreat, and teaching dynamic is an opportunity to learn
- The topics a teacher is drawn to teach are often the topics they personally need to grow in
- Teachers should not teach beyond what they genuinely know or have realized, as this can lead to self-delusion
**Authenticity and Self-Disclosure**
- Being real with students about personal struggles creates genuine connection
- There is a balance between appropriate vulnerability and oversharing
- Oversharing can burden students and undermine the teacher's role to inspire and uplift
- Most meditation teachers are actively struggling with their own practice, and acknowledging this is healthy
**Maintaining Personal Practice**
- One of the most significant pitfalls is believing one no longer needs a formal daily practice after becoming a teacher
- Stopping practice leads to gradual degradation of clarity and increased unconscious action
- The Dalai Lama practices four hours daily, waking at 3 AM, cited as an example of sustained commitment
- Regular retreat time keeps a teacher's channel alive, heart-centered, and less ego-driven
**Jealousy and Envy**
- Jealousy is a common but rarely discussed issue among spiritual teachers
- Comparing oneself to peers who receive more attention, book deals, or opportunities leads to suffering
- Unexamined jealousy can turn into resentment and hateful thinking toward colleagues
- Jealousy reflects a poverty consciousness — the belief that there is not enough success to go around
- The antidote is Mudita: the practice of cultivating happiness for others' happiness
- Mudita is described as the hardest of the four Brahma Viharas to genuinely generate
- Underneath jealousy is often deep sadness and the belief "I can't do it" or "I am not enough"
**Social Media and Platform**
- Teachers today must function somewhat like small businesses and engage with social media
- It is easy to lose sight of one's authentic purpose when focused on building a platform
- Spring shares her own experience of getting lost in platform-building pressure from her book publisher
- The key is engaging with social media authentically rather than performatively
- Social media can be used to connect with those who resonate with a teacher's particular frequency and style
- The Dalai Lama's use of Instagram, Twitter, and Facebook is cited as an example of using media in service of compassion
**The Teacher Title and Identity**
- Taking on the teacher title carries a level of maturity and responsibility
- Teachers are guides and spiritual examples for their students
- There is an inherent renunciation in the teacher role: limiting casual socializing with students, being mindful of physical contact, dress, and language
- Students hold teachers as spiritual examples, and teachers must recognize the power dynamic this creates
- Transference and counter-transference are real dynamics in the teacher-student relationship
- Teachers who do not recognize this dynamic risk hurting students through boundary violations
**Ethical Boundaries**
- Clear personal ethics and boundaries must be established before and during teaching
- When boundaries are crossed, it serves as a wake-up call to check in with one's practice
- Mistakes will happen; the goal is awareness, reflection, and learning rather than self-punishment
- Putting other teachers down, especially in front of students, is identified as an egoic behavior to avoid
**Feedback and Accountability**
- Incorporating evaluations into classes is recommended
- Recording oneself teaching and reviewing it is a valuable reflective tool
- Having a feedback loop of elders, mentors, and trusted peers is essential
- Teachers who go rogue and renounce their own teachers are identified as being at risk of ethical drift
**Mentorship**
- True mentorship is difficult to find but essential
- Spring recommends being specific about what kind of mentorship support one is seeking
- Having multiple mentors for different areas — teaching, meditation, shamanic practice, writing — is described as a tapestry of support
- Consistently showing up at a teacher's events is a practical way to get on their radar
- Spiritual friendships within a peer group can serve as mentorship in the absence of a formal mentor
**Building Community as a New Teacher**
- Start where you are: home groups, local yoga studios, community clinics, libraries
- A low-budget website and word of mouth are valid starting points
- Thinking of oneself as a teacher attracts teaching opportunities
- Starting small — once a month, a sitting group, a single class — is encouraged
- Every teaching experience, even poorly attended ones, offers growth
**Handling Praise and Blame**
- Praise from students often reflects the Dharma itself rather than the teacher personally
- Taking praise too deeply can feed grandiosity; taking blame too deeply can be destabilizing
- Spring describes herself as a channel: the student is touching their own insight, not the teacher
- Staying humble and maintaining high self-esteem without tipping into superiority is the balance to seek
**Fear and Imposter Syndrome**
- Feeling unprepared or scared is normal and even appropriate for new teachers
- Feeling completely ready can itself be a sign of delusion
- The ego will generate excuses to avoid teaching; the heart will signal genuine readiness
- Peer groups and mentors are key supports when navigating fear around stepping into the teacher role
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Two Streams: Buddhahood and Egohood**
- A Tibetan teaching describes two streams on the spiritual path that look nearly identical for a long time but have fundamentally different roots — one leads to liberation, the other to ego inflation
- This framework is offered as a lens through which teachers can examine their own motivations and trajectory
**Transparency in the Modern Age**
- We live in a time of radical transparency: teachings are recorded, shared, and commented on in real time
- This creates both accountability and vulnerability for teachers in ways that did not exist in earlier eras
- The immediacy of feedback — emails, social media responses — is a new form of mirror for teachers
**Poverty Consciousness vs. Abundance**
- Jealousy is rooted in a poverty mindset — the belief that success is finite and that another's gain is one's own loss
- The mindfulness movement is described as still being at ground level, with enormous opportunity for all teachers
- Shifting from scarcity to abundance thinking is presented as both a spiritual and practical necessity
**Mudita as a Societal Practice**
- The culture of internet trolling is identified as mass jealousy — people attacking others' happiness because of their own sense of lack
- Spiritual communities are not immune; subtle digs and dismissals of other teachers reflect the same dynamic
- Mudita — happiness for others' happiness — is offered as a direct antidote to this cultural pattern
**Renunciation in Contemporary Teaching**
- Renunciation is not framed as deprivation but as a conscious choice to hold a certain energy for others
- In a culture that prizes casual connection and social media intimacy, the teacher's need for appropriate distance from students runs counter to prevailing norms
- This tension is acknowledged as real and sometimes painful, but necessary for ethical teaching
**The Ego's Spiritual Disguise**
- The ego is described as enthusiastically adopting spiritual identity: wanting to be the most spiritual, look the part, perform wisdom
- This is identified as one of the subtlest and most persistent pitfalls on the teacher path
- Mindfulness of this dynamic is itself the practice that can transform teaching into liberation rather than ego reinforcement
**Historic Ethical Failures Across Traditions**
- Spring notes that ethical issues involving teachers have occurred across Catholic, Buddhist, and Insight traditions
- This is presented not as cause for fear but as context for why mindfulness of motivation and behavior is essential for all teachers

### Stories
**Spring Washam — Being Cornered into Teaching at Spirit Rock**
Spring describes how, coming out of teacher training at Spirit Rock, Jack Kornfield and another teacher cornered her in a room and essentially pushed her to start teaching. She resisted, saying she was not ready, that she had problems, that she was scared of being seen and of making mistakes. She eventually began teaching, and describes it as the best thing she ever did, though she was initially kicking and screaming.
**Spring Washam — Jealousy Toward a Colleague**
Spring shares a personal story about a friend and fellow teacher who rapidly rose to prominence — receiving book deals and being flown around the world — while Spring was struggling financially. She describes developing hateful thoughts toward this friend simply because of her success, despite genuinely loving her. This experience became the catalyst for her Mudita practice.
**Spring Washam — Getting Lost in Social Media After Book Publication**
Spring recounts how her book publisher pressured her to build her platform after the republication of A Fierce Heart. She began comparing herself to other authors with large Instagram followings and found herself subtly losing her sense of authentic purpose. She ultimately decided she would only post when it felt genuine to her.
**Spring Washam — Starting East Bay Meditation Center**
Spring describes being one of the founders of East Bay Meditation Center in Oakland, teaching large weekly classes of up to 100 people from diverse urban communities. She was initially reluctant and scared, but the experience became foundational to her growth as a teacher. She also describes the renunciation she encountered — realizing she could not socialize casually with students, attend their gatherings, or interact with them outside the teacher role.
**Spring Washam — Meeting Jack Kornfield as a Mentor**
Spring shares that when she was very young and seeking a teacher, she ended up on Jack Kornfield's interview list at her first retreat entirely by accident. She describes it as unheard of and attributes it to putting her intention out into the world. This encounter led to a lasting mentorship relationship.
**Spring Washam — A Teacher's Fall from Grace in LA**
Spring references, without naming, a well-known teacher and author in the Los Angeles area who had a significant ethical fall. She attributes it to the teacher not recognizing their role as a community leader and attempting to socialize casually with students after retreats, which led to confusion and ethical violations.
**Spring Washam — Starting Her Teaching Career at Home**
Spring describes beginning her teaching by hosting small groups at her home, making soup, and inviting friends to sit together. She then built a low-budget website and began receiving calls from the community to teach at local clinics and other venues in Oakland.
**Spring Washam — Catching Jealousy While Following the New York Times Bestseller List**
Spring shares a recent personal example of noticing jealousy arise while looking at authors online and seeing someone on the New York Times bestseller list. She describes catching the envious thought, recognizing it, and then consciously shifting to Mudita.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Fear and Resistance to Teaching**
- Spring describes experiencing significant fear about being seen and making mistakes when first stepping into the teacher role
- She recounts needing to be pushed by Jack Kornfield and another teacher before she would begin teaching
- New teachers are noted to commonly feel unprepared, scared, and as though it is hubris to assume they can hold space for students
**Jealousy as a Mental and Emotional Challenge**
- Jealousy is identified as a painful mental state that, if unexamined, leads to resentment, hateful thinking, and aggressive action
- Spring describes personally experiencing hateful thoughts toward a close friend due to envy of her success
- Underneath jealousy is identified as deep sadness and the core belief "I am not enough" or "I cannot do it"
**Poverty Consciousness**
- A persistent mental pattern of scarcity thinking is identified as affecting teachers at all levels, including very senior lamas
- This mindset contracts the practitioner and pushes away what they are trying to create
**Ego Inflation and Grandiosity**
- A gradual drift into grandiosity is identified as a risk when teachers receive significant praise or stop practicing
- The ego's adoption of spiritual identity is described as subtle and slow-moving, making it difficult to detect
**Degradation of Practice When Formal Sitting Stops**
- Spring identifies the cessation of formal daily practice as a significant risk factor for unconscious behavior and ethical drift
- The shift from heart-centered to head-centered teaching is described as a consequence of insufficient practice time
- Teaching becoming painful or joyless is identified as a signal that something has gone wrong and reconnection to practice is needed
**Social Media and Loss of Authentic Self**
- Engagement with social media is identified as a context in which teachers can lose sight of their purpose and authentic identity
- Spring describes personally experiencing this drift during the promotion of her book
**Imposter Syndrome and Self-Doubt**
- Spring is currently working on a second book and describes experiencing the same self-doubt and narrative of "can I do it" that she counsels others about
- She identifies old memories of being shut down — parental messages of "who do you think you are" — as underlying contributors to self-doubt in teachers
