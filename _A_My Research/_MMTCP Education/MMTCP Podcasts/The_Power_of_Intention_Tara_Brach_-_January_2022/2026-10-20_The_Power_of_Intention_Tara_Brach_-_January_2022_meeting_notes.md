### Outline
### The Relationship Between Intention and Attention in Buddhist Psychology

- Zen teacher Richard Baker described the very heart of Buddhism as the inseparable pairing of intention and attention.

- Deeper intention, such as a commitment to kindness or presence, is understood to produce fuller, more wakeful attention.

- Conversely, heightened attention brings practitioners into closer contact with what truly matters to them.

- Buddhist psychology holds that intention is the seed that creates the future, making it a subject worthy of dedicated focus.

- Beginnings of new periods, such as a new year or a new program, naturally invite reflection on the landscape of one's life and a rededication to what matters.

### What Distinguishes Students Who Continue to Unfold from Those Who Plateau

- Tara Brach observed across four decades of teaching that some students plateau while others continue to unfold in wisdom, insight, and openness of heart.

- The distinguishing factor is not the particular spiritual path followed, whether Christian, Sufi, Qigong, or Buddhist, nor the specific practice employed.

- Students who keep unfolding are those who remain in touch with a sincere and deep intention or dedication to waking up.

- This quality is described as an in-love feeling with truth and an in-love feeling with love itself.

### Deep Intention as Long-Range Aspiration and the Calling of Potential

- Deep intention is framed as a long-range aspiration rather than a short-term goal, representing the calling of one's awakened heart-mind to manifest.

- This aspiration arises from a felt sense of separation combined with a knowing that there is a possibility of manifesting one's full potential.

- The yearning embedded in deep intention is understood as the motivating force that draws practitioners toward awakening.

### The Jataka Tale of the Good Merchant as an Illustration of Aspiration Overcoming Fear

- The Jataka tales are mythical teaching stories about the many lives of the Buddha and the virtues expressed through his different incarnations.

- In one tale, the Buddha appeared as a good merchant in a small village in northern India who glimpsed a luminous, compassionate being crossing the town square.

- Instantly knowing he wanted to dedicate his life to awakening love, the merchant prepared an offering and moved toward the luminous figure.

- Halfway across the square, daylight turned to darkness, the earth quaked, a chasm opened, and terrifying demons appeared, representing Mara urging him to turn back.

- At the moment of greatest fear, the merchant remembered his longing for love and freedom, and that aspiration proved stronger than every voice of warning.

- Taking one step into the darkness and then another, he found the demons disappeared, the earth healed, and brilliant light returned.

- The luminous being embraced him and instructed him to walk on through all fears and pain, following his heart and trusting in the power of awareness, one step at a time.

- This story illustrates that egoic intentions pull people off course every day, and the practice of remembering deep intention is what allows practitioners to move through fear rather than retreat.

### Investigating the Mood Behind Daily Activity to Uncover Limbic Intent

- A useful inquiry for students involves reviewing the day and noticing not just the external activities but the mood or motivation underlying each one.

- Many moments throughout the day are driven by limbic intentions, such as avoiding discomfort, proving oneself, or relieving anxiety.

- Self-centered thoughts, which dominate much of ordinary mental activity, lead to actions that create separation from others.

- The Buddha taught that life is lived out of the tip of intentions, making the investigation of unconscious motivation a powerful and necessary practice.

- Because motivations are often unconscious, investigating what lies beneath thoughts, words, and actions becomes essential for both personal practice and teaching.

### The Concept of Marbled Intentions and the Role of Awareness

- Intentions are described as marbled, meaning that pure and egoic motivations are typically mixed together rather than cleanly separated.

- An example given is parental control, which can contain fear, love, and attachment simultaneously.

- The challenge is not the presence of multiple energies but the trance state that results from being unaware of them.

- Operating from an unconscious trance seeds the future with the consequences of unexamined motivation.

### The Process of Moving from Egoic Intent to Heart's Intent Through Self-Compassion

- The process of shifting from egoic to deep intention begins with becoming mindful of limbic intent, the fear and wanting arising from the survival brain's sense of unmet needs.

- Bringing compassion to that limbic stirring, rather than judging it, creates enough space and presence to ask the deeper question of what one truly cares about.

- Suffering must be met with self-compassion as a necessary step before the deeper intention can be accessed.

- A case example involved a long-time meditator whose decades of distance from her older sister was rooted in feeling unseen and disrespected.

- During a family gathering, a conflict over their mother's diet triggered familiar reactions, but her meditation practice led her to pause and investigate what was below the surface.

- Recognizing her unmet needs for respect and acknowledgment, she offered kindness to those parts of herself before asking what her deepest intention truly was.

- Her deepest intention turned out to be love and connection with her sister, which she named as her prayer and dedicated herself to.

- Releasing the demand that her sister understand or appreciate her, she became a better listener and less defensive over the following months.

- Intention is communicated through mirror neurons, meaning that when egoic intent is operating, others feel it, and when heart intent is operating, a different relational field is created.

- This shift from ego's will to the heart's will was described as creating different karma and seeding a different future.

### Guided Reflection on Limbic Intent and Heart's Intent in a Conflictual Situation

- Participants were invited to bring to mind a situation with another person where reactivity arises repeatedly.

- Turning attention inward, the practice involves sensing what unmet needs are driving the reaction, such as the need to be respected, seen, cared for, listened to, or supported.

- Identifying the limbic intent, the fear and wanting behind the reactivity, is the first stage of the investigation.

- Offering genuine kindness to the stirred-up inner experience allows it to belong as a natural part of human makeup.

- With compassion extended to the limbic experience, a sense of space, presence, and equanimity begins to emerge.

- From that more spacious place, the second question can be asked, which is what the heart's deepest intention truly is in the situation.

- Rilke's phrase "the winds of homecoming" is used to describe deep intent, as it brings practitioners more fully into who they are.

- Sensing the winds of homecoming opens up a greater sense of choice in how one might respond in the future.

### Cultivating Deep Intention Through Reflection on What Matters

- When there is no obvious suffering present, deep intention can be cultivated by simply reflecting on what really matters in one's life.

- Sufficient presence and attention are prerequisites for this reflection, as without them practitioners cannot tune in to what genuinely matters.

- Creating a clearing in the dense forest of daily life, as described by Martha Postleway, is necessary before meaningful reflection on intention can occur.

- Practical ways to assist students include inviting a reflection on intention after a sitting or incorporating a reflection on impermanence.

### The Bodhisattva Aspiration as a Template for Deep Intention

- The bodhisattva aspiration has two parts, the first being that whatever circumstances arise, may they serve to awaken the heart and mind.

- The second part is that the awakening thus cultivated may be of benefit to all beings.

- Participants were invited to identify a current difficulty, feel it in the body, and then sincerely offer the aspiration that these circumstances serve to awaken compassion.

- The prayer can also be framed as a question, asking how a given difficulty might awaken compassion, while still arising from the intention to wake up.

- Many people report that this frame inspires and resonates deeply, though some may not be able to connect with it at a given time.

- At the deepest level of silence and presence, this aspiration to have life serve awakening is understood to be present in everyone.

### Three Key Elements of a Living Aspiration

- The first element is that a true aspiration always concerns the manifestation of potential beyond the separate self, rather than external achievements or social approval.

- Embodiment is the second element, meaning that a genuine aspiration is felt in the body, as Oprah Winfrey expressed by saying the entire body will feel it when something is right.

- The third element is that a true aspiration can be experienced in the present moment, unlike a goal such as hiking a trail, which cannot be fulfilled immediately.

### Entraining to Deep Intention to Transform It from a State into a Trait

- When the winds of homecoming are touched, the instruction is to entrain to them by getting familiar and intimate with that experience.

- Drawing on Rick Hanson's work, the importance of turning a state of deep intention into a trait is emphasized, so that one's entire life becomes guided by the awake heart.

- In the moment of touching deep intention, the practice is to really get to know it and allow it to be absorbed fully.

- Expressing the felt sense of deep intention, for example through a phrase such as "I love you, beloved," helps to anchor and deepen the experience.

- Returning to that longing and tenderness whenever it arises, even briefly, gradually makes deep remembrance more consistently present.

- The aspiration to realize and live from loving presence, rather than merely to be comfortable or to look good, becomes the guiding orientation of practice.

### Distinguishing Deep Intention from Grasping and Manifestation Misconceptions

- The distinction between deep intention and grasping lies in where the motivation originates, either from a desire to inflate the ego or from the winds of homecoming toward wholeheartedness.

- The felt sense in the body is the most reliable indicator, as sincere aspiration produces tenderness and openness, while ego-driven manifesting produces grasping and tightness.

- Students can be taught to discriminate between these two through guided meditation and by learning to trace motivation back to its felt location in the body.

- An analogy of two people holding a knife illustrates that the same outward action can arise from entirely different intentions, one life-giving and one harmful.

- Intentions can easily become diluted or scrambled, making ongoing investigation of the felt sense an important ongoing practice.

### The Relationship Between Deep Intention and Narrower Goals

- A distinction is drawn between deep intention, understood as a long-range aspiration such as loving without holding back, and narrower intentions or goals that serve that deeper aspiration.

- Narrower intentions, such as staying present in the body while speaking or sensing connection with an audience, can shift and evolve during an activity.

- Allowing narrower intentions to change in response to what feels wise in the moment is appropriate, as long as the deep intention remains the guiding orientation.

- Goals are described as having a narrower aperture of awareness, being specific and particular, and are neutral in themselves.

- Whether goals create beneficial or harmful futures depends on whether they arise from care, creativity, and wisdom or from grasping and fear.

### Distinguishing Suffering from Valid Difficult Emotions

- The circle of awareness with a dividing line is offered as a teaching tool to help students understand the difference between experiencing emotions and being possessed by them.

- When emotions arise and are held in awareness, they function as waves in the ocean of experience, carrying intelligence and messages without defining the person.

- Emotions become suffering when they operate below the line of awareness, causing identification with them and a shrinking of one's sense of identity.

- The Tibetan Buddhist tradition is cited as honoring emotions as the richness and nectar of life, each one containing an embedded wisdom when held in awareness.

- The pathway of awakening through emotions, rather than around them, is described as a rich and valuable one.

### Understanding the Felt Sense When Setting Intentions

- Being aware at the level of thought, emotion, and bodily sensation simultaneously allows intention to come through in a more conscious and pure form.

- The felt sense refers specifically to the feeling sense in the body of energy and sensations, as distinct from thoughts or named emotions.

- When practitioners are fully present and sincerely ask what really matters, the intention that arises will not be about wealth or debt freedom but about living in integrity, honesty, or service.

- External goals such as financial security may or may not serve a deep intention, functioning at best as a strategy, but grasping around them will produce a felt sense of constriction that signals misalignment.

### Common Pitfalls in Teaching Intention to Students

- Insufficient presence is identified as the primary obstacle, as genuinely listening for deep intention requires time and a settled quality of attention.

- Students frequently land on habitual, prepackaged intentions such as being peaceful or being of service, which may not reflect their actual deepest aspiration.

- For an intention to be genuine, it must be felt viscerally in the body, making the practice of helping students arrive in their bodies a prerequisite.

- When students are suffering, they tend to loop in their thoughts rather than investigate intention, requiring guidance and experience to redirect attention toward inquiry.

- A case example of a woman awaiting cancer scan results illustrated how spiraling fear and anger can obscure deep intention until a period of quiet meditation reconnects the person with their core aspiration.

- Remembering her aspiration to be an instrument of service transformed her bodily experience and allowed her to rest in trust regardless of the outcome.

- Her reflection afterward was that not receiving the results immediately was a gift, as it reconnected her with what most mattered and with a sense that she would be okay no matter what.

### Closing Reflection on Impermanence and the Power of Conscious Intention

- Conscious intention in the present moment is understood to create the future, while the past cannot be changed.

- The point of empowerment lies in how one responds to what is happening right now, particularly by recognizing egoic or limbic willfulness and asking what truly matters.

- The shift from the ego's will to the heart's willingness to open and be present is described as the essential movement of the practice.

- Participants were guided through a reflection using the contemplation of impermanence, imagining having a year, a month, a week, a day, and finally only a few moments to live.

- At each stage, the invitation was to sense what activities and experiences would matter and to identify the deep aspiration guiding those choices.

- Surrendering into the longing and allowing it to fill one's entire being was offered as a way of becoming what one is longing for.

- Re-entering ordinary reality with that longing awakened and made intimate gives it the power to inform all moments and relationships and to seed the future.

- Hafez's teaching that every heart will get what it prays for most was offered as a closing affirmation of the formative power of deep intention.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to incorporate guided reflections on intention into their own teaching sessions, including mid-talk pauses for students to explore limbic and heart intent.

- Integrating reflections on impermanence into class openings or closings is recommended as a way to help students access deep intention.

- Introducing the bodhisattva aspiration as a template and inviting students to try it on is identified as a valuable teaching approach.

- Ongoing personal practice of entraining to the winds of homecoming whenever they arise is emphasized as the means of transforming deep intention from an occasional state into a stable trait.

- Developing skill in helping students arrive in their bodies before exploring intention is recognized as a foundational teaching competency to cultivate.

### Stories
**The Jataka Tale of the Good Merchant**
- Shared by Tara Brach, drawn from the Jataka tales — mythical teaching stories about the many lives of the Buddha
- A merchant in a small village in northern India sees a luminous, compassionate being crossing the town square and is instantly moved to serve the path of compassion
- He prepares an offering and crosses toward the being; midway, daylight turns to darkness, the earth quakes, a chasm opens, demons appear, and voices warn him to turn back
- At the moment of turning back, he remembers his longing for love and freedom; that aspiration is stronger than the fear
- He steps forward; the demons disappear, the earth heals, light returns
- The luminous being embraces him and says to walk on through all fears, following the heart, trusting awareness, one step at a time
- The merchant's entire being fills with light; he sees sacred presence shining through all of creation
**The Two Brothers and the Pancakes**
- Shared by Tara Brach as an illustration of self-centered motivation
- A mother is making pancakes for her sons Kevin, age five, and Ryan, age three; they argue over who gets the first pancake
- The mother uses it as a teaching moment, saying Jesus would let his brother have the first pancake
- Kevin turns to Ryan and says: "Ryan, you can have the first chance of being Jesus"
- Used to illustrate how self-centered intentions operate even in small, everyday moments
**The Woman and Her Sister at Thanksgiving**
- Shared by Tara Brach about a long-time meditator student
- The woman had decades of distance from her older sister due to differing lifestyles; she was seen as the family "bad girl"
- At a Thanksgiving gathering, a discussion about their mother's health escalated into conflict when the sister dismissed her suggestions about diet and nutrition
- The meditator left the room angry and hurt, with familiar childhood thoughts about not being respected
- Because of her practice, she paused, felt the anger and hurt, offered kindness, and investigated her limbic intent — the desire to be seen and respected
- She then asked her deepest intention and found it was love and connection with her sister
- She released the demand to be understood and appreciated; the rest of the evening she engaged without inserting opinions or defending herself
- At Hanukkah months later, there was more ease; they laughed together; the sister shared about a difficult time with her teenage son and thanked the meditator for being a good listener
- Illustrates the movement from egoic intention to heart intention and the different karma it creates
**The Woman with Cancer and the CT Scan**
- Shared by Tara Brach about a woman who had recovered from cancer and formed an organization to support other women affected by it
- Years after recovery, a chest X-ray showed a nodule; the doctor said it may have metastasized and ordered a CT scan
- Results were promised the next day; the doctor did not call back despite two follow-up calls
- The woman spiraled into fear, anger, and suffering — questioning whether her healthy lifestyle had made any difference
- She eventually settled into reading and meditating; in that quiet, she remembered her aspiration: "make me an instrument, use me"
- Remembering that aspiration changed her body's experience and brought peace; she was able to trust the larger unfolding whatever the result
- The results came the following day and showed nothing to worry about
- She said she was glad she did not get the results immediately because the waiting reconnected her with what most mattered and reminded her: "I'm not a body. Someday this body won't go on and I'll still be okay"
**Tara Brach's First Buddhist Retreat**
- Shared by Tara Brach about her own experience approximately thirty years prior
- A few days into her first Buddhist retreat, she became settled and touched a place of stillness and openheartedness
- She burst into tears with a feeling of "loving love" and sorrow about how many moments she forgets that state
- This led her to begin saying "I love you, beloved" as a way of entraining to that deep intention
- She describes "Beloved" as the loving presence that can be felt intimately
- Over time, whenever the longing and tenderness arise, she gets intimate with them as a practice, making deep remembrance more present
