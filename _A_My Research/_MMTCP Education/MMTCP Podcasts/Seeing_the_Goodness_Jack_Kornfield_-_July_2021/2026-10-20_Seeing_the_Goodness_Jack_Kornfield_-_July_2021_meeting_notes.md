### Seeing the Goodness — Jack Kornfield
📅 October 20, 2026
👥 Main Teacher: Jack Kornfield | Facilitator/Host: Steve Lessard | Introduction: Tara Brach
Tags: Mindfulness, Meditation, Teacher Training, Loving Awareness, Compassion, Basic Goodness, Buddhist Psychology, Skillful Means, Suffering and Cessation, Empowerment, Spiritual Practice, Paramitas, Buddha Nature
### Outline
### The Role of Seeing Goodness in Mindfulness Teaching

- Encouraging students and participants is identified as a core responsibility of mindfulness and meditation teachers.

- Imparting confidence, supporting students, and empowering them to take ownership of their practice are described as fundamental teaching objectives.

- Finding value in every question asked by students is presented as a practical method for demonstrating respect and fostering a sense of dignity in participants.

- Reframing difficult or frustrating questions to point toward deeper inquiry is highlighted as a key skill for teachers to develop.

- The Buddhist textual tradition of addressing practitioners as "nobly born" is cited as an example of how language can affirm the fundamental dignity and nobility of each person.

- Nelson Mandela's observation that people often act better when their goodness is acknowledged is offered as a guiding principle for teachers.

- Looking beyond a student's personality, body, or circumstances to perceive their deeper spirit or Buddha nature is described as an essential orientation for effective teaching.

### The Paramitas and the Concept of Fundamental Goodness

- The paramitas, or ten great perfections of the heart, are introduced as a framework from Buddhist psychology encompassing qualities such as patience, truthfulness, loving kindness, compassion, and virtue.

- A common misunderstanding of the paramitas is identified as the belief that spiritual practice is about perfecting oneself over vast periods of time.

- Teachers are encouraged to communicate to students that there is no such thing as a perfect person, perfect relationship, or perfect circumstance.

- Rather than striving for outer perfection, the practice is described as learning to see the inherent beauty and perfection of life as it already is.

- Fundamental goodness is presented as something already present in the heart that needs to be acknowledged and allowed to blossom, rather than developed through self-improvement.

### The Dalai Lama's Teaching on the Cessation of Suffering

- A series of mind-life meetings in Dharamsala focused on human flourishing, bringing together neuroscientists, contemplatives, and educators to discuss social and emotional learning for children.

- The Dalai Lama's guidance emphasized that teachers must not focus solely on suffering and its causes, but must make the cessation of suffering visible and central to their teaching.

- Making the end of conflict and difficulty a clear invitation for those who come to practice is described as a critically important responsibility for new teachers.

- Acknowledging the reality of suffering, its causes in reactivity, greed, hatred, and ignorance, and the possibility of its end are presented as the three essential truths teachers must help students discover within themselves.

- Living with a mind and heart filled with goodness and care, even amid life's inevitable difficulties, is offered as the vision teachers should communicate to their students.

### Understanding the Range of Suffering Students Bring to Practice

- Some students come to practice with a spiritual vision of what is possible, while many others arrive carrying immediate personal suffering such as divorce, financial loss, burnout, or family difficulties.

- Widespread anxiety about a divided society, concern for vulnerable populations, and fears about the future are acknowledged as common experiences among those who seek mindfulness practice.

- All forms of suffering brought by students are described as deserving to be held in deep compassion and loving awareness before any teaching or shift of perspective is introduced.

### Shifting Identity from the Small Self to Loving Awareness

- Once a student's experience has been acknowledged and held with compassion, the teaching practice is described as inviting a shift of awareness from the small, fear-based sense of self to a witnessing presence.

- Becoming the witness to one's own humanity means observing experiences of praise, blame, pleasure, pain, fear, joy, and sorrow without being identified with them.

- Ajahn Chah's response to a frightening experience is used to illustrate the quality of witnessing, where fear is acknowledged without being denied or amplified.

- Students are invited to step back from reactivity and recognize that the full range of emotional and physical experience is simply part of what it means to be born into human incarnation.

- Aldous Huxley's observation that an idolatrous religion substitutes time for eternity is cited to describe the liberation that comes from stepping out of the drive to fix or improve oneself.

- Becoming the space of awareness itself, rather than identifying with transient experiences, is described as the invitation teachers extend to their students.

- The teaching of Nisargadatta Maharaj is referenced, where wisdom recognizes itself as nothing and love recognizes itself as everything, pointing to the dual nature of mindfulness as both interconnection and impermanence.

### The Mystery and Wonder of Life as a Teaching Dimension

- Walt Whitman's poetry is drawn upon to evoke the miraculous nature of ordinary existence, from a blade of grass to the vastness of spiral galaxies.

- Encouraging students to hold their joys and sorrows within a sense of mystery is described as a way of opening them to a larger perspective on their lives.

- Seeing with clarity and love, and then offering a mindful and loving response to the world, is presented as the natural fruit of this expanded awareness.

- Pain and difficulty, when viewed through loving awareness, can be understood not as personal failure but as part of the shared human experience of birth, death, gain, and loss.

- Inviting students to witness their common humanity and their connection with the web of life is described as a transformative dimension of mindfulness teaching.

### Empowering Students Through the Recognition of Inner Capacity

- Malala Yousafzai is cited as an example of how grave difficulty can become the source of profound wisdom when met with loving awareness.

- Communicating to students that life becomes workable from the place of loving awareness is described as one of the most important gifts a teacher can offer.

- Chogyam Trungpa Rinpoche's description of difficulties as "manure for Bodhi" and Thich Nhat Hanh's teaching of "no mud, no lotus" are offered as ways of reframing suffering as a necessary condition for awakening.

- Mindfulness and loving awareness are described as becoming a kind of sanctuary that makes wherever one is more sacred, not in a formal religious sense, but through the recognition of mystery and joy.

- Students are encouraged to trust that mindfulness and compassion can hold all of life's experiences, and that who they are is not limited to their body, feelings, thoughts, or circumstances.

- Reminding students of their goodness and inviting them to grow in their trust and capacity to be present for all things is described as a central act of teaching.

### Skillful Means in Keeping Teaching Fresh and Authentic

- A participant raises the challenge of keeping expressions of gratitude to students fresh and avoiding a sense of inauthenticity when the same language is repeated.

- Taking time before teaching to personally reconnect with the meaning and impact of the practice is recommended as a way of bringing freshness to familiar expressions.

- Trying to articulate familiar ideas in new ways internally before teaching is suggested as a method for renewing authentic expression.

- Being transparent with students about the difficulty of finding the right words is offered as a way of modeling genuine presence rather than rehearsed delivery.

- Inviting the group to collectively offer their own expressions of gratitude or generosity is presented as a way of expanding beyond the teacher's individual voice to the wisdom of the community.

- Taking a moment of quiet before responding to a question is described as a practice that supports deeper connection with one's own authenticity.

### Practical Methods for Helping Students Experience the Cessation of Suffering

- Inviting students to bring loving awareness and compassion to a present difficulty during a class session is described as a direct way of making the cessation of suffering experiential rather than conceptual.

- Asking students to notice changes in spaciousness, relaxation, and breath as they hold difficulty with compassion is recommended as a concrete teaching tool.

- Inviting students to recall past moments when they were able to hold difficulty with greater ease, forgiveness, or mindfulness is presented as a way of pointing them back to their own existing capacities.

- Asking the group to share their experiences of inner freedom growing through a shift in perspective allows multiple voices to affirm the possibility of cessation, making it more accessible to all participants.

- Pointing people back to their own experience and capacities, rather than relying solely on conceptual explanation, is emphasized as the most effective approach.

### Adapting Language and Approach to Different Teaching Contexts

- Skillful means is identified as one of the most important principles in teaching, requiring the teacher to adapt language and approach to the specific culture and community being served.

- Teaching meditation to pregnant mothers, construction workers, or prisoners on death row would each require a fundamentally different approach and vocabulary.

- When the language of love creates confusion in a particular context, the language of mindfulness may be more appropriate, and the teacher's feedback from the group's responses should guide this adjustment.

- A Japanese professor's contrasting approaches to audiences in the United States and Japan is used to illustrate how cultural context shapes the opening moves of effective communication.

- In individualistic Western cultures, humor can serve to create a sense of connection before teaching begins, while in more collectivist cultures, an expression of humility may be needed to restore a sense of shared belonging.

- Despite the need for contextual adaptation, a hunger for love is described as present across cultures and contexts, and teachers are encouraged not to be overly cautious about introducing this dimension.

### Translating Core Teachings for Business Audiences

- A participant describes difficulty in communicating teachings on basic goodness to business-centric communities, noting that language such as "Buddha nature" or even "seeing the goodness" can feel like too great a leap.

- In business settings, the language of respect, dignity, and careful attention is recommended as a more accessible entry point for conveying the same underlying principles.

- Employees and coworkers who feel genuinely seen and listened to are described as working better and being more productive, providing a practical business rationale for mindfulness-based presence.

- Treating people with dignity is described as evoking a sense of well-being and capacity that is inherent in all people, and as part of the role of leadership to bring out the best in those they work with.

- Terms such as dignity, respect, caring, and empowerment, along with the benefits of stronger teams and more collaborative communities, are offered as appropriate substitutes for explicitly spiritual language in business contexts.

### Meeting Students Where They Are Before Offering Broader Perspective

- A participant raises the question of whether it is always appropriate to move directly to the ultimate dimension of teaching when a student is too close to their pain or fear.

- Acknowledging that someone in acute grief or trauma cannot simply be told that their suffering is part of the universal human experience is affirmed as an accurate and important read of the situation.

- Compassion, tenderness, and time to grieve are described as the necessary first response when someone has experienced a significant loss or carries deep trauma.

- Respecting the individual's experience in a meaningful, loving, and personal way is described not as the end of the story but as the compassion that begins to free the heart.

- Over time, whether days or weeks later, it can become helpful for a person to recognize that they are not alone in their suffering and that many others across the world share similar losses.

- The recognition of shared humanity in suffering, offered at the right moment, is described as an invitation to greater freedom rather than a minimization of individual pain.

- Knowing when and how to make this shift requires a sensitive heart and the teacher's own loving awareness, and must be approached one step at a time.

### Seeing the Goodness in People Whose Actions Cause Harm

- The question of how to see goodness in individuals whose actions are genuinely harmful or shocking is acknowledged as a compelling, powerful, and painful one that deserves a serious response.

- Bashar al-Assad is offered as a relatively non-political example of a figure who has caused enormous suffering, used to illustrate the practice of extending loving kindness even to those who cause great harm.

- Bringing such figures into a loving kindness practice using phrases such as "may you be free from hatred, may you be free from fear, may your heart be at peace" is described as a meaningful spiritual exercise.

- One effect of this practice is that it soothes the teacher's or practitioner's own heart, reducing inner reactivity without diminishing the commitment to justice or care for those who are harmed.

- Pouring reactivity and anger into already angry and harmful situations is described as unlikely to produce positive change, making the cultivation of inner equanimity a practical as well as spiritual concern.

- The interconnected nature of consciousness is invoked as a reason to believe that prayers and good wishes directed toward harmful individuals may have some effect beyond the individual practitioner.

- Consciousness is described as what we fundamentally are, and the seeds planted through loving awareness are said to enter the fabric of the universe in ways that science does not yet understand but that contemplative experience suggests are real.

### The Tao Te Ching as a Teaching Resource

- Passage 16 from Stephen Mitchell's translation of the Tao Te Ching is read as a closing teaching, recommended as a text that can be returned to repeatedly in one's own practice and in teaching others.

- The passage invites the emptying of the mind, the quieting of the heart, and the contemplation of all beings returning to stillness and to their source.

- Realizing one's source is described as the condition for becoming tolerant, amused, kind-hearted, and dignified, and for being able to deal with whatever life brings, including death.

### Next Steps

- New teachers are encouraged to continue developing their practicums with the understanding that there is no single correct way to teach, only the ongoing commitment to move in the right direction.

- Adapting language and approach to specific communities and contexts is affirmed as an ongoing practice of skillful means that teachers should continue to refine through direct feedback from their students.

- Cultivating personal practices of loving kindness, compassion, and loving awareness is identified as the foundation from which all effective teaching flows, and teachers are encouraged to tend this foundation continuously.

- Further broadcasts and training sessions are planned as part of the ongoing Mindfulness Meditation Teacher Certification Program.

### Stories
**The Scary Ride with Ajahn Chah**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to his teacher Ajahn Chah
- Jack was traveling as a monk with Ajahn Chah; they accepted an invitation to visit a village forest monastery on the Cambodian border
- They rode in a pickup truck driven by a young attendant who drove very fast on a one-and-a-half lane dirt road through mountains
- Water buffalo, buffalo carts, buses, and trucks came around blind curves, requiring the driver to swerve to the side repeatedly
- Jack was frightened and considered doing practices to prepare for death; he noticed Ajahn Chah's knuckles were also white and that Ajahn Chah had asked the driver to slow down several times without effect
- Upon arriving safely, Ajahn Chah turned to Jack, smiled, and said: "Scary ride, wasn't it?"
- The point: Ajahn Chah was not pretending the fear wasn't there but was simply witnessing it — like saying it was a scary ride at Disneyland
**Meeting the Dalai Lama in Dharamsala**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to himself and Trudy
- Jack and Trudy attended Mind Life meetings in Dharamsala with the Dalai Lama, focused on human flourishing, neuroscience, contemplative practice, and education
- They had a private sitting with the Dalai Lama who wanted first to talk Dharma and spiritual practice
- The Dalai Lama emphasized that teachers must make the cessation of suffering visible and central to their invitation to practice
**The Japanese Professor at the Jungian Conference**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Professor Kawai, described as a Japanese national living treasure
- Professor Kawai gave a talk on Eastern and Western psychology to approximately one thousand Jungian therapists in the US
- He opened with a joke, then explained: in the US, individualism separates people, and a joke brings them together so he can speak to them in a connected way
- In Japan, he would instead open with an apology, because the Japanese already feel connected and standing up to speak separates him from the group — the apology lowers him back into connection
- Jack used this story to illustrate cultural sensitivity and skillful means in teaching
**Tiffany Eastholm and the Nonviolent Peace Force**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Tiffany Eastholm, director of the Nonviolent Peace Force
- Described as a forty-year-old Western woman who has worked in Syria, the Burma border, the Philippines, and South Sudan in the midst of active conflict and war
- She listens to both sides of conflicts, trusting that if people can begin to listen to one another — exploring needs and fears — reconciliation becomes possible
- Used as an example of loving awareness in action in the world
**Julia Butterfly Hill and the Redwood Tree**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Julia Butterfly Hill, described as a friend
- Julia went up into a redwood tree to try to save it from being cut down, planning to stay only a few days
- She ended up staying for a year and a half
- Used as an example of starting one step at a time without knowing the full scope of what lies ahead
**March for Our Lives — Emma Gonzalez**

**Malala Yousafzai**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- Jack referenced the Nobel laureate young woman from Pakistan who was shot and then recently returned

- Many students come to practice carrying suffering: divorce, financial loss, fears, burnout at work, struggles with colleagues, family difficulties, anxiety about a divided society, concerns for the vulnerable, and grief over widespread violence
- Students who have experienced great trauma need to be respected, tended with care, and not rushed toward broader perspective — compassion and time to grieve must come first
- Parents who have lost children to accidents or overdoses carry almost unbearable grief that must be tended with every kind of care before any broader reframing is appropriate
- Fear is described as built into bodily incarnation — it is a natural response to certain circumstances, not a failure
- Jack noted that even Ajahn Chah experienced physical fear during the dangerous ride, with white knuckles, illustrating that fear arises in the body regardless of spiritual attainment
- The challenge of maintaining authenticity and freshness in teaching — a participant noted feeling inauthentic when gratitude expressions become repetitive
