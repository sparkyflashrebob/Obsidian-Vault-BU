### Key Points, Subjects and Themes
### Outline
### Meditation as Being Rather Than Doing

- Treating meditation as a scheduled activity creates an underlying assumption that it is something to be performed or achieved through effort.

- All doing occurs on the horizontal dimension of life, which is the dimension of form, thinking, time, past, and future.

- The essence of meditation is the realization of the vertical dimension, described as the dimension of being, which cannot be accessed through any form of doing.

- Continuous thinking constitutes the temporal mindset, and this mindset becomes a fundamental obstacle to the realization of being.

- A paradox exists in that many people require time and practice before they are ready to realize that being itself requires no time.

- Meditation practiced unskillfully can itself become an obstacle to the realization of being, rather than a gateway to it.

### The Two Dimensions of Human Existence

- Two vital dimensions exist within every person, the dimension of doing and the dimension of being.

- The dimension of doing encompasses form, thinking, time, past, and future, and is the space in which people become, acquire, and achieve.

- Most people are entirely consumed by the dimension of doing and know themselves only as a personality identified with physical and psychological form.

- Complete identification with form identity and the temporal dimension leads to frustration and a limited experience of life.

- The role of the meditation teacher is to point out the possibility of realizing the transcendent, vertical dimension that lies beyond the conditioned personality.

- Awakening begins when a person becomes aware of the continuous voice in the head that comments, complains, argues, remembers, and anticipates.

- This moment of awareness marks the arising of presence and is the point at which the vertical dimension opens up.

### Meditation as a Means to an End and Its Pitfalls

- Looking to meditation as a means of achieving a particular state of consciousness is a significant obstacle closely related to treating it as a doing.

- Seeking states such as enlightenment, bliss, or dramatic internal experiences keeps the practitioner oriented toward the future and within the horizontal dimension.

- Coming to meditation out of an inability to tolerate suffering is described as more conducive to genuine realization than coming with conceptual goals of achievement.

- People who arrive at meditation for egoic reasons, such as improving business effectiveness or gaining esoteric power, may still find that their original motivation transforms as they go more deeply within.

- Teaching meditation techniques remains valid regardless of the student's initial motivation, as the deepening of practice can naturally shift the practitioner's orientation.

### Preparing for Teaching Versus Allowing Teaching to Emerge in the Now

- A question was raised about whether a meditation teacher should prepare sessions in advance or allow them to emerge spontaneously in the present moment with the group.

- When a teacher is deeply rooted in presence and beingness, allowing the session to emerge from that state is described as infinitely more powerful than following a prepared script.

- The most important state for a meditation teacher to cultivate is the state of not knowing, which is freedom from mental conceptualization and thought.

- This state of not knowing is the same state Socrates referred to when he said he was the wisest because he alone knew that he knew nothing, pointing to a spaciousness beyond conceptual knowledge.

- In that spaciousness, the temporary form of the teacher merges with the formless and timeless essence, and words arise from that depth with a power that no prepared script could carry.

- Beyond the words themselves, there is an entire energy transmission, describable as presence or pure consciousness, that is conveyed to participants.

- Listeners who have even the slightest openness can sense this transmission, and something within them responds by recognizing that same state of unconditioned consciousness.

- Becoming comfortable with not knowing is described as the most powerful state from which effective teaching arises, and discomfort with not knowing allows the ego to block the transmission.

### The State of Not Knowing as the Source of Wisdom and Compassion

- Familiarity with the state of not knowing, understood as freedom from conceptual thought, is where wisdom, empathy, compassion, and love in their deeper sense naturally arise.

- While anyone can learn meditation techniques, the most vital skill for a teacher is the ability to access and become rooted in this state of presence.

- Even if a teacher cannot sustain presence in all areas of private life, they can still be highly effective in the teaching situation by embodying presence there.

- Continued practice of presence in private life deepens the teacher's familiarity with not knowing beyond the formal teaching context.

- Within the state of not knowing, a deep non-conceptual knowing exists, from which sudden insights, intuitions, and creative thoughts arise that were not previously known.

- Listening to another person in a state of pure openness, without internally formulating responses or objections, allows for spontaneous expression of things one did not previously know one knew.

- Dwelling in a state of no thought, described as pure presence, connects the teacher to what is called the deep I, the essence that connects all life to the source of the universe.

- Spaces between thoughts must be allowed to arise, both in formal meditation and throughout daily life, in order to remain connected to this deeper dimension.

### Non-Conceptual Knowing and the Experience of Presence in Daily Life

- Visiting a place such as a cathedral or forest with conceptual knowledge and a guided explanation provides information but misses an entirely different dimension of experience.

- Entering the same space without conceptualization, simply being present with the senses, allows for a non-conceptual knowing in which one merges with the essence and sacredness of the place.

- Pure awareness, rather than the screen of conceptualization, becomes the background to sense perception in this mode of experiencing.

- Walking through a forest in alert stillness, without conceptualizing, results in a merging with the environment in which the separation between self and other dissolves.

- Language creates nouns that imply autonomous, independent entities, but everything is ultimately a process of the totality in continuous flux.

- Alan Watts' formulation is cited to illustrate this point, describing the individual as something the universe is doing, just as a wave is something the ocean is doing.

- Meditation should not be a separate compartment in life but rather a quality of presence that permeates all of daily experience.

- The term mindfulness is noted as personally unused by Eckhart Tolle in favor of terms such as presence or pure consciousness, though the underlying meaning is acknowledged as equivalent.

### Practical Exercises for Accessing the State of Not Knowing

- Practicing the state of not knowing in small moments throughout daily life is recommended as a way to become familiar with presence outside of formal meditation.

- Every time something new enters the field of perception, such as turning a corner onto a new street or entering a hotel room for the first time, there is a brief moment of pure perception before conceptualization begins.

- Catching and extending that initial two-to-three-second window of pure perception, before the mind begins to interpret, is a practical method for becoming aware of oneself as awareness.

- As this practice deepens, the gap before conceptualization lengthens, and the practitioner becomes increasingly familiar with the state of presence.

- This state of not conceptually knowing is precisely where deeper knowing, creative insight, and genuine understanding arise.

### Working with Trauma, Pain, and Suffering in the Present Moment

- When trauma and pain arise, an important inquiry is to examine to what extent the mind is creating the suffering versus the actual situation producing it.

- In the majority of cases, the greater part of trauma and pain is produced by the mind's narrative about the situation rather than by the situation itself.

- Even minor situations such as being unable to reach a customer service line illustrate the same mechanism of unnecessary suffering that applies to more significant life events.

- The key question to ask is how one would experience a situation if the mental narrative asserting that it should not be happening were removed.

- What remains after removing the narrative is the bare reality of the situation, which either can be acted upon or simply accepted as it is.

- Arguing mentally with something that is already the case is described as a dysfunctional and in some sense irrational response, though it is widely normalized.

- Dropping the narrative and resting with the is-ness of the present moment removes a significant portion of unnecessary suffering from daily life.

- Unconscious thinking, defined as complete identification with the conditioned mind and belief in every thought that arises, is the root cause of dysfunctional emotional states.

- Times of challenge and discomfort are described as more conducive to spiritual awakening than times of comfort, as they provide the motivation to examine the source of suffering.

- The universe is described as arranged such that every life form experiences the precariousness and challenges of existence, and these challenges are the very conditions that can catalyze awakening.

### Collective Upheaval as an Opportunity for Awakening

- Times of collective uncertainty and upheaval are described as among the most conducive conditions for spiritual awakening, both individually and collectively.

- The responsibility of those who teach presence is to embody awakened consciousness and transmit it through the emanation of their presence, not only through words.

- Even teachers who have not yet achieved consistent presence in all areas of private life can still serve as powerful vehicles for teaching, provided they remain compassionate toward their own form identity.

- A significant danger arises when the egoic self identifies with the power that flows through the teacher, leading to the belief that the teacher is uniquely special.

- This contamination of teaching by ego is described as more insidious than purely egoic delusion, because it mixes genuine truth with ego-driven distortion, which is how cults can develop.

- Conceptual identity, built from thought-forms and narratives about who one is, is identified as a hindrance that must be continually released.

- The role of spiritual teacher is a function, not an identity, and stepping away from the teaching situation means stepping back into presence rather than carrying a self-image.

- True identity is described as presence or conscious awareness, which has no past or no future, and in which one is essentially nobody in particular, a state that carries enormous freedom.

- The same danger of adopting a new conceptual identity applies in monastic contexts, where the external shedding of identity through robes and a new name can be immediately replaced by identification with a new spiritual role.

### Pointing to Awareness of Awareness

- A practical pointer for directing attention to awareness itself involves inviting the listener to imagine having no past and no future and asking what remains.

- Asking what it feels like to be oneself without memory or anticipation directs attention to the essence of presence that persists independent of content.

- The question "Who am I" used as a meditative pointer, associated with Ramana Maharshi, is described as useful when the instruction is added that any answer expressible in words is incorrect.

- Formulating the question creates a final thought, after which a space of alert presence arises as the mind searches for an answer it cannot think its way to.

- In a high state of alertness, thought ceases, and what remains is the direct knowing of awareness itself.

- Language creates an apparent duality when one says "I am aware of awareness," but consciousness is in fact the eternal subject and can never become an object.

- Scientists searching for consciousness through physical investigation cannot find it because the very instrument of searching is itself the consciousness being sought, analogous to a spotlight looking for its own light.

- The statement "I am" is described as the purest pointer to being, representing the first-person singular of the verb to be, and connecting to the ancient inscription at the Temple of Apollo at Delphi.

- Most humans have forgotten the being dimension of their nature, living only as the human, the conditioned historical personality, without recognizing the being that they also are.

### The Purpose of Teaching Being and the Integration of Being and Doing

- The function of the meditation teacher is to teach being so that the being and the human can live as one integrated whole.

- All concepts and words used in teaching serve only as pointers, either pointing directly to being or pointing to the obstacles that arise in its realization.

- Students who have initial glimpses of being during meditation or retreat must then integrate that realization into daily life, bringing being into the dimension of doing.

- When being and doing are integrated, the unconditioned dimension shines through the conditioned, and the transcendent shines through the temporal even during ordinary activity.

- Embodying this integration is described as the life purpose of every human being, whether or not they are yet aware of it.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to prioritize cultivating and sustaining the state of not knowing and presence as the foundation of their teaching practice.

- Ongoing integration of presence into private life is identified as essential for deepening the quality of teaching over time.

- Vigilance is recommended to prevent egoic identification from contaminating the teaching situation, particularly as students begin to project power onto the teacher.

- The broader aim discussed is for participants to embody awakened consciousness and contribute to collective awakening during a period of global upheaval.

### Stories
**Eckhart Tolle — Informal Teaching Encounters**
Eckhart described meeting people by accident, having conversations that began with ordinary topics such as the weather, and then entering a state of pure, open listening. From that state of spaciousness, without formulating answers in advance, he found himself saying things he had not known before the moment of speaking. He offered this as an illustration of wisdom arising from the state of not knowing.
**Eckhart Tolle — Living on Park Benches**
Eckhart recounted a period of his life when he had no home and spent significant time on park benches. He described this not as a time of suffering but as a time of presence, because he had already released the complaining narrative voice of the ego. He contrasted this with how the same situation would have been experienced had he remained identified with a narrative about his circumstances, his past decisions, and his future prospects — which would have produced suffering. He used this story to illustrate that suffering arises from the narrative, not from the situation itself.
**Eckhart Tolle — The Cathedral or Forest Analogy**
Eckhart described two ways of experiencing a cathedral or a forest. The first is through conceptualization — using an audio guide, learning facts, being able to talk about the place afterward. The second is entering without conceptualization, simply being present with the senses, and experiencing a non-conceptual knowing — a merging with the sacredness of the place. He used this to illustrate the difference between conceptual knowledge and the deeper, non-conceptual modality of knowing.
**Eckhart Tolle — The Spotlight Looking for Light**
Eckhart described scientists searching for consciousness by examining the brain — cutting it up, looking at molecules and chemical substances — and concluding that consciousness must be an illusion because it cannot be found. He compared this to a spotlight looking westward, searching for itself, unable to find the light because it is the light. He used this story to point to the nature of consciousness as the eternal subject that can never become an object of its own search.
**Eckhart Tolle — The Monk or Nun and Conceptual Identity**
Eckhart described the practice of entering a monastery — giving up one's clothes, shaving one's head, receiving a new name — as a meaningful shedding of conceptual identity. He then described the danger that immediately follows: taking up a new conceptual identity as "a highly spiritual person," "a Buddhist monk," or "a Buddhist nun." He noted that in certain Southeast Asian countries, people show great respect to monks and nuns, and that this respect can feed a new egoic identity, replacing the old one rather than dissolving identity altogether.
