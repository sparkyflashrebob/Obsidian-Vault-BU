### Outline
### Definition and Three-Component Model of Self-Compassion

- Conceptualized as a construct rooted in Buddhist psychology, self-compassion was first formally researched and operationally defined by Kristin Neff in 2003 as no prior scientific framework existed.

- Drawing from how compassion for others is understood, the model was developed by examining what elements are required to feel compassion toward a suffering person, such as a homeless woman on the street.

- Three foundational components were identified as necessary for a response to qualify as self-compassion, presented as a Venn diagram showing mindfulness, kindness, and common humanity.

- Mindfulness is described as the first and necessary step, requiring awareness of one's own suffering without avoidance, resistance, or over-identification with the pain.

- Kindness refers to treating oneself with the same care and understanding one would offer a good friend, encompassing both soft qualities like soothing and comforting, as well as protective qualities like setting boundaries.

- Common humanity involves recognizing that imperfection, failure, and suffering are universal aspects of the human experience, distinguishing self-compassion from self-pity by replacing isolation with a sense of shared connection.

- All three components are required simultaneously, as the absence of any one element means the response does not fully constitute self-compassion.

### Physiology of Self-Criticism and Self-Compassion

- Self-criticism activates the body's threat defense system, also known as the reptilian brain, triggering the amygdala and releasing adrenaline and cortisol in response to perceived threats to one's self-concept.

- Fight, flight, and freeze responses manifest psychologically as self-attack, emotional isolation, and rumination respectively, all of which are natural but ultimately counterproductive attempts to feel safe.

- Self-compassion activates the mammalian caregiving system, also referred to as the attachment system, which evolved to ensure the survival of immature offspring through sustained parental care.

- Oxytocin and opiates are released when compassion is given or received, creating a physiological sense of safety that mirrors the comfort provided by a caregiver.

- Gentle touch and a warm tone of voice are identified as the two primary triggers of the compassion response, making physical gestures and vocal quality important tools in self-compassion practice.

### Self-Compassion as Loving Connected Presence

- An experiential description of self-compassion is offered as a state of loving, connected presence, where presence corresponds to mindfulness, connectedness to common humanity, and loving to kindness.

- This framing is presented as a more embodied and evocative way to describe self-compassion to students, compared to its conceptual three-component definition.

- When pain is met with loving, connected presence, it can be held without being suppressed, while simultaneously generating a positive emotional state that supports healing.

- This dual capacity to reduce negative states of mind and increase positive ones, such as happiness and life satisfaction, is attributed to the inherently positive quality of the loving, connected presence state itself.

### Common Misgivings About Self-Compassion and Research Disproving Them

- Five recurring misgivings about self-compassion are identified as consistently arising across diverse audiences worldwide, including fears that it leads to self-pity, weakness, reduced motivation, self-indulgence, and selfishness or narcissism.

- Research shows self-compassion reduces rumination and self-focus rather than increasing them, directly countering the fear that it leads to self-pity.

- A study of veterans returning from Iraq and Afghanistan found that self-compassion was a stronger predictor of PTSD development than the level of combat exposure, demonstrating that self-compassion builds resilience rather than weakness.

- Students helped to be self-compassionate after failing a vocabulary test studied longer and harder for a subsequent test than those given a self-esteem boost, showing that self-compassion increases motivation by reducing fear of failure and enabling recovery from setbacks.

- Self-compassionate people are shown to exercise more, eat healthier, and visit doctors more frequently, indicating that self-compassion promotes genuine self-care rather than self-indulgence.

- A study of 100 couples found that self-compassionate individuals were rated by their partners as more caring, more capable of intimacy, less controlling, and less prone to anger, demonstrating that self-compassion enhances rather than diminishes relational generosity.

- Being in a self-compassionate state provides more internal resources to give to others, whereas self-critical states consume those resources through isolation and negative self-focus.

### Research Growth and Measurement of Self-Compassion

- Over 1,200 empirical journal articles have now examined the benefits of self-compassion, following a trajectory similar to the growth of mindfulness research a decade earlier.

- A self-report scale was created to measure self-compassion by describing behaviors associated with both the presence and absence of the construct, and it has since been used in thousands of studies.

- Experimental methodologies are increasingly being used alongside the scale, examining how inducing a self-compassionate state changes behavior in controlled conditions.

- The scale is publicly available at selfcompassion.org for practitioners and individuals wishing to assess their level of self-compassion.

### Teaching Self-Compassion and the Mindful Self-Compassion Program

- Self-compassion can be learned and is not solely determined by genetics or early family environment, though both factors influence baseline levels.

- Mindfulness practice alone increases self-compassion for some individuals, as the act of turning toward pain with acceptance naturally fosters a friendlier relationship with oneself.

- For others, more explicit instruction is needed, which led to the development of the eight-week Mindful Self-Compassion program created with colleague Chris Germer, containing 26 concrete practices designed to build self-compassion as a teachable skill.

- A randomized controlled trial of the program showed increases in self-compassion, mindfulness, compassion for others, happiness, and life satisfaction, alongside decreases in depression, anxiety, and stress, with all gains maintained at one-year follow-up.

- Explicit training is considered more durable than implicit exposure to self-compassion through a teacher's warmth, as it equips participants with specific skills they can continue using independently.

- An adapted version of the program for teenagers called Making Friends With Yourself has also been developed.

### Backdraft and Managing Resistance to Self-Compassion

- Many people experience a negative reaction when first practicing self-compassion, a phenomenon termed backdraft by Chris Germer, referring to the release of old pain when compassion opens the heart.

- The backdraft metaphor describes how, just as firefighters avoid flinging open doors of a burning building to prevent explosive flames, self-compassion should be introduced gradually to avoid overwhelming emotional reactions.

- Backdraft is reframed as a positive sign that the healing process has begun, since suppressed pain must be contacted before it can be resolved.

- Teachers are advised to inform students about backdraft in advance so they do not interpret emotional distress during practice as evidence that they are doing something wrong.

- When backdraft occurs, returning to basic mindfulness practices such as breath awareness or grounding in physical sensation is recommended as a way to remain present without being overwhelmed by the affective intensity of compassion.

- Consciously choosing to step back from a practice when feeling overwhelmed is itself an act of self-compassion, as it reflects the habit of giving oneself what is needed in the moment.

- An alternative metaphor of warming numb hands after being in the cold is offered for contexts where the backdraft metaphor feels too intense, conveying the same message that temporary discomfort during warming is a natural and passing part of the process.

### Distinction Between Mindfulness and Self-Compassion

- Mindfulness is directed at experience itself, maintaining open, non-resistant awareness of whatever is arising, whereas self-compassion is directed at the experiencer, specifically when that experience involves suffering.

- Compassion requires the presence of suffering to be distinguished from loving kindness, which is a general open-hearted goodwill that can be felt regardless of whether pain is present.

- A common misuse of self-compassion involves using it as a tool to eliminate pain rather than to be present with it, which effectively becomes a subtle form of resistance.

- The paradox of self-compassion is articulated as giving oneself compassion not in order to feel better, but because one already feels bad, with the mindfulness component accepting the pain while the compassion component cares for the person experiencing it.

- Mindfulness and self-compassion are described as mutually reinforcing, with self-compassion creating the sense of safety needed to remain mindfully open to pain, and mindfulness providing the awareness that makes a compassionate response possible.

- Research indicates that teaching self-compassion before mindfulness increases the likelihood that practitioners will sustain their mindfulness practice, as it reduces self-criticism and frustration during the learning process.

### Adapting the Language of Self-Compassion for Different Audiences

- The term self-compassion itself can trigger resistance in certain audiences, making it useful to substitute alternative language that conveys the same meaning without the same associations.

- Alternative phrases such as inner support, inner strength, resilience, being a good friend to yourself, or being a supportive inner coach are suggested as effective substitutes in contexts such as sports teams or business settings.

- Asking people to compare how they treat their friends during difficulty with how they treat themselves is identified as a universally accessible entry point that does not require use of the term self-compassion at all.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Three-Component Model of Self-Compassion**
- Kristin Neff derived her definition of self-compassion by first examining what compassion for others entails
- The three components are: mindfulness, kindness, and common humanity
- All three elements must be present for a response to qualify as self-compassion
- Mindfulness must come first temporally, as awareness of suffering is a prerequisite for compassionate response
- Mindfulness in this context means being with feelings as they are, acknowledging pain without over-identification or avoidance
- Kindness means treating oneself as one would treat a good friend who is suffering
- Kindness includes both soft qualities (soothing, comforting) and active qualities (protecting, supporting, saying no to harm)
- Common humanity is the acknowledgment that all humans lead imperfect lives, make mistakes, and are flawed
- Common humanity distinguishes compassion from pity; pity involves looking down, compassion involves recognizing shared experience
- The state of self-compassion can be described as "loving, connected presence": presence = mindfulness, connectedness = common humanity, loving = kindness
**Physiology of Self-Compassion and Self-Criticism**
- Self-criticism activates the body's threat defense system, sometimes called the reptilian brain
- The amygdala is triggered, leading to fight, flight, or freeze responses, with release of adrenaline and cortisol
- Fight response manifests as self-attack and self-criticism
- Flight response manifests as isolation and withdrawal
- Freeze response manifests as rumination
- Self-compassion activates the mammalian caregiving/attachment system
- Mammals, unlike reptiles, evolved a caregiving system because their young are born immature and require extended care
- Humans have the longest developmental period of any mammal, making the caregiving system especially developed
- Self-compassion triggers release of oxytocin and opiates, creating a sense of safety
- The caregiving system is activated by gentle touch and gentle vocalizations
- Soothing touch and a kind tone of voice are practical ways to access the compassion response
**Research on Self-Compassion**
- Kristin Neff published the first operational definition of self-compassion and a measurement scale in 2003
- Over 1,200 empirical journal articles now examine the benefits of self-compassion
- The self-compassion scale is available at selfcompassion.org
- Research methodologies have expanded from self-report scales to experimental designs
**Five Common Misgivings and Research Responses**
- Self-compassion leads to self-pity: Refuted — self-compassion reduces rumination and self-focus through mindfulness and common humanity
- Self-compassion is weak: Refuted — research on veterans showed self-compassion was more predictive of avoiding PTSD than level of combat exposure
- Self-compassion undermines motivation: Refuted — self-compassionate students studied longer and harder after failure than those given a self-esteem boost; self-compassion reduces fear of failure and helps people recover and persist
- Self-compassion leads to self-indulgence: Refuted — self-compassionate people go to the doctor more, exercise more, and eat healthier; a compassionate parent chooses long-term wellbeing over short-term pleasure
- Self-compassion is selfish or narcissistic: Refuted — in a study of 100 couples, self-compassionate partners were rated as more caring, more capable of intimacy, kinder, less controlling, and less angry
**Mindfulness vs. Self-Compassion**
- Mindfulness is aimed at experience; compassion is aimed at the experiencer
- Mindfulness involves accepting awareness of what is happening without resistance
- Compassion specifically arises in the context of suffering; without suffering, open-hearted care is loving kindness rather than compassion
- Compassion involves soothing, comforting, and caring for the person having the experience
- A risk when learning self-compassion is using it as a new form of resistance — trying to make pain go away rather than being with it
- The stated paradox: self-compassion is given not to feel better, but because one feels bad
- Mindfulness and self-compassion are described as complementary, like a tango — mindfulness accepts the experience, compassion cares for the experiencer
- Self-compassion enhances mindfulness by creating a sense of safety that allows people to open to pain
- Research suggests teaching self-compassion before mindfulness increases the likelihood of maintaining a mindfulness practice
**Self-Compassion vs. Loving Kindness**
- Loving kindness is a general open-hearted, caring, friendly attitude that can be present regardless of suffering
- When loving kindness meets joy, it manifests as sympathetic joy (mudita)
- When loving kindness meets suffering and remains open, it manifests as compassion
- Compassion is described as bittersweet, tinged with sadness, because it involves opening to sorrow
- Both are rooted in an open heart
**The Mindful Self-Compassion (MSC) Program**
- Developed by Kristin Neff and Chris Germer over approximately ten years
- An eight-week program with 26 explicit self-compassion practices
- Includes a professional manual, workbook, and an online course through Sounds True
- A randomized controlled trial showed increases in self-compassion, mindfulness, compassion for others, happiness, and life satisfaction, and decreases in depression, anxiety, and stress
- Skills were maintained at one-year follow-up
- An adapted version for teens called Making Friends With Yourself has been created
- Explicit training is believed to produce more durable skills than implicit learning through teacher warmth alone
- The self-compassion break is highlighted as a portable practice combining soothing touch with words evoking the three components
**Backdraft**
- Term coined by Chris Germer to describe resistance or emotional release when beginning self-compassion practice
- Named after the firefighting phenomenon where opening a burning building causes flames to rush out explosively
- When compassion is introduced, old pain and memories of being unloved can surface intensely
- Backdraft is described as a sign that healing has begun, not a sign of doing it wrong
- Strategies for managing backdraft include: labeling the experience, returning to basic mindfulness (breath, soles of feet), physical grounding, and consciously choosing to close down
- Consciously choosing to stop when overwhelmed is itself an act of self-compassion and builds the self-compassion muscle
- An alternative metaphor: numb hands warming up indoors after being in the cold — temporary pain as warmth returns
- Teachers are advised to always inform students about backdraft before beginning self-compassion practices
**Language Adaptations for Teaching**
- The word "self-compassion" can trigger resistance, especially in certain audiences
- Alternative language includes: inner support, being a good friend to yourself, inner strength, resilience, supportive inner coach, courageous way of being with difficulty, friendliness, supportiveness
- Asking people how they treat friends versus how they treat themselves can illustrate the concept without using the term
- Example given: Kristin Neff spoke to the UT Longhorn men's basketball team without using the word self-compassion once
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Self-Compassion as a Buddhist Concept**
- Self-compassion is described as a central tenet of Buddhist psychology
- Kristin Neff encountered it through a meditation course she joined to manage stress following a divorce
- The concept of being a supportive friend to oneself during difficulty is rooted in this tradition
- Over-identification with suffering is identified as a Buddhist-rooted concept
**Shared Suffering and Human Connection**
- One of the most damaging aspects of lacking self-compassion is the feeling of isolation
- When people remember that suffering is shared and part of the human experience, any moment of suffering can become a moment of connection
- The irrational assumption when things go wrong is that "this is not supposed to be happening," as if a perfect life is the norm
**Compassion as a Resource for Others**
- Being in a compassionate state of mind provides more resources to give to others
- Self-criticism and isolation deplete the resources available for giving
- Self-compassion is not selfish; it actively supports more caring, intimate, and generous relationships
**Societal Resistance to Self-Compassion**
- Cultural norms do not encourage self-compassion
- Resistance is particularly strong among males, for whom self-compassion can feel soft or weak
- Fear of self-compassion is documented and consistent across diverse audiences worldwide
- The same five misgivings arise reliably regardless of the audience
**Compassion and Safety**
- Both self-compassion and compassion for others tap into the same physiological caregiving system
- Feeling safe is a fundamental human need; self-compassion provides safety through the caregiving system rather than through threat defense
- The caregiving system, not the threat defense system, is the healthy pathway to feeling safe in the face of emotional difficulty
**Motivational and Behavioral Implications**
- Self-compassion reduces fear of failure, enabling greater risk-taking and persistence
- When failure occurs, self-compassion prevents derailment and supports learning and recovery
- Self-compassionate individuals demonstrate healthier long-term behaviors across physical and relational domains

**Backdraft — The Burning Building**
- Chris Germer's firefighting metaphor is shared: firefighters do not fling open the doors of a burning building because the rush of air causes an explosive reaction
- Instead, they poke small holes to let air in slowly

**Numb Hands Warming Up**
- An alternative metaphor for backdraft: hands go numb shoveling snow, and when returning indoors, they begin to hurt as they warm up
- Applied to self-compassion: numbing oneself to pain is a survival mechanism; as the relationship with oneself warms, old pain surfaces temporarily before passing
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Backdraft as an Emotional and Physiological Challenge**
- When beginning self-compassion practice, individuals may experience a sudden and intense release of old pain and memories of being unloved
- This can feel explosive and overwhelming, particularly for those with a trauma history
- People with abusive histories may find soothing touch practices, such as placing hands on the heart, to feel terrifying
- Teachers are advised to always prepare students for this possibility before introducing self-compassion practices
**Resistance and Fear of Self-Compassion**
- Documented fear of self-compassion exists across diverse populations
- Resistance is especially pronounced in males and in business or performance-oriented contexts
- People may unconsciously use self-compassion as a new form of resistance — attempting to make pain go away rather than being with it
**Rumination as a Freeze Response**
- Getting stuck in negative thoughts such as "I'm so bad" or "this problem is so terrible" is identified as a freeze response within the threat defense system
- This is a mental challenge that self-compassion practices aim to address through mindfulness
**Stress Physiology of Self-Criticism**
- Self-criticism is associated with significant adrenaline and cortisol release, producing a high-stress physiological state
- The body reacts to threats to the self-concept as if physical survival were at stake
**Managing Overwhelm During Practice**
- When backdraft or overwhelm occurs, returning to basic mindfulness practices such as focusing on the breath or the soles of the feet is recommended
- Other options include taking a walk, petting an animal, having a cup of tea, or taking a warm bath
- Consciously choosing to close down and stop a practice when overwhelmed is itself considered an act of self-compassion
- Practitioners are encouraged to go as deep or as shallow as they choose, with no pressure to push through discomfort
**Self-Compassion and Abandonment of Mindfulness Practice**
- People who begin mindfulness meditation and lack self-compassion are more likely to give up the practice
- Reasons include self-criticism for not being good enough at it, frustration, or being unable to handle the pain that arises
- Teaching self-compassion before or alongside mindfulness is suggested as a way to support sustained practice
