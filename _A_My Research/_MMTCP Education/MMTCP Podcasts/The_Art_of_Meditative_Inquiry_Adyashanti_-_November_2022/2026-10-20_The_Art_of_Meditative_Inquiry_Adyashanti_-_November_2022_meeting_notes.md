### The Art of Meditative Inquiry — Adyashanti
📅 October 20, 2026
👥 Main Teacher: Adyashanti | Facilitator/Host: Christy Peoples | Introduction: Tara Brach, Jack Kornfield

### Outline
### The Nature of Meditation as an Art Form

- Meditation involves both a technical, disciplined aspect and a more creative, artistic dimension that emerges as practice matures.

- Transitioning from technique-based discipline to an art form represents a shift from trying to perform meditation correctly toward allowing it to breathe and expand beyond its structural bounds.

- At its deepest level, meditation functions as an embodied activity of curiosity rather than a purely mental exercise, making it difficult to reduce entirely to overt technique.

- Questions about oneself, life, and existence naturally arise through sustained practice, positioning meditation as a form of creative exploration into the deeper issues of the self.

### Groundlessness as a Shared Experience and Its Relationship to Practice

- A pervasive sense of groundlessness was identified as a defining challenge of the pandemic period, compounded by political, cultural, and global instability.

- Orienting beliefs, identity frameworks, moral principles, and group affiliations that previously provided security were described as being destabilized for many people during this time.

- The coronavirus pandemic was understood not as the origin of this groundlessness but as an amplifier of an existing undercurrent already present in the broader environment.

- Attempting to grasp for stability during groundlessness, whether through ideology, pleasurable experiences, or centering states, tends to increase feelings of fracture and fear rather than resolve them.

- A skydiving metaphor was used to illustrate how relaxing into groundlessness, rather than resisting it, can transform the experience from one of panic into one of unexpected refuge.

- Meditation was reframed as an opportunity to embody groundlessness rather than escape it, offering a path toward sanity precisely through the willingness to remain present with instability.

### Two Approaches to Mindfulness and the Value of Opening Awareness

- Mindfulness can be practiced as a narrowing of attention onto a single object in order to avoid disturbance, which was acknowledged as valid but limited in scope.

- An alternative approach involves opening the field of awareness so that all present experience, including anxiety, scattered thoughts, and difficult emotions, is allowed to exist simultaneously without suppression.

- Relaxing the listening faculty to receive all sounds at once was offered as a sensory analogy for this expansive mode of mindful awareness.

- Paradoxically, the more awareness is allowed to open and embrace the totality of experience, the more an inherent stillness and sanity become accessible within that openness.

- This wider stillness is not something to be grasped or focused upon but rather something recognized as already present within the midst of challenge.

### Meditation as an Embodied Truth Practice

- Truth was distinguished from abstraction, facts, or intellectual comprehension and reintroduced as an environment and a living process rather than a fixed thing.

- Sitting in meditation for even thirty minutes reliably demonstrates that experience does not conform to expectation or belief, making the cushion itself a direct encounter with groundlessness.

- Meditation was described as sitting in front of a mirror, meaning it is fundamentally an opportunity to encounter oneself as one actually is rather than as one wishes to be.

- Framing meditation as an embodied truth practice emphasizes that it is something done with the whole body and psyche, not merely contemplated by the mind.

### Meditative Inquiry as a Method for Accessing Deeper Wisdom

- Inquiry within meditation involves formulating a clear, concise question and dropping it into the quiet domain of the psyche rather than analyzing it consciously.

- Posing the question to the unconscious rather than the conscious mind is essential because the conscious mind, having already failed to resolve the question, cannot supply the answer.

- A rhythm of silence, re-posing the question, and releasing it again into stillness was described as a powerful method for accessing one's own deeper well of wisdom.

- This practice is called meditative rather than analytical inquiry because it deliberately bypasses the top layer of conscious thought to reach the remaining depth of the psyche.

- Questions can be carried for extended periods beyond formal sitting sessions, making inquiry an ongoing attitude of being rather than a cushion-bound technique.

### Translating Insights into Embodied, Human Terms

- Insights gained through meditation, whether small or transformative, require translation into practical, human living terms in order to have genuine impact on daily life.

- Large revelatory experiences such as perceiving oneself as vast emptiness are profound but not inherently human, and must be humanized to become functionally meaningful.

- Emptiness, as one example, translates humanly into openness, curiosity, and a disposition of living in discovery rather than conclusion.

- Asking what the humanized version of an insight looks like in a specific relationship or conversation was presented as a vital continuation of the embodied practice.

- The question of how to take a meditative experience into daily life was identified as a less useful framing because it treats peace or insight as an experience to be transported rather than a ground from which to function.

- Relating to peace as an orientation toward life rather than a feeling to be preserved changes the inquiry from maintenance of a state to embodiment of a way of being.

### Love, Acceptance, and the Shadow Elements of Consciousness

- The aspiration to love all people equally was examined through the tension between idealized unity and the reality of feeling upset, disappointed, or withholding toward specific individuals.

- Orienting from love as a foundational ground rather than policing one's emotional responses was described as producing a more dynamic, alive, and generous experience of connection.

- Parental love was used as an illustration of how genuine love does not require approval of every action but remains constant beneath disagreement, disappointment, or frustration.

- Accepting shadow emotions such as fear, anger, and divisiveness with compassion rather than recoiling from them or indulging them was presented as the more effective spiritual approach.

- Spaciousness around difficult emotions tends to prevent those emotions from driving behavior, while simultaneously reconnecting the person with their underlying care and concern for others.

- Contracting around a contraction, or resisting an unwanted feeling, was identified as a common pattern that meditation practice directly addresses by exercising the capacity to remain present with discomfort.

- Expanding the container of awareness so that it is larger than any particular experience was described as having an intrinsic relationship to love and the ability to stay connected even when disconnection feels tempting.

### The Physical Dimension of Openness and Closing

- A direct correlation was noted between emotional shutdown and physical responses such as shallow breathing, clenching, and folding inward.

- Returning to the breath and physically opening the body, squaring the shoulders and facing outward, was recognized as a somatic pathway back to presence and engagement.

- The Zen image of standing on one's own two feet and growing one's shoulders back was cited as a metaphor for the ultimate affirmation of life that meditation cultivates.

- This affirmation is not a spiritualized claim that everything is perfect but rather an expanding capacity to hold challenge, tragedy, sorrow, love, and connection simultaneously.

### Meditation as an Attitude of Being and a Series of Commitments

- Formal sitting practice was described as a ritualized, intensified version of an attitude that need not be abandoned upon leaving the cushion.

- The commitments made during meditation, including staying present, not willingly indulging analytical narrative, facing experience directly, and acknowledging a deeper ground of stillness, apply equally well in daily life and relationships.

- Getting up from the cushion and unconsciously reverting to habitual reactivity was identified as a common pattern that undermines the transformative potential of practice.

- Meditation understood as an attitude of being and a series of internal commitments has the capacity to transform an entire life rather than functioning as a supplementary hobby.

- Part of a teacher's role was described as helping students recognize that these commitments are not confined to formal practice but extend into every dimension of living.

### Response-Ability, Responsibility, and the Critical Inner Narrative

- A distinction was drawn between being reactive and being response-able, with meditation expanding the gap between stimulus and response that makes conscious choice possible.

- Becoming response-able was linked directly to becoming responsible, as the capacity to choose one's response rather than simply enact a reaction constitutes a deeper form of accountability.

- The internalized critical narrative was identified as a voice that primarily functions to diminish capacity and reinforce fear rather than to support genuine growth.

- Looking into and feeling the fear or pain beneath self-judgment, rather than running from it or indulging it, allows the secondary judgment to dissolve by addressing the primary emotion fueling it.

- Experiencing the primary emotion underneath criticism directly connects the practice of response-ability back to the broader theme of embodied openness and expanded awareness.

### The Role of the Teacher in Engendering Trust and Creative Discovery

- Sensitivity to the specific needs, motivations, and goals of individual students and groups was described as foundational to effective teaching of meditation.

- Different orientations toward practice, such as stress relief versus awakening to one's true nature, require substantially different approaches and should not be conflated.

- Asking students why they came, what they want, and what first drew them to meditation was offered as a practical method for understanding the appropriate teaching orientation.

- A teacher's most important transmission may be an unshakable faith in the student's deepest nature and inherent capacity, communicated not necessarily through words but through attitude.

- Leaving space undefined and resisting the impulse to provide complete, prescriptive answers was described as a way of engendering the student's own creative and improvisational relationship with practice.

- When a teacher trusts a student more than the student trusts themselves, that mirroring of faith can be profoundly catalytic in unlocking capacities the student could not previously imagine.

### Next Steps

- Practitioners and teachers were encouraged to carry meditative inquiry as an ongoing attitude beyond formal sitting sessions, integrating it into daily life and relationships.

- A commitment to translating insights into humanized, practical terms was identified as an essential continuation of embodied practice following any significant meditative experience.

- Teachers were directed to prioritize understanding the specific needs and motivations of their students as a foundation for determining the appropriate meditative approach to offer.

- Cultivating and transmitting an unshakable faith in students' inherent capacities was affirmed as a central ongoing responsibility of the teacher's role.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Meditation as Art vs. Technique**
- Meditation begins as a discipline with a technical aspect aimed at awareness and mindfulness
- Over time it transitions into a creative process that expands beyond technique
- The art of meditation involves letting the practice breathe and become a form of embodied curiosity
- Like a musician who needs discipline as a foundation before improvisation becomes possible, meditators need structure before the creative dimension opens
**Groundlessness**
- Groundlessness described as a collective theme, accentuated by the pandemic and broader political and cultural upheaval
- Orienting principles — ethical, moral, identity-based — have been destabilized for many people
- The more one grasps for something solid during groundlessness, the more fractured and afraid one feels
- Meditation can become the embodied embracing of groundlessness rather than a refuge from it
- Groundlessness itself can become its own kind of refuge when one stops grasping
**Meditation as Embodied Truth Practice**
- Truth introduced not as abstraction or idea, but as an environment and a process
- Meditation is something done with the body, not just the mind
- Sitting in meditation is described as sitting in front of a mirror — an encounter with oneself
- The practice involves bringing the mind back into the body rather than lifting experience up into a mental ideal
**Opening the Field of Awareness**
- Two modes of mindfulness contrasted: narrowing attention to avoid disturbance versus opening the field to embrace all experience
- Opening awareness described as similar to relaxing one's listening to hear all sounds simultaneously
- The openness itself becomes the stillness and sanity, not a constrained focus
- A wider context — awareness, silence, stillness — is recognized as inherent within the challenge
**Meditative Inquiry**
- Inquiry involves posing a clearly defined question, then dropping it into the quiet domain of the psyche
- The question is posed to the unconscious, not the conscious mind, since the conscious mind already lacks the answer
- A rhythm is described: silence, posing the question, letting it go, returning to silence
- This process can be carried for years and does not require formal meditation once the approach is internalized
- Meditative inquiry is not an analytical process; it attempts to access the deeper 95% of the psyche
**Taking Insights Off the Cushion**
- Insights must be translated into humanized, approachable living terms to be meaningful
- Example given: an experience of vast emptiness translates humanly into openness, curiosity, living in a state of discovery rather than conclusion
- The wrong question is "how do I take this experience into my life?" because it treats insight as an experience to maintain
- The better question is what does this insight mean in terms of how I relate, speak, and act
**Love as Orientation vs. Experience**
- Attempting to maintain a feeling of universal love leads to policing one's spirituality and measuring love out
- Orienting from love as a foundational ground makes it dynamic, lush, and alive
- One can feel upset, disappointed, or in disagreement with someone and still be connected to a deeper caring
- Accepting all arising emotions — including divisive ones — with compassion, without acting on them, reconnects one to the greater field of connection
**Shadow Elements and Compassion**
- Spiritually oriented people often recoil from difficult emotions, layering resistance on resistance
- Accepting shadow elements without acting on them allows the space around them to grow
- When there is great space for difficult feelings, they lose their driving force
- The primary emotion underneath judgment — often fear or insecurity — can dissolve the secondary judgment when experienced directly
**Response-ability and Responsibility**
- Meditation opens the capacity to be response-able rather than simply reactive
- Creating a gap in reactive activity allows for chosen responses
- Being response-able leads to being responsible
- The critical inner narrative that judges one's failures serves mainly to diminish capacity and is often rooted in fear
**The Role of the Teacher**
- Teachers must know their audience — individual and group — and understand why people are meditating
- Different orientations require different approaches: stress relief versus awakening are not the same path
- One of the most important things a teacher can transmit is unshakable faith in the student's deepest nature
- The teacher's unspoken trust that the student can find the answers within themselves is described as among the greatest gifts
**Meditation as Attitude and Commitment**
- Meditation defined as a commitment: to stay present, not to run from experience, not to willingly indulge analytical narrative, to face what is present, and to acknowledge the deeper ground of stillness
- None of these commitments need to change when one leaves the cushion
- Meditation is an attitude of being and a series of internal commitments applicable to all of life
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Groundlessness as a Collective Spiritual Condition**
- The pandemic and political upheaval have surfaced a widespread experience of groundlessness
- Orienting beliefs and identity structures that provided security have been destabilized across society
- Rather than a problem to be solved, groundlessness is presented as a threshold — an invitation to find a deeper, non-grasping stability
**Truth as Environment, Not Abstraction**
- Spiritual literature often capitalizes Truth and makes it abstract and distant
- A more useful framing is truth as an environment one inhabits through embodied practice
- This reframing makes spiritual practice immediate, practical, and accessible
**The Limits of Experience-Based Spirituality**
- Seeking to maintain peak spiritual experiences — peace, unity, love — is a common but limiting approach
- Experiences are transient; orientations and attitudes are sustainable
- The shift from "how do I hold onto this experience" to "how does this inform how I live" is a key maturation in practice
**Love, Unity, and the Human Condition**
- The insight that all is one and that love is universal does not eliminate human reactions of upset, disappointment, or disagreement
- Authentic love includes the full range of human emotion, as illustrated by the parent-child relationship
- Compassion for one's own difficult emotions — recognizing the fear and insecurity beneath them — is the path back to genuine connection
**Fear as the Root of Division**
- Much of what drives divisive emotions is fear, insecurity, and a survival mechanism oriented toward self-protection
- Recognizing and compassionately experiencing this fear, rather than indulging or suppressing it, dissolves the secondary judgment built on top of it
**The Societal Need for Expanded Capacity**
- The challenges of the current era require an expanded capacity to experience and respond rather than a retreat into defensiveness
- Each person's response to collective crisis is what registers with the world and with others
- Meditation as a practice that grows the container — the ability to experience more without shutting down — is presented as a direct response to societal need
**Faith in Human Potential**
- Teachers and practitioners are encouraged to hold an unshakable faith in the capacities of others
- People possess capacities they can hardly imagine; the mirroring of trust by another can be transformative
- This is framed as one of the most valuable things a teacher can offer

### Stories
**The Skydiver Metaphor**
- Shared by Adyashanti to illustrate groundlessness and the possibility of relaxing into it
- A person involuntarily pushed from a plane grasps frantically at air; another person nearby is simply floating, relaxed, enjoying the fall
- The image conveys that groundlessness itself can become a refuge when one stops grasping for something solid
**Adyashanti's Mother at a Gathering**
- Shared by Adyashanti, referring to his own mother
- His mother attended one of his gatherings but did not want people to know she was his mother to avoid attention; the word got out
- She was surrounded by young mothers during a break; one asked if she ever got upset with Adyashanti as a child
- She replied casually that he absolutely drove her nuts some days
- The story is used to illustrate that love does not require agreement or approval of all behavior — one can feel frustrated and still love deeply
**Adyashanti's Teacher and the Undefined Space**
- Shared by Adyashanti, referring to his own teacher
- His teacher rarely gave him direct answers about meditation, always leaving a large undefined area
- He initially disliked this and wanted precise instructions
- In retrospect he recognized she was engendering in him the understanding that meditation is an art requiring creative, improvisational engagement
- Her approach transmitted an unspoken faith that he could find the answers within himself
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Christy Peoples reported difficulty sitting still during the period of the conversation, which took place during the coronavirus pandemic
- She described a sense of disruption from external events — news, the pandemic, collective upheaval — impacting her ability to settle into practice
- She described feeling groundless and unable to establish consistency or grounding in her meditation practice
- She noted that meditation had previously been a haven and open space for her but felt different during this period
- She described a collective experience of disruption shared among peers and friends, not only a personal one
- She described a physical response to shutting down: shallow breathing from the chest, physical clenching and closing
- She noted that the invitation to expand brought her back to her breath and allowed her to square her shoulders and face outward rather than fold inward
- Adyashanti acknowledged that the pandemic accentuated a pre-existing sense of insecurity and groundlessness already present in the broader environment
