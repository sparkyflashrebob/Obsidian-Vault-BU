### Outline
### Introduction to the Mindfulness Meditation Teacher Certification Program

- Designed as a special podcast series, the session draws from the best wisdom teachings of previous years of the teacher training program.

- Listening without watching a screen is presented as an effective way to open, receive, and learn the timeless wisdom being shared.

- Mentors have consistently reported that trainees are receiving an overwhelming volume of material, described as a "fire hose effect."

- Rather than introducing new content, the session is intentionally focused on the most foundational reminders to help trainees assimilate the material already covered.

- Tara Brach focuses on reminders related to the inner life, while Jack Kornfield explores how those reminders affect the way we are with others.

### The Four Core Reminders Overview

- Four foundational reminders are introduced as the framework for the session, each serving as a recurring message to students of mindfulness.

- Pausing and arriving is identified as the first reminder, emphasizing the importance of stopping and becoming present.

- Saying yes to what is arising is the second reminder, pointing to the practice of noticing and allowing experience without judgment.

- Turning towards love is the third reminder, highlighting the role of kindness and care in genuine transformation.

- Resting in awareness is the fourth reminder, inviting a shift from effortful doing to simply being present in open, alert stillness.

### Reminder One - Pausing and Arriving in the Present Moment

- Scientific research indicates that the human mind wanders approximately 47% of the time, keeping people caught in a virtual reality rather than embodied presence.

- American adults spend more than 11 hours per day interacting with media, up from nine hours and 32 minutes just four years prior, illustrating how removed people have become from direct experience.

- One of the greatest gifts mindfulness teaching offers is the invitation to pause and notice the difference between virtual reality and genuine presence.

- Pausing is connected to slowing down, as much of daily life is spent speeding toward the next thing under the background assumption that there is never enough time.

- The concept of "induced meandering," drawn from rainwater harvesting, is offered as a metaphor for how slowing down allows life to sink in, nourishing aliveness, heart, and spirit.

- A mantra shared by a terminally ill woman during the first year of her baby's life, "I have no time to rush," is presented as a powerful reminder of the first foundational practice.

### Reminder Two - Saying Yes to What Is Here

- Saying yes to what is arising is described as intrinsic to mindfulness, involving recognition and allowance of experience without judgment, pushing away, or reacting.

- Students are taught that they do not have to like what is happening, but are encouraged to listen and pay attention with an allowing quality, cultivating fearless receptivity.

- A common pattern among students is the belief that pain or difficulty is getting in the way of meditation, which reflects the deeper habit of saying no to experience.

- Whatever is practiced grows stronger, meaning the habit of resistance leads to increasing tightness and inner conflict, while the habit of saying yes leads to greater spaciousness.

- Experimenting with a personal "Yes Meditation" during a retreat revealed that mechanical repetition of yes gradually became playful, then spacious, then deeply witnessing, and ultimately filled with tenderness.

- A phrase attributed to a Japanese teacher named Sono, "Thank you for everything, I have no complaints whatsoever," is offered as a practical tool for cultivating the spirit of saying yes.

- Different expressions of yes are suggested for teaching, including mentally whispering yes, using the phrase "this belongs," or energetically opening the body and heart to what is present.

### Reminder Three - Turning Towards Love

- All real transformation is described as requiring kindness, making the intentional turn toward love an essential element of practice.

- Remembering or turning toward care has been shown to reduce limbic activity and unpleasant emotions while activating higher centers of the brain.

- Research supports the idea that remembering love reactivates learning, reinforcing the belief that people love themselves and each other into healing.

- Even the slightest gesture of kindness, such as a simple internal reminder to "be kind," can produce an immediate softening of experience.

- Turning toward love can take many forms including messages of acceptance, forgiveness, and compassion, and teachers are encouraged to begin with whatever resonates most for their students.

- A story about researcher Shauna Shapiro illustrates how a teacher wisely downgraded the practice from "I love you, Shauna" to simply placing a hand on the heart and saying "good morning, Shauna," making it accessible enough to sustain.

- Through consistent practice, Shapiro eventually felt genuine love when repeating the phrase, experiencing her grandmother's love, her mother's love, and her own love, demonstrating that a pathway of kind attention can be established over time.

### Reminder Four - Resting in Awareness

- The ocean and waves metaphor is used to illustrate the difference between identifying with the temporary, changing aspects of experience and recognizing the deeper, unchanging awareness that underlies them.

- Most people live under the illusion that they are a set of waves trying to reach their ocean-ness, which creates a forward-leaning, effortful orientation that generates more waves rather than stillness.

- Teaching non-doing is described as particularly challenging because it runs counter to survival strategies and ego strategies, with most people fearing that stopping will lead to stagnation or regression.

- Resting in awareness begins with the invitation to let life be just as it is in the present moment, allowing attention to shift from changing waves to the background of open, knowing, tender presence.

- When resting in ocean awareness, one becomes truly able to cherish the waves, reflecting Rumi's teaching that one is not a drop in the ocean but the entire ocean in a temporary, changing drop.

### Guided Reflection on the Four Reminders

- A brief guided reflection is offered to allow trainees to directly experience the four reminders in sequence rather than only understanding them conceptually.

- Participants are invited to pause without needing to change posture or accomplish anything, simply arriving with the sense that there is no time to rush.

- Saying yes is practiced by gently whispering yes to whatever is present, whether vulnerability, tension, peace, or excitement, with the recognition that it all belongs.

- Kindness is introduced as a deepening gesture, particularly when discomfort or imbalance is present, with the option to place a hand on the heart to deepen intimacy with the moment.

- Resting in awareness is invited by letting everything be just as it is and sensing the alert inner stillness, tender presence, and wakefulness in the background of experience.

- A poem by Dana Falls closes the reflection, describing how a single breath and moment of stillness allows something in us to settle, soften, and remember that life is not a relay race but an ongoing invitation to wake up.

### The Outer Dimension of Mindfulness - Presence with Others

- The word mindfulness in Sanskrit and Pali is often paired as "Sati Sampajanna," meaning it has two dimensions, mindful presence and mindful response, like breathing in and out.

- Stories are emphasized as a particularly powerful pedagogical tool because people remember and embody them in a way that abstract instruction does not achieve.

- Author Barry Lopez is quoted as writing that sometimes a person needs a story more than food, reinforcing the value of narrative in teaching.

### The Aikido Story - Love as the Essence of Conflict Resolution

- A story from martial artist Terry Dobson set on a Tokyo commuter train illustrates the difference between responding to conflict with force versus responding with compassionate presence.

- A large, drunk, and violent laborer enters the train car and begins assaulting passengers, creating a situation in which Dobson, trained in Aikido, prepares to intervene physically.

- Just before a confrontation erupts, a small elderly Japanese man in a kimono calls out to the laborer with a joyful, lilting tone, redirecting the situation entirely.

- Without any trace of fear or resentment, the old man engages the laborer in warm conversation about sake and his persimmon tree, gradually softening the man's rage.

- As the conversation continues, the laborer reveals that his wife has died, he has no home, no job, and no money, and he breaks down weeping with shame and despair.

- Dobson reflects that standing in his self-righteous readiness to fight, he suddenly felt dirtier than the laborer, recognizing that what he had intended to accomplish with muscle and meanness had been achieved with a few kind words.

- The story is presented as a direct illustration of Tara Brach's four reminders applied outwardly, showing how pausing, saying yes, turning toward love, and resting in awareness can transform conflict between people.

### Seeing with the Eyes of Loving Kindness

- Teaching people to pause in conflict or difficulty allows them to let go of judgment and begin to see the vulnerability beneath another person's behavior.

- Loving kindness does not require approving of harmful actions but involves recognizing that underneath those actions lies a measure of pain, struggle, and burden.

- A story from a seminar led by Frank Ostaseski in Germany illustrates the power of bearing witness, in which a woman whose father survived a concentration camp and a woman whose father was a Nazi guard found each other across a room of 200 people and wept together without words.

- Their silent embrace demonstrated the recognition that no one is alone in their pain and that the mysteries of life, its joys and sorrows, are shared among all people.

- When students are taught to pause and see with the heart, they begin to notice gestures of goodness everywhere, as described by Fred Rogers who encouraged people to widen their lens during disasters and look for the helpers.

- A poem by Laszlo Slomovits describes a man who stops a bus not for himself but to help an elderly woman board, prompting a crowd of strangers to cheer, illustrating the fundamental goodness present in ordinary human moments.

- Tuning into common humanity and the shared nature of difficulty and beauty allows students to move through the world with a wise heart, a caring orientation, and a deeper sense of freedom and well-being.

### The Dalai Lama and the Shared Difficulty of Sitting Still

- Dr. Sanjay Gupta's account of meditating with the Dalai Lama is shared to illustrate that even the most experienced practitioners find the practice difficult.

- When the Dalai Lama acknowledged that meditation is hard for him too, it initially seemed surprising given his depth of training and experience.

- Reflection on this reveals that when the Dalai Lama sits still, he holds in his heart the Tibetan people in exile, the destruction of temples, the oppression of monks and nuns, and the suffering of people in conflicts around the world.

- This understanding reframes the difficulty of sitting not as a personal failing but as a natural consequence of the fact that when we sit, we are not sitting alone but with the whole world.

- Teachers are encouraged to remind students of this shared humanity, helping them see that the challenges they face in practice are not obstacles but expressions of their deep connection to others.

### The Buddha's Teaching on the Possibility of Awakening

- A teaching attributed to the Buddha is offered as a closing reminder that the release of entanglement, fear, greed, judgment, hatred, and ignorance is genuinely possible for every person.

- The awakening of the great heart of compassion and freedom is described not as an ideal but as an actual possibility, which is the very reason these teachings are offered.

- Trainees are framed as carriers of a lineage of compassion, wakefulness, and inner freedom, whose role is to remind others of what is possible and to provide the tools to realize it.

### Next Steps

- Trainees are encouraged to integrate the four foundational reminders, pausing, saying yes, turning toward love, and resting in awareness, as the core framework for their teaching practice.

- Storytelling is identified as a key pedagogical approach to be developed and drawn upon regularly in teaching contexts.

- Applying the inner reminders outwardly, by teaching students to pause in conflict, see with loving kindness, and recognize common humanity, is established as a central direction for the teaching work ahead.

- The two-year program is acknowledged as an ongoing, deepening journey that is both challenging and meaningful, with the shared commitment of the community serving as a source of support and inspiration.

### Stories
**The Cherry Blossom Tree — Tara Brach**
Tara shared a personal story from her junior year of college. After a yoga class, walking home in springtime, an inner voice told her to stop by a cherry blossom tree. She smelled the fragrance, felt a gentle breeze, and noticed her mind was quiet. Her body and mind were in the same place at the same time, and life felt sacred. She uses this as an illustration of the gift of pausing.
**The Yes Meditation — Tara Brach**
At a retreat, Tara was experiencing what she called multiple hindrance attacks — pervasive negativity and judgment. She assigned herself the task of saying yes to whatever arose. It began mechanically, became playful, then spacious, then witnessing, and finally deepened into tenderness. She shares this as a personal illustration of the second reminder.
**Teaching Her Son — Tara Brach**
Tara's son, around age eight or nine, was a habitual naysayer. She taught him the phrase from Japanese teacher Sono: "Thank you for everything. I have no complaints whatsoever." She recounts driving him to the dentist, cursing at Washington D.C. Beltway traffic, and him nudging her hard with the reminder.
**Shauna Shapiro and the Morning Greeting — Tara Brach, referring to Shauna Shapiro**
Shauna Shapiro, described as a fellow teacher and friend of Jack and Tara, was going through a difficult divorce and waking each morning with a pit of shame. Her meditation teacher suggested saying "I love you, Shauna" each morning. Shauna refused. The teacher downgraded it to placing a hand on the heart and saying "Good morning, Shauna." After months of practice, the teacher introduced the more advanced version: "Good morning, I love you, Shauna." One morning Shauna felt it fully — her grandmother's love, her mother's love, and her own love.
**The Drunk on the Tokyo Train — Jack Kornfield, referring to Terry Dobson**
Terry Dobson, described as one of the most celebrated Western martial artists, shared this story from his training in Japan. On a suburban Tokyo train, a large, drunk, violent laborer boarded and began attacking passengers. Dobson, trained in Aikido and eager to be a hero, stood to confront him. Before he could act, a small elderly Japanese man in a kimono called out joyfully to the drunk, asked what he had been drinking, and began warmly sharing a story about drinking sake with his wife in their garden and watching their persimmon tree. The drunk softened, revealed he had no wife, no home, no job, no money, and began to sob with shame. By the time Dobson's stop arrived, the laborer was lying with his head in the old man's lap, being stroked gently. Dobson realized that what he had wanted to accomplish with force had been done with a few kind words, and that the essence of Aikido was love.
**The Two Women at the Forgiveness Seminar — Jack Kornfield, referring to Frank Gostosowski**
Frank Gostosowski was teaching a seminar in Germany on trauma and forgiveness. A woman stood up and said her father had been a prisoner in a concentration camp and her heart was like ice — she could not forgive. Then a woman on the other side of the room stood up trembling and said her heart felt like stone — her father had been a Nazi officer and guard in the camp who had killed people, and she could not forgive him. The two women made their way across the large conference hall of 200 people, did not speak, and simply held each other and wept. Jack offers this as an illustration of recognizing shared pain across profound division.
**Dr. Sanjay Gupta and the Dalai Lama — Jack Kornfield**
Dr. Sanjay Gupta, CNN's public physician, was given permission to meditate with the Dalai Lama in Dharamsala. He entered the room early in the morning, paid his respects, and sat beside the Dalai Lama in silence. After some time, the Dalai Lama turned to him and asked, "Any questions?" Gupta said, "Yes, this is hard." The Dalai Lama smiled and replied, "This is hard for me too." Jack reflected that the Dalai Lama holds in his heart the Tibetan people in exile, the destruction of temples, the oppression of monks and nuns, and global conflicts — and that sitting still means sitting with the whole world.
**The Bus Stop Poem — Jack Kornfield, referring to Laszlo Slomowicz**
Jack shared a poem by Laszlo Slomowicz describing a man running hard to catch a bus that has just left. A woman yells to the driver who stops and opens the door. But the man does not board — he points back to an elderly woman who has not run in a long time, shuffling slowly toward the bus. He does not leave until he has helped her up both steps, then walks back slowly, still breathing hard. The bystanders waiting for a different bus all cheered, compared to the moment Jesse Owens entered the Olympic Stadium.
