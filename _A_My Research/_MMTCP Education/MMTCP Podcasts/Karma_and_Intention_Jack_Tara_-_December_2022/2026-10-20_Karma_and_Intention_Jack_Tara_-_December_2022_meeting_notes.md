### Outline
### Introduction to the Podcast Series for Mindfulness Teacher Training

- Created as a special podcast series, the recordings compile the best wisdom teachings from previous years of teacher training to support students in their development.

- Listening without watching a screen is presented as an effective way to open, receive, and learn the material being shared.

- Timeless wisdom is the intended quality of the content, meaning references to specific time periods should not detract from the universal truths being communicated.

### Karma as a Universal Law of Cause and Effect

- Karma is introduced as the law of cause and effect, a concept found across virtually every spiritual tradition and scientific worldview.

- Cosmology, defined as the belief systems people hold about the cosmos, is presented as relevant regardless of whether one comes from a Christian, Jewish, Buddhist, Muslim, atheist, or scientific background.

- The concept is framed not as a doctrine requiring belief, but as a description of how human life unfolds that can be observed directly in everyday experience.

- Ajahn Chah, Jack Kornfield's teacher, is cited as having taught that karma is available to be understood in every moment and every day, without requiring belief in past or future lives.

- A parable of two travelers arriving at a new city illustrates how the spirit and attitude one brings to the world determines what one encounters and receives in return.

- Ruth Dennison's simple formulation is offered as a distillation of the concept, stating that karma means you do not get away with nothing and that how one acts returns to them in some fashion.

- Karma is described as both a personal and a collective law, meaning that the seeds planted by individuals and societies alike will determine the nature of what grows from them.

### The Role of Intention as the Key to Understanding Karma

- Intention is identified as the central factor in understanding karma, making it directly relevant to mindfulness and compassion teaching practice.

- An illustrative example contrasts a man who deliberately crashes his car into a neighbor's house out of rage with one whose pedal sticks accidentally, showing that identical actions carry entirely different karmic weight based on intention.

- Neuroscience is cited to support the idea that human brains are wired to read the intentions of others, with the nervous system tuned to predict behavior as a survival mechanism.

- St. Vincent de Paul is quoted to convey that it is only by feeling the love behind a gift that the recipient can truly receive it, highlighting how intention is felt as much as action.

- Two dimensions of intention are distinguished, namely long-term intention that sets the overall direction of one's heart and life, and short-term intention that governs moment-to-moment responses.

### Long-Term Intention and the Direction of the Heart

- Long-term intention is characterized by the vows and commitments through which a person orients the direction of their life.

- The Bodhisattva vows from the Buddhist tradition, as expressed by the poet Shantideva, are offered as an example of a morning dedication to being of service as a bridge, medicine, food, lamp, and resting place for all beings.

- Gandhi's practice of contemplating the face of the poorest person he had met before acting is presented as a powerful example of long-term intention guiding an entire life.

- The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo in Argentina are described as a historical example of long-term collective intention, where mothers of disappeared children marched every Thursday for years despite fear, police intimidation, and infiltration of their group.

- Their persistence is noted as having spread internationally, with Argentine writer Cortázar declaring that once the mothers were out, the military had already lost, illustrating the far-reaching power of sustained intention.

- A key quality of long-term intention is that one does not necessarily see the fruits of that intention, yet it continues to guide one's direction through all circumstances.

### Intention in Meditation Practice

- Setting an intention at the beginning of a meditation session is presented as a simple and powerful practice that gives greater depth and stability to the sitting.

- A personal example is shared of resolving to remain seated through three waves of restlessness, demonstrating how a clear inner intention allowed for progressively deeper stillness.

- Dream yoga is introduced as an advanced meditation practice in which a deep intention to remain aware during sleep is cultivated over time, now validated by lucid dreaming research originating at the Stanford Sleep Lab.

- Inviting an intention for insight or wisdom before entering stillness is described as a way of turning consciousness toward understanding, particularly when facing a difficulty or problem.

### Planting and Watering Seeds as a Teaching Metaphor for Karma

- The metaphor of planting seeds is offered as a beautiful and accessible language for teaching karma and intention, since an avocado seed produces an avocado tree and not a sunflower.

- Henry David Thoreau is quoted expressing great faith in a seed, suggesting that convincing someone a seed is present is enough to expect wonders, which parallels the power of planting intentional seeds in consciousness.

- Students can be invited to ask not only what seeds they are planting but also what seeds they are watering, since all kinds of seeds exist in the heart and the ones that receive attention are the ones that grow.

- Alan Chadwick's biodynamic gardening philosophy is cited as an analogy, describing how he transformed a junkyard lot filled with rusted cars and broken glass into a garden famous for extraordinary vegetables and flowers by cultivating the soil itself.

### Short-Term Intention and the Mindful Pause

- Short-term intention is described as the practice of pausing before responding when triggered, taking a breath, and asking what one's highest goal or best response is in that moment.

- Checking in with one's best intention in a conflict or difficult communication is shown to shift the energy from defending, being right, or putting someone down toward connecting, learning, or working things out.

- Pema Chödrön's story of receiving essential teachings as a child from an old woman who told her not to let life harden her heart is offered as a lifetime teaching about the practice of short-term intention.

- A scene from Kurosawa's film about the healer Redbeard is described, in which the physician responds to a traumatized, mute young woman's repeated rejection of medicine with calm, patient, and loving persistence until she finally accepts it.

- This scene is presented as a demonstration of what it means to respond to the world with a different kind of intention, and as a model for the capacity that teachers can develop through meditation training.

- Teachers are encouraged to practice the mindful pause themselves and to train students in it, since people will come with anger, fear, confusion, and longing, and the teacher's role is to meet them with loving awareness and ask what their highest intention is.

### Practical Guidance for Teaching Intention to Students

- Tara Brach observes that many teachers already invite students to set an intention at the start of a class, and notes that students sometimes discover they had no idea what their intention was until asked.

- D.H. Lawrence's distinction between what the self wants and what the deepest self wants is cited to illustrate that finding true intention requires diving beneath the surface.

- Three qualities of a liberating aspiration are identified, the first being that it relates to manifesting one's innate potential rather than achieving external goals.

- The second quality is that intention must be embodied and felt as a live energy in the heart rather than retrieved as a prefabricated mental idea.

- Oprah Winfrey is quoted to reinforce this point, stating that when something is right, the entire body will feel it.

- The third quality is that intention can be experienced in the present moment rather than deferred, contrasting with St. Augustine's famous prayer for chastity but not yet.

- A poem by Denise Levertov is shared, in which the speaker reflects on how the mind darts away like a minnow rather than resting in presence, pointing to the ongoing practice of returning to deep intention.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to continue or begin the practice of inviting students to set intentions at the start of each class session.

- Exploring the practicalities of guiding students into embodied, present-moment intention is identified as an ongoing area of development for teaching practice.

- Cultivating the mindful pause as both a personal practice and a teachable skill is affirmed as a priority for those in training.

- Further work on long-term intention, including the use of vows and directional commitments, is indicated as a topic to be developed in subsequent sessions.

### Stories
**Jack Kornfield's Father in the ICU**
Jack Kornfield shares a personal story of sitting with his father, a biophysicist and designer of artificial hearts, who was dying of congestive heart failure in the ICU at the University of Pennsylvania. His father, a scientist, believed death meant simply returning to dirt. Jack described his own out-of-body experiences in meditation, near-death accounts from accident survivors, conversations with comatose patients who later recalled what was said, and past life memories from meditation retreats. He encouraged his father to keep an open mind and, if he found himself drifting out of his body toward light, to let go into it.
**The Alzheimer's Patient's Final Words — from Kitchen Table Wisdom by Rachel Remen**
Jack Kornfield recounts a story from the book Kitchen Table Wisdom by Rachel Remen, a physician at Stanford Medical School. A man with severe Alzheimer's had not spoken for years. When he suffered a heart attack, his son — a physician — was about to call 911. The man opened his eyes, said "Don't call 911. Tell your mother I love you. I'm about to die," and died. His son noted that the Alzheimer's appeared to have destroyed the neurological capacity for speech, yet a cogent message emerged after seven years of silence.
**The Two Travelers and the Wise Woman**
Jack Kornfield shares a parable. A traveler approaching a new city asks an old wise woman what the people there are like. She asks how he found the people in the city he left. He says they were gossips, mean-spirited, and selfish. She tells him he will find the people of this city to be likewise. A second traveler asks the same question. He says the people in his home city were fine, industrious, helpful, and open-minded. She tells him he will find this city to be likewise. The parable illustrates that the spirit we bring to the world determines what returns to us.
**The Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo — Argentina**
Jack Kornfield recounts the story of the Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo during Argentina's dirty war. The military was kidnapping people and children deemed threats to their rule. Mothers seeking their lost children were turned away. A group of fourteen mothers began marching in slow circles in the plaza when police told them they could not stand still. They continued to march every Thursday, braving nightsticks, police dogs, and military spies who infiltrated and killed several of their leaders. They never found their children but described the plaza as their educational academy and what saved them from madness. Their white head scarves became an international symbol of the fight for human rights. Argentine writer Cortázar, upon hearing of them in Paris, declared the military had already lost.
**Jack Kornfield's Meditation Intention Practice**
Jack Kornfield shares a personal story from his early years of meditation when he felt restless and frustrated. He set an intention to only get up after feeling the urge three times. Each time the wave of restlessness arose, he noted it mindfully and let it pass, going deeper and stiller each time. He describes this as an example of how a simple inward intention gives greater power to meditation practice.
**The Redbeard Physician — from Kurosawa's film**
Jack Kornfield describes a scene from Akira Kurosawa's film featuring a healer named Redbeard. A young woman who had been severely abused was brought to him, mute and in shock. His apprentice had failed to help her. The master physician sat quietly and repeatedly offered her medicine on a spoon. Each time she smashed it away and hit his face, he sat quietly, poured another spoon, and moved it slowly to her mouth. After approximately six attempts, she opened her mouth and took the medicine. In the next scene she spoke for the first time in years, asking the apprentice: "Why didn't he hit me?" Jack connects this to the capacity a teacher develops through meditation training to respond to pain and anger with patient, loving intention.
**Pema Chodron's Childhood Teaching — from The Places That Scare You**
Jack Kornfield recounts the opening story from Pema Chodron's book The Places That Scare You. When Pema was about six years old, she was walking past an old woman's house feeling lonely, unloved, and angry, kicking anything she could find. The old woman, laughing, called out: "Little girl, don't you go letting life harden your heart." Kornfield presents this as the essential teaching of the awakened heart and connects it to the practice of short-term intention.
**Alan Chadwick and the Junkyard Garden**
Jack Kornfield shares the story of Alan Chadwick, described as one of the fathers of biodynamic gardening and a faculty member at UC Santa Cruz. Chadwick gardened by growing soil, understanding soil as a power in the universe that could be concentrated to capture cosmic magic. He took a class to a junkyard lot filled with rusted cars, broken glass, cement, and trash, and asked the owner for permission to grow flowers and vegetables there. The owner agreed, saying the soil was dead. The garden later became famous for its extraordinarily delicious vegetables and gorgeous flowers. This story is offered as a metaphor for what can be cultivated in human consciousness.
**Tara Brach's Story of the Fortune Teller**
Tara Brach shares a brief humorous story. A woman whose husband had passed away wanted to contact him after his death and went to a fortune teller. After incantations, the fortune teller said she was in contact with the departed husband, who reportedly said he couldn't believe she paid $45 for this. Tara uses this to illustrate how intentions can get waylaid by surface-level or misdirected goals.
