### Outline
### Welcome and Introduction to the Session

- Participants are welcomed as future teachers of mindfulness meditation, with Tara Brach and Jack Kornfield introducing a special podcast series drawn from previous years of teacher training.

- Listening without a screen is presented as a valuable way to receive and internalize wisdom teachings, with the content described as timeless despite any contextual references.

- The session is framed around the theme of releasing aversive judgment and blame, described as a primary domain of suffering that affects personal relationships, workplaces, and society at large.

- A genuine namaste is offered as an opening gesture, communicating safety and openness, and is connected to the concept of basic goodness shared among all participants.

### The Pervasiveness and Impact of Blame in Human Relationships

- Blame is identified as one of the most corrosive forces in intimate relationships, with chronic resentment toward a partner described as creating a wall that prevents tenderness and spontaneity.

- The broader societal dimension of blame is highlighted, with the healing of the planet and the pursuit of peace framed as requiring a collective disarming of the heart and a letting go of othering.

- A reading from Ram Dass is shared, using the metaphor of trees to illustrate how humans naturally allow and appreciate differences in nature but lose that acceptance when relating to other people.

- Practicing "turning people into trees" is offered as a way of appreciating others just as they are, conditioned by various forces and causes, without emotional reactivity.

### The Evolutionary and Psychological Origins of Blame

- Blame is explained as an expression of the fight response within the fight-flight-freeze stress response, described as biologically wired into every human being as a reaction to perceived threat.

- The separate self is described as carrying a primal mood of fear and vulnerability, which triggers an orienting response that seeks the source of potential trouble and assigns wrongness either to others or to oneself.

- Negativity bias is identified as a contributing factor, as the tendency to scan for what is wrong increases the likelihood of feeling endangered and defaulting to blame.

- On a societal level, blame is directed most strongly toward those who are most different, including people of different political views, race, religion, or moral frameworks, as these differences are perceived as threats to safety.

- Research on brainwave patterns is cited to illustrate that similarity predicts friendship, while difference predicts perceived threat and blame.

- On an individual level, the "bad other" is defined as whoever is not meeting one's basic needs for safety, gratification, and connection.

### Distinguishing Healthy Anger from the Trance of Blame

- Anger is described as a form of intelligence that alerts and mobilizes individuals to deal with obstacles, and is presented as a necessary and valid response to being wounded, abused, or oppressed.

- For those who have experienced trauma, anger and blame are identified as necessary tools to mobilize out of a freeze response, and premature forgiveness is cautioned against as it can prevent proper attention to wounds.

- Blame becomes unhealthy when the "on button jams" and stories of wrongdoing continue to fuel anger without resolution, leading to unwise reactivity such as withdrawal or lashing out.

- Prolonged blame is described as hardening into armoring around the heart, functioning like a scab that prevents the light and warmth of awareness from facilitating healing.

- Blame is acknowledged as a temporary fix that provides a sense of superiority and power, which explains why it can feel good and empowering in the short term.

### The Concept of Unreal Others and Unreal Selves in the Trance of Blame

- The central element of the trance of blame is described as a narrowing of perception in which the other person is seen only through their wrongness and becomes unreal.

- In moments of blame, the self also becomes unreal, disconnected from its own wholeness and trapped in a limited story of victimhood.

- A guided reflection is introduced to help students recognize how blame distorts their perception of both the other person and themselves.

- Participants are invited to recall a recent conflict, identify the peak moment of tension, and notice what they were most focused on, including the other person's facial expressions, tone of voice, and perceived disrespect.

- The reflection then invites a shift in perspective by imagining the challenges the other person faces, including their own hurt, stress, and anxiety, and recalling the qualities that make them valued.

- Attention is then turned inward to examine how one looks and sounds when caught in blame, how the body and heart feel, and whether one inhabits the role of angry victim, self-righteous judge, or threatening aggressor.

- The reflection closes with the question of whether one likes oneself in that state, and what is being forgotten about one's own goodness and values in those moments.

### Recognizing the Signs of Being in a Trance of Blame

- Being "blame ready" is described as a state in which one is easily triggered by minor stimuli such as tone of voice, an offhand comment, or being kept waiting, with reactions that are disproportionate to what is actually occurring.

- Habitually assuming that others are judging or taking advantage of oneself is identified as a hallmark of the blame trance.

- Feeling blame or upset toward multiple people simultaneously, and then recognizing oneself as the common denominator, is offered as a signal that one is operating from within the trance.

### What We Protect Through Blame and Why We Hold On to It

- A key inquiry for teachers is presented as exploring what vulnerable feelings are being protected underneath the armoring of blame.

- A quote from James Baldwin is shared, suggesting that people cling to hate and prejudice because releasing it would force them to confront their own pain.

- A guided reflection is described in which students are asked what unpleasant feelings they would have to face if they stopped seeing the other person as bad or wrong.

- Common vulnerable feelings identified beneath the armoring of blame include powerlessness, deficiency, fear of continued harm, grief, feeling unlovable, and the sense that if the other is not wrong then the self must be.

- Blame is summarized as an armoring that temporarily buffers vulnerability, inflates the sense of self, and provides feelings of superiority, righteousness, and power.

### Misunderstandings About Forgiveness That Sustain Blame

- A common belief is identified in which releasing blame is seen as sacrificing justice or removing the ability to hold others accountable for harmful behavior.

- Teachers are encouraged to help students distinguish between blame as an aversive accusation and wise discrimination that recognizes harm without adding the quality of badness to the other person.

- Another misunderstanding is that forgiving condones harmful behavior, and it is clarified that forgiveness does not mean saying what was done is acceptable or allowing harm to continue.

- Examples are offered to illustrate that one can forgive a friend for betraying confidence while establishing boundaries around privacy, or forgive an abusive ex-partner while choosing never to be alone with them again.

- A further misunderstanding is that releasing blame leads to passivity or inaction, and it is clarified that one can forgive those responsible for harm while still actively supporting movements, leaders, and activities that reflect one's values.

- Seeking justice and reparation for oppressed people is presented as fully compatible with releasing hatred and blame toward those perpetuating the oppression.

### The Three Steps Toward Forgiveness

- Three steps toward forgiveness are introduced as a practical framework for guiding students, beginning with awakening the intention to forgive, followed by making the U-turn inward, and concluding with widening perception to see the other more truly.

- Forgiveness is described as something that cannot be willed by the ego, and self-judgment about one's anger or lack of forgiveness is identified as making the process more difficult rather than easier.

- The distinction is drawn between being able to will forgiveness and being willing to forgive, with the latter described as an accessible and meaningful starting point.

- A guided reflection is offered to nourish the intention to forgive, inviting participants to sense how blame makes them small and rigid, and to imagine the heart space of their future or most evolved self that is capable of including the person they are blaming.

- Participants are invited to mentally whisper an intention to forgive the person by name, and to trust that if the intention is present, the door to a forgiving heart is already opening.

- The second step, the U-turn, involves disengaging from blaming thoughts and bringing full attention inward to the vulnerable experience beneath the blame, using the two wings of mindfulness and compassion.

- The third step involves widening perception from a more awake state of being, allowing one to see the other person's unmet needs, suffering, and the trap their leg is caught in, and to respond from wisdom rather than reactivity.

### Case Study Illustrating the Three Steps in Practice

- A man working as an editor whose boss was brilliant but a poor manager and highly critical is described as having his sense of wellbeing entirely dependent on how his boss treated him, leaving him feeling victimized, self-doubting, and grim.

- His intention, clarified through meditation, was to free himself from the small, victimized, blaming self rather than to change his boss's behavior.

- Through the U-turn, he identified that the core wound was feeling disrespected, which connected to earlier experiences of not being valued by his father.

- His practice involved nurturing compassion inwardly, honoring his own care, confidence, and wholeheartedness, which helped him become less reactive to his boss's behavior.

- Seeing his boss more clearly, he recognized the stress she was under, including fear of failure in a competitive industry, and began to wish her more ease.

- He was able to speak with her without blame, acknowledging her high standards while communicating what he needed to do his best work, including boundaries around overtime and project interruptions.

- While his boss's behavior did not fully change, he found increasing freedom from the victim role, greater humor, and more creativity in his daily experience.

- The concept of being 100 percent responsible is introduced, not as condoning harmful behavior, but as recognizing that the only place of real freedom is in taking responsibility for one's internal reactions.

- The principle is stated that there is no healing when acting from blame, and that vengeance is described as a lazy form of grief that buries the wound most in need of attention.

### Releasing Blame as the Foundation for Healing the World

- The ongoing cycle of war and violence is described as rooted in the cycling of blame and the creation of unreal others, and healing is identified as coming not from blame but from the intention to establish understanding and connection.

- Growing movements of spiritually based activism are highlighted as examples of this approach, including the Buddhist Peace Fellowship, the Poor People's Campaign, Love Army, and Revolutionary Love.

- Taking responsibility for changing the causes and conditions one wishes to see transformed is presented as an alternative to blaming others for the state of the world.

- The story of Valerie Carr, a Sikh activist whose uncle was killed in a hate crime after 9-11, is shared as an example of activism rooted in love rather than blame.

- Her response to the killing was to love more inclusively, choosing to wonder about those who caused harm rather than treating them as enemies, expressed in the phrase "you are the part of me I don't yet know."

- Her U-turn involved spending years bringing loving presence to her own fear and hurt, refusing to let it harden into anger.

- She and the murdered man's brother eventually called the killer, who expressed remorse, and the brother offered forgiveness, which Valerie described as an embodiment of the teaching that in the face of suffering the intention is to love more.

- A story from a grief and forgiveness workshop in Berlin is shared in which a daughter of a concentration camp prisoner and a daughter of a Nazi officer both described their hearts as like ice, and the two women crossed the room and embraced without words.

- This moment is described as illustrating that honest acknowledgment of pain, even without full release of armoring, creates connection across profound difference and makes further opening possible.

- Forgiveness is described in Buddhist teaching as what clears the way for love, and the motivation for doing the difficult work of releasing blame is identified as the desire to inhabit one's true nature and love without holding back.

### Closing Guided Meditation on the Three Steps

- A final guided meditation is offered to allow participants to explore the three steps of forgiveness for themselves and to inform their future work guiding students.

- Participants are invited to bring to mind a situation of moderate blame, not a traumatic injury, but a place where some disarming feels possible and worth exploring.

- The meditation moves through sensing the intention to forgive from the perspective of one's wisest and most loving self, getting honest about what triggered the blame, making the U-turn to identify the vulnerable experience underneath, and placing a hand on the heart as a gesture of compassionate presence.

- Participants are guided to offer whatever comfort, nurturing, or love is needed to the vulnerable place inside, and then to gently open attention to the other person from a more evolved and open-hearted perspective.

- The meditation closes with a shared prayer that all may awaken from the trance of blame, see each other truly in their vulnerability and goodness, feel their connectedness, and offer namaste to all life.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to integrate the three-step framework of intention, U-turn, and widening perception into their guidance of students working with blame and forgiveness.

- Ongoing personal practice with blame is identified as essential for teachers, with the understanding that it is a continuous sadhana rather than a one-time resolution.

- Guided reflections on unreal others and unreal selves, the vulnerable feelings beneath blame, and the intention to forgive are to be used as practical tools in teaching contexts.

- Spiritually grounded activism that replaces blame with responsibility for changing causes and conditions is presented as a direction for applying these teachings at a societal level.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Origins and Universality of Blame**
- Blame is described as an expression of the fight response within the fight-flight-freeze stress response
- It is wired into every human being as part of the orienting response to perceived threat
- The negativity bias causes people to scan for what is wrong, reinforcing a tendency toward blame
- Blame is directed most strongly toward those perceived as most different — different political views, race, religion, or moral frameworks
- On an individual level, the "bad other" is whoever is not meeting basic needs for safety, gratification, and connection
- The Garden of Eden is referenced as a popular legend illustrating the existential universality of blame
**Healthy Anger vs. the Trance of Blame**
- Anger is described as intelligence — it alerts and mobilizes people to deal with obstacles
- For those who have experienced trauma, anger and blame are necessary to move out of a freeze response
- Premature forgiveness or suppression of anger can prevent full attention to wounds and self-protection
- Blame becomes a trance when the "on button jams" and stories of blame continue to fuel ongoing reactivity
- Unwise reactivity — withdrawal or lashing out — follows when blame hardens into armoring around the heart
**Unreal Other and Unreal Self**
- In the trance of blame, perception of the other person narrows to only their wrongness — they become an "unreal other"
- Simultaneously, the person blaming becomes an "unreal self," disconnected from their own wholeness
- The blaming self is described as inhabiting a limited story of a victimized self
- A guided reflection is offered to help students recognize how blame shrinks both self and other
**Why People Hold Onto Blame**
- Blame temporarily provides a sense of superiority, power, righteousness, and justification
- It buffers vulnerable feelings including: powerlessness, deficiency, fear of continued harm, grief, feeling unlovable, and the pain of accepting loss
- James Baldwin is quoted on the idea that people cling to hate because releasing it would force them to face their own pain
- A reflection exercise is described where students identify what unpleasant feelings they would face if they let go of blame
**Misunderstandings About Forgiveness**
- Belief that forgiving sacrifices justice or removes accountability — addressed by distinguishing between aversive blame and wise discrimination
- Belief that forgiving condones harmful behavior — addressed by clarifying that forgiveness does not mean saying harmful behavior is acceptable
- Belief that forgiving means allowing continued harm — addressed with examples of maintaining boundaries while forgiving
- Belief that releasing blame leads to passivity in activism — addressed by affirming that justice-seeking and forgiveness can coexist
**Three Steps Toward Forgiveness**
- Step One: Awakening the intention to forgive — recognizing that forgiveness cannot be willed but can be chosen as an intention; the willingness opens the door
- Step Two: The U-turn — shifting attention from outward blame to inner experience; bringing mindful attention and compassion to the vulnerable feelings beneath the blame
- Step Three: Widening to see the other more truly — after inner healing, perceiving the other person's unmet needs, suffering, and humanity; seeing their "leg in a trap"
**Practical Teaching Approaches**
- Using the evolutionary perspective to normalize blame without making students wrong for experiencing it
- Guided reflections on unreal other and unreal self to help students recognize the narrowing effect of blame
- Asking students what unpleasant feelings they would face if they let go of blame, then sharing responses in group
- The "blame police" dynamic is noted as a pitfall — exploring blame without causing defensiveness in others
- The concept of 100% responsibility is introduced — not as condoning harm, but as recognizing that inner freedom comes from taking responsibility for one's own reactions
- "Vengeance is a lazy form of grief" is offered as a teaching phrase that resonates with students
**Blame at the Societal Level**
- War and ongoing violence are described as cycles of blame and othering
- Peace and reconciliation processes and restorative justice are cited as healing approaches not rooted in blame
- Movements referenced as examples of spiritually-based activism: Buddhist Peace Fellowship, Poor People's Campaign, Love Army headed by Van Jones, Revolutionary Love by Valerie Carr

### Stories
**The Dog with Its Leg in a Trap**
- Shared by Tara Brach as a metaphor
- A person approaches a dog to pet it and the dog lunges at them in aggression; upon discovering the dog's leg is trapped, the response shifts from anger and blame to compassion — "you poor thing" — while still maintaining appropriate caution and not necessarily petting the dog
- Used to illustrate how seeing another's suffering shifts the heart from blame to care without requiring abandonment of self-protection
**The Editor and His Boss**
- Shared by Tara Brach about a man she was working with
- A man working as an editor at a major publishing house had a senior editor boss described as brilliant but a poor manager — critical, unappreciative, disorganized, and prone to last-minute demands
- He felt his sense of wellbeing was entirely dependent on how she treated him; he dreaded work, felt victimized, and experienced self-doubt
- Tara walked him through the three steps: he identified his intention not to live as a blaming, victimized self; made the U-turn and discovered the core wound was feeling disrespected, connected to feeling devalued by his father; brought compassion inwardly to that place
- He was then able to see his boss's own stress, fear of failure, and the pressures of the publishing industry
- He spoke with her without blame, acknowledging her high standards and communicating what he needed
- The situation did not fully resolve, but he found increasing freedom from the victim role, more humor, and less reactivity over time
**Valerie Carr and the Killing of Her Uncle**
- Shared by Tara Brach about Valerie Carr, a Sikh woman and social activist
- The first hate crime killing after 9/11 was of a Sikh man close to Valerie, whom she called uncle; he was killed by a man who called himself a patriot
- Valerie had a young son and feared for his safety growing up in a country where he could be seen as a terrorist
- Her inner response to her uncle's killing was: "Love more inclusively. See no one as enemy. You are the part of me I don't yet know. I choose to wonder about you."
- She spent years bringing loving presence to her own fear and hurt, refusing to let it harden into anger
- Fifteen years later, she and the murdered man's brother called the murderer, Frank, on the phone
- Frank expressed sorrow for those killed on 9/11 and then specifically said he was sorry for what he did to the brother
- The brother told Frank they had already forgiven him
- Valerie described this as an embodiment of the teachings — in the face of suffering, the intention is to love more, bring presence inwardly, and open to include the other
**The Two Women in Berlin**
- Shared by Tara Brach, sourced from Frank Ossostescu's book, during a workshop on grief and forgiveness in Berlin
- A woman at the back of the room said her father had been a prisoner in concentration camps and she could not forgive his killers — her heart felt like ice
- From the other side of the room, another woman said her heart also felt like a stone — her father had been a Nazi officer and guard in the camps who had killed people, and she could not forgive him either
- The two women made their way through approximately 200 people and embraced without words, simply holding each other
- Frank Ossostescu reflected that in those moments, their suffering was all of our suffering
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Chronic resentment and low-level blame are described as corrosive to intimacy, creating a wall that prevents tenderness and spontaneity in relationships
- The trance of blame is associated with physical sensations including a clenched gut, heat and pressure in the chest
- Being in blame is described as producing a tight, defended heart, a sense of grimness, self-doubt, and feeling victimized
- Trauma is specifically addressed — traumatic experiences can produce a freeze response where anger and blame are initially necessary to mobilize out of powerlessness; premature forgiveness in trauma contexts can prevent proper attention to wounds
- The "blame ready to happen" state is described as being easily triggered by sound, tone of voice, offhand comments, or being kept waiting — reactions disproportionate to what is actually occurring
- Self-judgment and shame about one's own blame or lack of forgiveness are noted as making forgiveness more difficult, not less
- The habit of blame is noted as playing out across multiple relationships simultaneously when one is deeply in the trance
- Suppressing anger is identified as burying the wound that most needs attention and preventing necessary inner work
