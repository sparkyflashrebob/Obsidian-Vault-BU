### Outline
### Introduction to the RAIN Practice Session

- Tara Brach opens the session by inviting participants to pause, close their eyes, and arrive in the present moment through breath and body awareness.

- Collective intention is set by sensing the shared field of all participants and their commitment to waking up together.

- The session is framed as a deep dive into RAIN, with Tara noting it is a living pathway of practice that continues to evolve and holds great value for teaching.

- Participant questions are identified as the primary structure for the session, with RAIN Partners introduced as an additional topic to be covered toward the end.

### The Goal of the RAIN Practice

- The goal of RAIN is described as no different from the goal of the entire meditative and spiritual path, which is to awaken the two wings of awareness — mindfulness and compassion.

- Through consistent practice, RAIN reveals an identity beyond the separate self, loosening identification with limited beliefs about who one is.

- Practicing RAIN builds deep trust in one's own capacity to find inner freedom, reducing dependence on external support systems such as therapists.

- A real-world example is shared of a man facing divorce after 30 years who, unable to access therapy, turned to RAIN and discovered self-compassion, which then opened compassion toward his wife and revealed an inner spirit he did not know existed.

### The Evolution of the RAIN Acronym

- Originally, RAIN stood for Recognize, Allow, Investigate, and Non-identification, a formulation Tara learned approximately 20 years prior.

- Over time, Tara added "investigate with kindness" to bring in the wing of the heart, as the original acronym lacked a compassion component.

- Feedback from practitioners revealed that the original acronym did not cut through deep self-aversion, fear, or anger, and that moving directly from investigation to non-identification did not work for most people dealing with significant emotional tangles.

- A dedicated step of self-compassion was found to be necessary for most major emotional difficulties, leading to the replacement of Non-identification with Nurture as the final active step.

- Non-identification is clarified as not a doing but rather the gift or natural outcome of completing the RAIN process, described as a freedom that arises rather than a step one performs.

- Practitioners using the original acronym without nurture often experienced what Tara calls a "dry emptiness," a cognitive sense of disidentification that was not embodied or emotionally integrated.

- A couple's example is used to illustrate the difference, where a husband who believed he had forgiven his wife's affair was shown through deeper RAIN practice to have bypassed genuine vulnerability, and only through nurture did he access real grief, compassion, and eventually a felt sense of oneness.

- A post-RAIN resting phase called "after the rain" is introduced, in which practitioners rest in the unfolded space of non-identification as the realized gift of the full practice.

### When RAIN May Not Be the Best Practice

- RAIN is not always the most appropriate practice, particularly when there is a risk of re-traumatization from directly contacting difficult material.

- In cases of trauma or significant fear, the recommended approach is to begin with self-soothing and nurturing to establish safety before engaging the full RAIN sequence.

- Resourcing techniques such as placing a hand on the heart with a few grounding words are suggested as anchors that students can return to if they become destabilized during investigation.

- Depression is identified as another context where beginning with RAIN may not be ideal, as it can lead to becoming swamped in the depressive state rather than finding freedom from it.

- Physical activity, social engagement, or other energy-shifting practices are recommended before RAIN in cases of depression.

- Some students simply lack the focus or clarity required to sustain the RAIN process and need to build foundational presence and resourcing capacity first.

### Addressing the Experience of RAIN Feeling Forced or Artificial

- It is described as entirely expected for RAIN to feel forced, because the practice interrupts habitual patterns of resistance or rumination that have significant momentum.

- Pausing to recognize and allow is inherently counter to the mind's tendency to continue generating reactive thoughts, and this friction is framed as a sign that a harmful habit is being interrupted.

- When RAIN feels artificial, it is understood as an indication that the practitioner is engaging with the steps mentally rather than somatically, meaning they are not yet embodied in the experience.

- The recommended response is to deepen the investigation by asking where the feeling lives in the body and guiding the student to feel it from the inside out.

### Using Stories as Entry Points in the Recognize Step

- Stories are acknowledged as a legitimate and honest part of human experience, and students can use them as portals into the body rather than obstacles to be bypassed.

- A technique of playing out the story like a movie and freezing it at the frame of greatest emotional intensity is offered as a way to use narrative as a bridge into somatic awareness.

- There are moments in the RAIN process where a student may lose the thread of their experience, and revisiting the triggering story can help reorient them before returning attention to the body.

### The Relationship Between the Recognize and Allow Steps

- The Recognize and Allow steps are described as naturally interpenetrating, with Recognize representing the wing of mindful noticing and Allow representing the wing of the heart making space.

- Neither step can occur in complete isolation, as some degree of allowing is required before anything can be recognized, and some recognizing must be present for there to be anything to allow.

- Despite their overlap, each step calls on a distinct dimension of attention, and students can still sense the intentional shift from noticing what is present to deliberately making room for it.

- Recognizing is described as having the immediate effect of enlarging one's sense of identity, moving from being inside the emotional cloud to becoming the sky that is aware of it.

- Allowing is identified as a powerful mechanism for dissolving identity because resistance is what keeps one hooked to an emotion, and releasing that resistance begins to free it.

- Krishnamurti's statement "I don't mind what happens" is cited as an expression of the deepest gift of allowing, pointing to an awareness that has room for whatever arises.

### Addressing the Fear of Being Overwhelmed by Allowing

- A common concern among students is that allowing sadness, grief, or anger will result in being completely swamped by the emotion.

- The longer a feeling has been resisted, the greater the fear of allowing it, and in cases of trauma this fear can reflect a genuine risk that must be respected.

- A distinction is drawn between allowing angry thoughts, which proliferate and can lead to acting out, and allowing the felt sense of anger in the body, which allows the emotion to arise and naturally fall away.

- Students are taught to titrate their exposure by committing to allow the experience fully for only a defined short window of time, giving them a sense of control and an exit if needed.

- Embodied contact with depression as a felt sense in the body, rather than engagement with depressive thoughts, is identified as the key to working with it through allowing without becoming overwhelmed.

### The Importance of Somatic Investigation Over Cognitive Analysis

- Investigation in RAIN is clarified as primarily a somatic and embodied process, with approximately 90 to 95 percent of the work directed toward felt experience in the body rather than cognitive understanding.

- The phrase "our issues are in our tissues" is used to convey that genuine healing requires direct contact with the place in the body where vulnerability actually lives.

- Habitual thought patterns are described as representations of reality rather than reality itself, and investigation is meant to move practitioners out of looping thoughts and into direct contact with living experience.

- A story from Malidoma Somé about village elders turning off lamps to see what truly wants to be seen is used as a metaphor for turning off the light of thought to access the deeper intelligence of the body.

- The body is framed as a portal to the formless, a living source of wisdom and love through which identity with the separate self begins to dissolve when one fully inhabits its aliveness.

- For highly analytical students prone to self-interrogation, curiosity is identified as the key quality to cultivate, contrasting genuine interest in reality with the mind's tendency to analyze for the purpose of control and security.

- Directly addressing the body's intelligence through questions such as "how do you want me to be with you" or "what else do you want me to know" is described as a powerful way to deepen investigation and open connection.

### The Nurture Step and Approaches to Self-Compassion

- A participant's description of nurture as having three parts — connecting with one's most awake self, offering loving energy to the vulnerable part, and resting in the resulting sense of wisdom — is affirmed as a powerful and accurate account of how nurturing can unfold.

- Investigation and nurture are described as naturally overlapping, as the act of asking what a vulnerable place most needs already carries care and begins the transition into nurturing.

- For students who remain identified with their vulnerability and cannot access a nurturing presence, going through the motions of self-nurturing is recommended as a valid approach, as the physical gesture and words can call forth the already-present capacity for care.

- Phrases such as "I care about you" or "I'm not leaving" are offered as examples of words that, even when not fully felt, can activate a deeper part of the self that genuinely holds those qualities.

- Prayer-like invitations to one's most awake and wise self are described as another approach, where sincere calling forth of the nurturing self actually brings it into presence.

- When no internal nurturing presence is accessible, calling on an external person or spiritual figure and imagining their loving energy flowing inward is presented as a widely used and effective method found across many traditions.

- A story of a priest comforting a dying woman by telling her that God has her picture on his dresser is shared as an example of the power of sensing oneself as held by an external source of compassion.

- Nurture is described as a life practice in which repeated engagement, even through going through the motions, progressively makes the nurturing response more accessible through neurological reinforcement.

- The experience of becoming both the holder and the held is described as a natural outcome of receiving nurturing, in which one relaxes open into a field of loving awareness that is one's true nature.

### The Organic and Flexible Nature of the RAIN Process

- RAIN is described not only as a stepwise technique but as a way of arousing the two wings of mindfulness and compassion that can unfold organically depending on the situation.

- At times, simply noticing a feeling of tightness and letting it be is sufficient for it to dissolve spontaneously, without the need for active investigation or nurturing.

- A deliberate full sequence of approximately 20 minutes can produce a genuine sense of freedom and disidentification from the stuck self, though multiple rounds may be needed for deeper issues.

- Core issues may require decades of gradual practice, with RAIN progressively loosening identification and deepening the resting in a larger, freer sense of being.

- Students are encouraged to understand that RAIN can operate across a spectrum from spontaneous momentary releases to gradual long-term transformation.

### RAIN Partners as a Co-Meditation Practice

- RAIN Partners is introduced as a co-meditation protocol designed to bring the empowering qualities of RAIN into a shared peer practice, removing the hierarchical dependency of the therapist-client model.

- The inspiration for the protocol came from years of facilitating RAIN in groups of four during weekend workshops, where participants would share their stuck place, allow together in silence, investigate and nurture individually, rest in after the rain, and then share insights.

- A pilot study conducted the previous fall with participants from the training program and the Washington area helped refine the protocol and generated reports of deepened inner work and increased intimacy between partners.

- RAIN Partners sessions are designed to take approximately 35 to 45 minutes and can be conducted weekly, biweekly, or monthly depending on what works for the pair.

- Sessions can take place in person, by phone, or over the internet, and the protocol is available on the training platform under resources.

- Prerequisites for RAIN Partners include having some existing mindfulness practice and prior experience working with RAIN.

- Partners can be friends, family members, colleagues, or people from the training program, including those from mentoring groups or the Facebook group.

- Reported benefits from pilot participants include increased accountability, fuller engagement with the process, reduced shame around personal difficulties, deeper learning that sticks, a sense of safety to explore avoided issues, and financial accessibility compared to therapy.

- One participant described the experience as a deep spiritual practice in which the sense of a separate self fades and is replaced by a sense of "we" and open-hearted awareness.

### Guided Light RAIN Practice

- A brief guided RAIN practice called "light rain" is facilitated as a closing exercise, inviting participants to identify a mild current difficulty rather than a deeply charged issue.

- Participants are guided through Recognize by naming where they feel caught or separate, through Allow by intentionally making room for the discomfort, and through Investigate by locating the felt sense in the body and listening to what most wants attention.

- The Nurture step is guided by sensing how the vulnerable place wants to be met and offering words or energy of kindness to it.

- After the rain is guided as a resting in the quality of presence that has unfolded, described as wakefulness, kindness, and a sense of being at home.

- The session closes with a verse adapted from poet Danna Faulds, pointing to love as not a place but a state of being, and grace as both received and offered back.

- Exploring RAIN in small group workshop formats is recommended as a powerful teaching application.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The RAIN Acronym and Its Evolution**
- RAIN originally stood for Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Non-identification
- Tara Brach taught this version for years but added "investigate with kindness" to bring in the heart
- Deep emotional tangles required a dedicated step of self-compassion; the original acronym did not provide this
- Without nurture, practitioners reached a "dry emptiness" — an intellectual non-identification that was not embodied
- The N was changed from Non-identification to Nurture to ensure both wings of awareness were present
- Non-identification is now understood as the gift or outcome of RAIN, not a step to be performed
- A pause called "After the Rain" was introduced to allow resting in the space of non-identification once it arises naturally
**The Two Wings of Awareness**
- Mindfulness and compassion are described as the two wings required for waking up
- Recognize initiates the wing of mindfulness; Allow initiates the wing of the heart
- Investigate deepens mindfulness; Nurture provides the full quality of heart
- The two wings spiral and interpenetrate throughout the process
**Recognize and Allow**
- Recognizing enlarges the sense of self — one becomes the sky aware of the cloud rather than being inside it
- Allowing releases resistance that keeps emotions hooked; it is described as an incredibly powerful piece of dissolving identity
- The two steps naturally interpenetrate: you cannot recognize without some degree of allowing, and allowing requires some recognizing
- Krishnamurti's statement "I don't mind what happens" is offered as the essence of allowing
**Investigate**
- Investigation is 90–95% somatic, not cognitive
- The phrase "your issues are in your tissues" is used to explain why embodied investigation is essential
- Thoughts are representations; the body is the portal to living reality
- Students are guided to feel where vulnerability lives in the body — throat, chest, belly
- Highly analytical students may slip into self-interrogation; curiosity is the key corrective
- Directly addressing the body — asking "what do you want me to know?" or "how do you want me to be with you?" — opens a deep level of connection
- Stories and narrative can be used as portals: students are guided to freeze the story at its most charged frame and then drop into the body
**Nurture**
- One student described nurture as having three parts: connecting with the most awake part of oneself, offering loving energy to the vulnerable part, and resting in the awakened sense of wisdom
- Investigation transitions naturally into nurturing; the act of asking "what does this vulnerable place most need?" already carries care
- For students still identified with vulnerability, going through the motions of nurturing — hand on heart, words of care — calls forth the already-present capacity for compassion
- Calling on a spiritual figure or trusted person and imagining their loving energy flowing inward is a widely used approach across traditions
- The more times nurturing is practiced, even through motions, the more accessible it becomes due to neurological wiring
**After the Rain**
- After completing RAIN, practitioners rest in what has unfolded — a sense of oneness, freedom from small identity
- This is described as the realization of non-identification, the gift of the full RAIN process
**When RAIN Is Not the Best Practice**
- Not appropriate when there is risk of re-traumatization
- In cases of trauma or great fear, safety and self-soothing must come first; nurturing may need to precede the full RAIN sequence
- With depression, physical activity or social engagement may be needed before RAIN to avoid being swamped
- Some students lack the focus or clarity for RAIN and need to build presence capacity first
**RAIN Partners**
- Inspired by group RAIN exercises done in fours during weekend workshops
- A protocol was developed for pairs, piloted in the fall prior to the session
- Sessions run 35–45 minutes, can be done by phone, in person, or online
- Partners share their stuck place, allow together in silence, then each investigates and nurtures individually, rests in After the Rain, and then shares what was learned
- Prerequisites: some mindfulness practice and prior experience with RAIN
- Partners can be friends, family, colleagues, or fellow program participants
- Reported benefits: accountability, deeper engagement, reduced shame, increased safety, sense of intimacy, and empowerment without dependence on a therapist

### Stories
**Man Facing Divorce Using RAIN**
Shared by Tara Brach. A man whose wife sought divorce after 30 years tried to access therapists but was met with delayed callbacks and wait lists, deepening his sense of rejection. He found guided meditations and then RAIN. Through the practice, his key realization was: "This is painful. I can be compassionate to myself." This opened compassion toward his wife. They are separated but both engaged in a healing process. He reported that RAIN revealed an inner spirit he did not know existed.
**The Couple and the Affair**
Shared by Tara Brach. A couple, both meditators, came to Tara for help. The husband had been heavily focused on his career and traveled frequently. During this time, his wife had an affair. He claimed to have forgiven her using RAIN in its original form, but his wife reported he was still pushing her away and being mistrustful. Tara guided him to deepen his investigation until he contacted real vulnerability, then offered kindness to it. This broke him open, allowing him to register the full weight of his hurt and grief. He entered couples therapy and gradually moved from self-compassion to compassion for his wife and then to a true, embodied sense of non-identification and belonging.
**Maladoma Somé and the Village Elders**
Shared by Tara Brach, referring to Maladoma Somé. After many years away at a Catholic boarding school, Somé returned to his home village. One night he tried to turn on the lights and was told by the village elders not to. They explained that in daylight you only see what you want to see, but when the lights are off at night, you see what wants to be seen. Tara uses this as a metaphor for moving beyond habitual thought into the body's deeper intelligence.
**The Dying Woman and the Priest**
Shared by Tara Brach, attributed to a priest. A woman dying of AIDS tells a priest she is lost, has ruined her life and every life around her, and believes there is no hope for her. The priest notices a framed photo of a pretty girl on her dresser and asks who it is. The woman brightens and says it is her daughter, the one beautiful thing in her life. The priest asks if she would forgive her daughter if she made a mistake and still love her. The woman says of course, she would do anything for her. The priest responds: "I want you to know that God has a picture of you on his dresser." Tara shares this as an example of the power of sensing oneself received by an external compassionate presence.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Risk of Re-traumatization**
- RAIN is not recommended when there is potential to re-traumatize; safety and resourcing must come first
- Practitioners with trauma may need months of nurturing and resourcing before they are ready to fully contact what is arising
- Having a resource available — such as a hand on the heart with a few words — provides a place to return to if investigation becomes destabilizing
**Fear of Being Overwhelmed**
- Many students fear that allowing sadness or grief will cause them to be swallowed up by it
- The longer something has been resisted, the greater the fear of allowing
- Guidance: staying with the felt sense rather than the proliferating thoughts allows the emotion to arise and fall away naturally
- Titration is recommended — allowing in small amounts within a defined time window
**Depression**
- With depression, beginning RAIN directly can lead to being swamped rather than finding freedom
- Physical exercise, social engagement, or energy-shifting activity may be needed before RAIN
- The training is to feel depression as a felt sense in the body rather than engaging with depressing thoughts
**Feeling That RAIN Is Forced or Artificial**
- Feeling forced is expected because RAIN interrupts habitual patterns of resisting or grasping
- Feeling artificial indicates the practitioner is engaging mentally rather than somatically — they are not in their bodies
- The corrective is to deepen investigation into the body: locating where the feeling lives, feeling it from the inside out
**Highly Analytical Students**
- Intelligent, analytical students may turn the investigation step into critical self-interrogation rather than spacious curiosity
- The corrective is to evoke genuine interest in truth and reality, contrasting it with the security-seeking function of mental analysis
**Inability to Access Nurturing**
- Some students remain identified with vulnerability and cannot access a nurturing presence
- Strategies include going through the motions of nurturing, using words of care even without feeling them, calling on the nurturing self through sincere inner prayer, or visualizing a trusted person or spiritual figure offering loving energy
**Lack of Focus or Clarity**
- Some students do not yet have sufficient presence or focus to sustain the RAIN process
- These students need to build their capacity for presence through other means before RAIN is accessible
