### Outline
### George Mumford's Background and Path to Mindfulness

- Chronic pain and substance abuse derailed an early aspiration to play professional basketball, ultimately redirecting George Mumford toward a path of self-development.

- Enrollment in Dr. Jon Kabat-Zinn's mindfulness-based stress reduction program marked the beginning of a sustained engagement with contemplative practice.

- Mindfulness was not initially pursued out of intellectual curiosity but out of urgent necessity, as it was the only remaining option for managing pain and supporting recovery.

- Collaboration with Dr. Kabat-Zinn led to the creation of the Inner City Stress Reduction Clinic in the early 1990s.

- Living at the Cambridge Insight Meditation Center for approximately six years provided an immersive foundation in meditation practice and teaching.

- Teacher Larry Rosenberg identified Mumford's potential and encouraged him to teach, despite his initial reluctance, framing that reluctance itself as a reason for suitability.

- Work with inmates in prisons preceded the invitation to teach mindfulness to the Chicago Bulls under Coach Phil Jackson, a collaboration that has continued for over 25 years.

- A parallel career as a financial analyst spanning 16 years ran alongside the developing contemplative practice before Mumford fully transitioned into teaching.

### The Threefold Training as the Foundation of Mindfulness Teaching

- The Noble Eightfold Path is organized into three interconnected trainings of wisdom, morality, and mental discipline, all of which must function together rather than in isolation.

- Wisdom encompasses right view and right intention, providing the understanding of impermanence, suffering, and the illusion of separateness that underpins all practice.

- Morality covers right speech, right action, and right livelihood, creating the purity of heart necessary for genuine investigation and practice.

- Mental training includes right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration, and cannot function effectively without being grounded in wisdom and morality.

- Mindfulness practiced without the support of wisdom and morality is not considered right mindfulness, as it lacks the ethical and understanding framework that gives it direction.

- Suffering is understood to arise from greed, hatred, and confusion, and is addressed respectively through renunciation and generosity, loving kindness and compassion, and clear understanding of how things work.

- Alignment with the lawfulness of the universe, rather than personal belief, is presented as the operating principle, illustrated by the analogy that gravity functions regardless of belief.

### The Five Spiritual Powers and Balancing Effort with Poise

- The five spiritual powers, referred to as superpowers, operate within the context of the threefold training and require careful balance to be effective.

- Faith and wisdom must be held in balance, as too much wisdom without trust leads to cynicism, while too much trust without wisdom leads to naivety.

- Right effort is distinguished from general effort by being grounded in wholesome intention, meaning it arises from a mind oriented toward compassion, renunciation, and love.

- Effort and concentration must also be balanced, as excessive concentration without effort produces sluggishness, while excessive effort without poise produces freneticism.

- The quality of one's being as a teacher directly influences what can be communicated, making the internal state of the teacher inseparable from the teaching itself.

### Self-Knowledge as the Starting Point for Leadership and Teaching

- Leading with wisdom is understood to begin with self-leadership, rooted in the ancient principle of knowing oneself.

- The process of knowing oneself requires a willingness to let go of fixed identities, as finding oneself involves a form of de-identification from what one previously believed oneself to be.

- Forgiveness of oneself is identified as the most direct path to self-discovery, based on the principle that one is not what one thinks one is, but rather what one thinks one is.

- The concept from Earl Nightingale's "The Strangest Secret" is invoked to reinforce that people become what they think about consistently throughout the day.

- Core values such as love, curiosity, truth, wisdom, integrity, and selfless service are presented as the moral anchors that prevent compromise from becoming a violation of one's essential character.

- Self-integrity, defined as not violating one's own core values, is emphasized as particularly foundational, especially in the context of teaching and leadership.

### Defining Leadership as Making Others Better

- Leadership is defined not as control or authority but as the capacity to make those around one better and to help others flourish.

- The analogy of a star surrounded by a cluster of stars is used to illustrate that the best way to shine is to help the entire community shine.

- Everyone, regardless of role or seniority, is considered a leader under this definition, provided they take personal responsibility for their actions and development.

- Treating people as whole beings with body, mind, heart, and spirit, rather than as objects or problems to be fixed, is presented as the prerequisite for genuine engagement.

- Seeing the divine spark or Buddha nature in every person one works with is described as the orientation that enables authentic teaching and leadership.

- Entering a teaching situation with the intention of being of service and meeting people where they are, rather than delivering a predetermined agenda, is identified as the most effective approach.

### Mindfulness in Practice and the Role of Personal Practice in Teaching

- The teacher's own mindfulness practice is what enables them to hold space, remain calm under pressure, and respond skillfully to unexpected situations.

- Being in the eye of the hurricane, meaning maintaining inner stillness amid external chaos, is described as the essential capacity that allows a teacher to keep a room from escalating.

- Preparation is valuable but must be held lightly, as real teaching requires the ability to abandon prepared material and respond to what is actually present in the room.

- Compassion toward oneself when mistakes occur is necessary for continued growth, including the willingness to say one does not know and to return with an answer later.

- Recording and reviewing one's own teaching is offered as a practical method for developing self-awareness and recognizing when one is functioning as a conduit rather than performing for an audience.

- Flow in leadership and teaching is described as a state in which there is no sense of a leader present, just the natural unfolding of what is needed.

### Working with Anxiety, Doubt, and the Hindrances in Teaching

- Anxiety is reframed not as a character flaw or disqualifier but as a mind state that comes and goes like all other phenomena, and one that can serve as a doorway rather than a barrier.

- Kierkegaard's concept of the alarming possibility of being able is cited to suggest that anxiety is inherently linked to freedom and potential rather than to incapacity.

- The nervous system's default programming to approach the pleasant, avoid the unpleasant, and space out the neutral is identified as the mechanism that must be worked with through wisdom and equanimity.

- Doubt is named as one of the classical hindrances alongside sensual desire, aversion, sloth and torpor, and restlessness, and is addressed by questioning whether the thought is true and returning attention to present bodily experience.

- Experiencing doubt or a sense of not belonging during a teaching, as occurred during an NBA championship preparation session, is normalized as something that can be treated as background noise without interrupting the teaching.

- Shifting from the story about a feeling to the direct physical sensation of it in the body is presented as the practical method for preventing a momentary hindrance from becoming a consuming narrative.

### Creating Space Between Stimulus and Response

- Viktor Frankl's principle that between stimulus and response there is a space, and in that space lies the power and freedom to choose, is identified as central to both mindfulness practice and leadership.

- The default future is described as the tendency to interpret new situations through the lens of past experience rather than remaining open to present possibility.

- A bus analogy is used to illustrate how the mind fills in incomplete information based on prior knowledge, bypassing the raw data of present experience.

- Pausing physically, breathing, and allowing a situation to unfold without immediately reacting is presented as the practical application of creating that space.

- Repeating the same behaviors while expecting different results is identified as the consequence of living from a default future rather than from present awareness.

- Accepting that a previously held belief or pattern was mistaken, and choosing to do better upon knowing better, is described as the act of de-legitimizing a limiting identity in order to grow.

### Making Mindfulness Accessible Across Diverse Contexts

- Sharing one's own experience, strength, and hope from the heart is identified as the most effective way to connect with audiences whose backgrounds differ significantly from one's own.

- Universal human experiences such as feeling disrespected, treated as a second-class citizen, or experiencing moments of happiness provide common ground across demographic differences.

- Listening deeply to understand how others see things is presented as a prerequisite for being understood, and as more important than finding culturally specific examples.

- Relating to every person as a masterpiece, from one Buddha nature to another, is described as the orientation that transcends identity differences and enables genuine connection.

- Practical entry points such as asking about happy moments and exploring how to create more of them are offered as accessible alternatives to formal silent sitting for resistant or unfamiliar audiences.

- The shared human experience of separation and powerlessness is identified as a universal touchstone that can anchor mindfulness teaching in any context.

### The Role of Positive Mindset, Social Support, and Growth Orientation in Practice

- Research on positive psychology supports the view that people operating from optimistic or positive mind states demonstrate enhanced cognition and broader perceptual capacity, described as the broaden and build theory.

- Community and social support are identified as the second key predictor of wellbeing and effectiveness, functioning not only through encouragement but also through loving accountability.

- A growth mindset, defined as the orientation that treats mistakes as feedback and life as a continuous opportunity for learning, is presented as essential for both practitioners and teachers.

- Joy of discovery and living in the mystery are offered as the experiential qualities that sustain a growth orientation over time.

- Success is defined, drawing on Earl Nightingale, as the progressive realization of a worthy cause or goal, meaning happiness must precede achievement rather than follow it.

- The Brahma Viharas of loving kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, and equanimity are described as active practices that cultivate positive mind states and serve as antidotes to jealousy, aversion, and reactivity.

### Finding One's Purpose and Niche as a Teacher

- Following one's bliss is offered as the primary guidance for aspiring teachers seeking to identify a niche or area of focus.

- If a clear direction is not yet apparent, teaching for the pure joy of alleviating suffering is presented as a sufficient and valid starting point.

- The specific population or sport one works with is less important than the quality of engagement with the individual or group in front of one.

- Developing a personal charter, meaning a clear articulation of one's purpose, core values, and what one can be counted on for, is described as a clarifying practice that emerged from a structured program called Living Life Creatively and Fully.

- Mumford's own charter is articulated as releasing the divine spark in each and every person encountered, grounded in values of love, courage, truth, wisdom, integrity, and selfless service.

- The question attributed to John Capitan of identifying one's big job, meaning what one can do that no one else can do, is presented as a useful frame for discovering one's unique teaching contribution.

### Next Steps

- Aspiring teachers are encouraged to deepen their grounding in the threefold training of wisdom, morality, and mental discipline as the non-negotiable foundation of effective mindfulness teaching.

- Continued personal practice, self-study, and self-reflection are affirmed as ongoing commitments rather than preparatory phases, with the understanding that the teacher's inner state directly shapes the teaching.

- Developing a personal charter articulating purpose, core values, and one's unique contribution is recommended as a clarifying step for those building a teaching identity.

- Teachers are encouraged to approach each new context with openness, meeting people where they are and releasing attachment to outcomes, trusting that showing up fully and authentically is sufficient.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Threefold Training**
- George Mumford grounds all his teaching in the fundamental teachings of the Buddha, specifically the Threefold Training: Wisdom, Morality, and Mental Discipline
- Wisdom encompasses right view and right intention
- Morality encompasses right speech, right action, and right livelihood
- Mental training encompasses right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration
- All three elements are interdependent and must work in sync; none is sufficient alone
- Mindfulness without wisdom and morality is not right mindfulness
**Wisdom**
- Wisdom has three components: revelation or insight, intellect and rational thought, and direct experience or intuition
- Wisdom must drive morality, and morality creates purity of heart enabling proper investigation
- Wisdom is understanding impermanence, suffering, and the illusion of separateness
- The universe operates according to a lawfulness; aligning with that lawfulness is the goal
- Einstein's question cited: whether the universe is friendly or unfriendly is the most important question
**Morality and Non-Harming**
- Right speech: speaking in ways that create harmony, honoring one's words, avoiding lying and gossiping
- Right action and right livelihood as expressions of non-harming
- Core values named by George: love, curiosity, commitment to truth, wisdom, integrity, honoring one's word, selfless service, compassion, courage
- Self-integrity means not violating one's own core values
**Mental Training and Mindfulness**
- Mindfulness is the heart of the practice; with it, other qualities can be developed
- Right effort must be grounded in right intention and wholesome mind states
- Effort and concentration must be balanced: too much concentration leads to sluggishness, too much effort leads to frenetic behavior
- Mindfulness helps right effort and right effort helps mindfulness; they are mutually supportive
- From waking to sleeping, one can practice mindfulness by observing the quality of mind: wholesome or unwholesome, loving or lustful
**The Noble Eightfold Path and Suffering**
- Suffering is caused by greed, hatred, and confusion
- Greed is countered with renunciation and generosity
- Hatred is countered with love and kindness/compassion
- Ignorance is countered with understanding how things work
- The lawfulness of the universe means hatred addressed with hatred does not work; only love addresses hatred
**The Five Spiritual Powers**
- Faith must be balanced by wisdom; too much wisdom leads to cynicism, too much faith leads to being overly credulous
- Effort must be balanced with poise and centeredness
- Mindfulness and concentration are the remaining powers, all operating within the context of the whole system
**The Brahma Viharas**
- Loving kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, and equanimity are the divine abodes
- Appreciative joy is the antidote to jealousy and envy
- Living in joy enhances cognitive function, increases pain tolerance, and deepens connection to self and others
- Equanimity relates to the eight worldly conditions including praise and blame
**Self-Leadership and Know Thyself**
- Leading with wisdom begins with self-leadership and self-development
- The formula stated: know thyself, be thyself, express thyself, share thyself
- Forgiveness of self is the best way to find oneself
- One is not what one thinks one is, but what one thinks one is
**Flow of Leadership**
- Leadership defined as making people around you better
- In true flow of leadership, there is no leader; it is just happening
- George describes himself as a star surrounded by a cluster of stars; helping the cluster shine is how he shines
- Everyone is a leader when they take personal responsibility
- People will not take personal responsibility if treated as a thing rather than a person
**Teaching Approach**
- George sees a masterpiece, Buddha nature, or divine spark in every person he works with
- He goes into teaching situations asking how he can be of service and meet people where they are
- Preparation is important but must yield to what is actually present in the room
- Personal practice and mindfulness inform his ability to hold space and respond skillfully
- Recording his own sessions helped him recognize when he was in flow and acting as a conduit
**Creating Space Between Stimulus and Response**
- Viktor Frankl cited: between stimulus and response there is a space; in that space lies the power and freedom to choose
- The process of perception is mechanical; the mind fills in blanks based on prior experience
- Pausing, breathing, and being still creates the space needed to see clearly and choose differently
- The default future: people see things based on past experience rather than open possibility
- Doing the same things expecting different results is described as insanity
**Readiness and Commitment to Practice**
- The readiness principle: once someone is truly ready, the practice becomes accessible
- Getting a direct experience of the practice working is the key to sustained engagement
- Even the Buddha forgot his childhood meditation experience and later remembered and returned to it
- Once a commitment is made and one consents to the practice being true and helpful, it becomes effective
**Niche and Teaching Opportunities**
- George advises following one's bliss when choosing a teaching focus
- If no clear niche exists, teach for the joy of it and for alleviating suffering
- George himself does not have a favorite sport to work with; what matters is the engagement with the person or team in front of him
**Optimism, Social Support, and Crisis as Challenge**
- Three factors cited for wellbeing and performance: positive mind state/optimism, social support and community accountability, and seeing crisis as a challenge
- The broaden and build theory: positive mind states enhance cognition and the ability to see possibilities
- Community holds individuals accountable in a kind way
- Seeing crisis as a challenge draws on Viktor Frankl's framework
**Core Values**
- Core values are universal and timeless in essence, though their manifestation may change
- Love, joy, and appreciative joy are cited as values that remain constant
- George's personal charter: purpose is to release the divine spark in each and every person; values are love, courage, truth, wisdom, integrity, selfless service, compassion; commitment is to be loving with a warrior spirit and a serving and compassionate heart
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Illusion of Separateness**
- George prefers the framing of the illusion of separateness over the traditional teaching of no-self
- All things are connected in more ways than we think
- Acting for oneself is simultaneously acting for the whole universe
**Buddha Nature and the Masterpiece Within**
- Every person carries Buddha nature, Christ consciousness, or a divine spark regardless of background
- The teacher's role is not to fix anyone but to relate to the whole person: body, mind, heart, and soul
- Michelangelo's approach to sculpture is used as a metaphor: the masterpiece is already there; the work is chipping away what conceals it
**The Lawfulness of the Universe**
- There is a lawfulness to how things work that is not personal
- Gravity does not require belief; similarly, the lawfulness of cause and effect operates regardless of belief
- Aligning with this lawfulness through wisdom, morality, and mindfulness is the path to reducing suffering
**The Word as Creation**
- What one holds in mind becomes reality and gets manifested
- Self-talk and internal dialogue shape external outcomes
- The word, intention, and what one is creating are the starting points of all action
**Impermanence and Non-Identification**
- Everything changes, including conditions, identities, and beliefs
- When conditions change and one is identified with a fixed view, letting go requires de-identifying and delegitimizing who one thinks one is
- Anxiety is described as a mind state that comes and goes like everything else; identifying with it is the problem, not the anxiety itself
**Joy as a Spiritual Orientation**
- Krishnamurti's phrase peace now and ever is adapted by George to joy now and always
- Joy is not a future reward contingent on success but a present orientation that enables success
- Success defined via Earl Nightingale: the progressive realization of a worthy cause or worthy goal
**Societal Application of Mindfulness**
- Mindfulness has been brought into prisons, schools, youth centers, corporate settings, and professional sports
- The practice of sharing experience, strength, and hope is universally relatable across demographic differences
- Speaking from the heart about common human experiences such as disrespect, powerlessness, and separation bridges gaps between teacher and student
**The Butterfly Effect and Collective Responsibility**
- The wings of a butterfly can affect a tsunami; every thought either makes things better or worse
- Human beings have the capacity to manifest what they hold in mind
- All that is given is returned: giving grief returns grief, giving love and wisdom returns love and wisdom

### Stories
**Burning the Ships**
- Shared by George Mumford, referencing the film Captain of Castile with Tyrone Power and Cesar Romero
- The Spanish conquistadors burned their ships upon arrival so there was no retreat
- Used as a metaphor for hitting bottom and having no option but to commit fully to the practice
**The Prison Class with Spanish-Speaking Inmates**
- Shared by George Mumford about one of his first teaching experiences at Norfolk Prison
- He arrived with detailed lecture notes prepared to deliver a comprehensive Buddhist teaching
- Of approximately 30 inmates, 28 spoke only Spanish
- He had to abandon his notes, work through an interpreter, and simply be present and responsive to what was in the room
- Used to illustrate the importance of mindfulness, adaptability, and meeting people where they are
**2,000 High School Students in a Gym**
- Shared by George Mumford about a speaking engagement in Roanoke
- He was not informed in advance that he would be speaking to 2,000 high school students in a gymnasium for 90 minutes
- He had to figure out how to hold the space and keep the group engaged
- Used to illustrate the necessity of holding space and remaining calm in unexpected situations
**The NBA Championship Pre-Game**
- Shared by George Mumford, referencing what he believes was the 2009 Celtics vs. Lakers NBA Championship
- While leading the team in meditation before a game, a voice in his head said he did not belong there
- He recognized this as doubt, one of the five hindrances, and treated it as background music without engaging the story
- Used to illustrate how to work with doubt and self-questioning during teaching
**The Dying Uncle**
- Shared by George Mumford about his uncle who moved to Boston for cancer treatment
- His uncle asked why George did not visit more; George disclosed he was attending recovery meetings for substance abuse
- When the uncle died, he chose not to take pain medication so he could have a clear consciousness when meeting his maker
- Used to illustrate the impact of honest communication and the power of choosing one's relationship to experience
**The Bus to Brockton**
- Shared by George Mumford as a personal story from growing up in Boston
- He was running to catch a bus, saw the letters BR on the front, assumed it was going to Brockton, and jumped on
- He had not given himself enough time to read the full destination and ended up going to Bridgewater instead
- Used as an analogy for how the mind fills in blanks based on prior experience rather than taking in raw data fully
**The Two Prison Classes**
- Shared by George Mumford about teaching at a recovery unit prison
- He gave the same presentation to two groups: newer inmates who responded with enthusiasm, and longer-term inmates who asked when it would be finished
- Used to illustrate equanimity in the face of praise and blame, and the importance of doing one's best and releasing attachment to results
**The Rugby Team and Leadership**
- Shared by George Mumford about a Massachusetts rugby coach speaking to five freshmen
- The freshmen defined leaders as seniors and captains and defined leadership as making people comply through reward and punishment
- The coach offered his own definition: leadership is making people around you better
- Used to introduce George's own definition of leadership
**Larry Rosenberg Asking George to Teach**
- Shared by George Mumford about his teacher Larry Rosenberg at the Cambridge Insight Meditation Center
- Rosenberg approached George and told him he should teach; George declined
- Rosenberg said the fact that George did not want to teach was exactly why he wanted him to teach
- George reluctantly entered the teaching path out of deference to his teacher
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Chronic Pain**
- George suffered from chronic pain stemming from injuries sustained as an aspiring basketball player at the University of Massachusetts
- Chronic pain persisted even after he got clean from substance abuse
- Learning to manage chronic pain was a primary motivation for entering the mindfulness-based stress reduction program
- Mindfulness practice proved effective not only for recovery but for managing chronic pain
**Substance Abuse and Recovery**
- George struggled with substance abuse alongside chronic pain
- Getting clean from substance abuse was a turning point that led him to mindfulness practice
- He describes hitting bottom as the readiness principle: trying everything else and finding nothing worked
- He has been in recovery for 34 and a half years at the time of the talk and has averaged over a book a week during that period
**Anxiety and Freedom**
- George observed that the more freedom he gained in recovery, the more anxiety he experienced
- He references Kierkegaard's concept of the alarming possibility of being able as the existential basis of anxiety
- Anxiety is described as a mind state that comes and goes; the problem is identification with it, not the anxiety itself
- The practice of being in the eye of the hurricane is offered as the response to anxiety
**Doubt as a Hindrance During Teaching**
- During the NBA Championship pre-game meditation, George experienced doubt arising, with an internal voice questioning whether he belonged
- He identified this as one of the five hindrances: sensual desire, aversion, sloth and torpor, worry and restlessness, and doubt
- His response was to treat the doubt as background music, stay in his body, breathe into the sensation, and continue teaching
**Student Question on Anxiety and Teaching**
- A student in the program raised the concern that dealing with anxiety makes the teaching role demanding on the nervous system despite practicing to calm it
- George responded that anxiety is always present and is on the other side of freedom and potential
- He advised changing the mind state rather than trying to eliminate anxiety, noting that the nervous system is programmed to approach the pleasant, avoid the unpleasant, and space out the neutral
- Wisdom and equanimity allow one to observe anxiety as a passing condition rather than an identity
**Feelings of Hypocrisy and Inadequacy After Teaching**
- A student question addressed the feeling of hypocrisy when leading while still finding one's own path, and feelings of inadequacy or vulnerability following a strong teaching session
- George acknowledged this experience directly through the NBA Championship story
- He advised recognizing such feelings as mind states based on past memories rather than present reality, staying in the body, breathing into the sensation, and not building a story around it
