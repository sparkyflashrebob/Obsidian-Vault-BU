### Key Points, Subjects and Themes
### Outline
### Forgiveness as an Inner Way of Being Rather Than a Reactive Act

- Forgiveness is described as a major gateway to awareness that extends far beyond behavioral or reconciliatory acts such as saying "I'm sorry."

- Treating forgiveness as a path rather than a response to harm means it must be cultivated as an ongoing inner orientation to life.

- When forgiveness is not already part of one's inner way of being, the skill, openness, and understanding required to enact it are absent in moments of harm.

- Embodying forgiveness as a constant inner state allows it to arise naturally and organically from the heart when it is needed, rather than being performed from the intellect.

### Understanding Embodied Harm as a Prerequisite to Embodied Forgiveness

- Most people have embodied harm through their life experiences far more deeply than they have embodied forgiveness, and this shapes how their acts of forgiveness emerge.

- Forgiveness that arises from a place of embodied harm is conditioned by suffering received from others, including individuals, strangers, spiritual communities, and society at large.

- Awareness of one's own suffering is identified as the essential starting point for any genuine forgiveness practice, because unawareness of personal suffering is itself a source of harm.

- A strong sense of right and wrong is presented as one of the primary internal conditions that precedes harmful action, as it narrows one's perspective and prevents genuine consideration of others.

- Motivation is identified as the first action before harm, and examining one's motivations reveals how suffering drives behavior before any conscious decision is made.

### Separation, Objectification, and the Conditions That Enable Harm

- A strong sense of right and wrong naturally produces a sense of separation from others, creating a dividing line that makes it easier to objectify people, communities, or ideas.

- Objectification of the other, whether a person, a community, or an idea, makes it psychologically easier to cause harm or to attack.

- Historical examples such as the dehumanization of Africans during slavery are cited to illustrate how this same mental framework operates at a societal scale to enable atrocity.

- Examining whether one objectifies others, regardless of who or what those others are, is presented as a necessary part of the forgiveness journey.

- Feeling separate from others is not framed as morally wrong or right but rather as a fundamental aspect of being human that must be understood in order to live in greater harmony.

### Perception, Belief, and Ingrained Patterns That Precede Harmful Action

- Harmful actions are often preceded by deeply ingrained beliefs and unconscious ways of being that have nothing to do with forgiveness and keep it at a distance.

- A common pattern is treating forgiveness as something reserved for later or for others, rooted in a belief that one is not capable of causing harm.

- Controlling behavior is identified as a common response to suffering, arising from the desire to protect oneself, yet it frequently causes harm to oneself or others in the process.

- Maintaining a reputation is another source of harm, as people may say or do things intended to prove who they believe themselves to be rather than acting from genuine awareness.

- Arrogance is named as a significant and often unrecognized form of suffering, illustrated through the experience of being told by a teacher that one's complaints about another person were rooted in arrogance rather than insight.

### The Mirror Exercise and Recognizing Oneself in the Other

- A self-created exercise of drawing a face and listing the perceived faults of another person on one side, then attempting to list one's own on the other, is described as a transformative practice.

- The difficulty of populating one's own side of the exercise, compared to the ease of listing the other person's faults, revealed a significant imbalance in self-awareness.

- Upon reflection, the qualities attributed to the other person were recognized as also present in oneself, which proved to be a startling and pivotal realization.

- Recognizing oneself in the other does not excuse the other person's behavior but shifts the focus to where genuine transformation is possible, which is within oneself.

### Forgiveness as Ceremony and the Role of the Body in Practice

- Embodying forgiveness is described as bringing something already present in one's DNA to the forefront of daily life in every moment, rather than reserving it for after harm has occurred.

- Ceremony is proposed as a more effective vehicle for embodied forgiveness than techniques or methods alone, because ceremony engages the whole body and all the senses.

- Zen ritual practices such as chanting, bowing, and prostration are cited as examples of how the body is used to attend to suffering of self and others simultaneously and in community.

- The Zen full moon ceremony known as fusatsu is described as a powerful forgiveness ceremony that extends reflection not only to one's own actions but to ancient karma spanning generations before and after the present.

- Ceremony creates a context in which forgiveness can be practiced even in the presence of people one dislikes, because the act of offering and bowing transcends personal conflict.

### The Five Gifts Visualization Practice

- A practice drawn from the teachings of Thich Nhat Hanh is offered as a ceremony that can be done individually or in community to cultivate the presence of love and forgiveness.

- The first gift offered in the practice is the statement "My dear, I am here for you," directed toward oneself, a friend, a community, the earth, or one's ancestors.

- The second gift is "My dear, I know you are there," acknowledging the existence and presence of the other.

- The third gift is "My dear, I know you suffer, and that is why I am here for you, I offer you my presence," which connects awareness of suffering to the act of showing up.

- The fourth gift is "My dear, I am suffering, and I want you to know I am suffering, I am doing my best," which introduces honesty and vulnerability into the practice.

- The fifth gift is "This is a happy moment," offered regardless of whether it is felt to be true, as an act of intentional orientation toward the sacredness of the present.

### Forgiveness as Adoration for Life and Seeing Life as Sacred

- Embodied forgiveness is ultimately described as adoration for life and for those who share it, which allows one to live without waiting for a future moment to forgive.

- Touching the earth by touching the heart is offered as a way of understanding how inner transformation connects to the world around us.

- Each moment is presented as a chance for transformation, meaning that what has happened is not fixed and can change through sustained awareness and practice.

- Recognizing that others suffer just as one does, and that suffering is expressed in different ways, is identified as a foundation for genuine forgiveness and compassion.

### Forgiveness and Systemic or Institutional Harm

- A question is raised about the difficulty of extending forgiveness to institutions, systems, or large-scale historical harms such as genocide, even when compassion for individuals feels accessible.

- Zenju Earthlyn Manuel clarifies that not all Dharma practices are designed to address systemic oppression directly, and that practitioners are not required to extend compassion to harmful leaders or institutions.

- The purpose of inner forgiveness practice in relation to systemic harm is to cultivate a resource within oneself so that when one encounters the worst suffering, something stable and clear is available from within.

- Formal apologies for historical atrocities such as slavery are viewed skeptically without the accompanying inner and structural transformation that would make such gestures meaningful.

- Practitioners are encouraged to examine what their compassion actually is, what wisdom it is based on, and what they believe they are offering, before attempting to extend it to large-scale harm.

- Anger, grief, and other human responses to systemic harm are affirmed as valid and necessary, and practitioners are encouraged not to suppress their humanity in the name of spiritual practice.

### The Relationship Between Inner Transformation and Outer Action

- Inner transformation and interrelationship with others are described as inseparable, because a shift in one's inner state inevitably affects how one shows up with every person encountered.

- Sitting with a practice over months or years, rather than seeking quick resolution, is presented as the mechanism through which genuine change becomes possible in relationships.

- Students are encouraged to engage in social justice and community action in the world while using the contemplative space to reflect inward, so that external action arises from clarity rather than reactivity.

- The book "The Way of Tenderness Awakening Through Race, Sexuality, and Gender" is clarified as being primarily about awakening rather than about race, with oppression serving as one gateway among many into deeper self-knowledge.

- Practitioners are invited to identify their own gateway to awakening, whether through oppression, illness, death, family, or other life experiences, rather than assuming one path applies to all.

### Holding Complexity in Relationships Where Harm and Suffering Coexist

- A question is raised about how to hold the tension between understanding that a person's harmful behavior stems from their own suffering and recognizing that they still bear responsibility for how they act.

- Zenju Earthlyn Manuel responds by emphasizing that taking responsibility for another person's situation, even a family member's, is not the work being called for in this practice.

- Understanding the historical and generational context of a person's harmful behavior, such as a parent who was themselves abused, can shift one's perception without excusing the harm caused.

- The focus of the practice is on living one's own life fully and with presence, rather than on fixing or managing others, and this fullness itself tends to create distance from harmful dynamics.

- People who carry a perpetrating energy often naturally withdraw from those who are grounded and fully present, which is offered as evidence that inner work has tangible relational effects.

### Patterns of Attraction and the Journey of Self-Understanding in Relationships

- A question is raised about repeatedly attracting people with narcissistic personalities and what this pattern might reflect about oneself.

- Zenju Earthlyn Manuel suggests that such people may be appearing in one's life as an invitation to practice saying no and to make choices that align with one's own wellbeing.

- Examining what draws one to certain types of people before their harmful qualities become apparent is more useful than categorizing them or oneself after the fact.

- The question of what exists just before the choice, described as the breath before the word, is offered as the deeper inquiry that can illuminate the pattern.

- Understanding such patterns is framed as a potentially lifelong journey that goes deeper than projection theory or psychological labeling, and what one learns through it becomes what one has to teach.

### Forgiveness in the Context of Colonialism and Collective Historical Harm

- A question is raised about how to begin to bless or forgive the effects of colonialism after a day of confronting its ongoing impact through affinity group work.

- Zenju Earthlyn Manuel reaffirms that inner work is the starting point, noting that just as harmful actions ripple outward and affect everyone around a person, so does genuine inner transformation.

- Practitioners who have already studied political and historical frameworks are encouraged to use contemplative practice to go deeper than the information they already hold, rather than repeating the same entry point.

- Allowing the full pain of confronting colonial harm to be felt and moved through is presented as the path, rather than trying to resolve or transcend it prematurely.

- Remaining fully human and fully present within that pain is described as the condition for genuine awakening, and one's presence in that state is itself an expression of love.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to continue developing forgiveness as a daily inner practice rather than a reactive response, using ceremony, visualization, and sitting practice as ongoing tools.

- The five gifts practice is offered as something participants can take into their own lives and adapt for use with themselves, friends, communities, or the natural world.

- Practitioners are directed to examine their own suffering, motivations, and patterns of separation as foundational preparation before attempting to extend forgiveness outward.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Forgiveness as an Inner Way of Being**
- Zenju framed forgiveness not as something done after a harmful act, but as an ongoing inner orientation
- She described it as a major gateway to awareness, more than a behavioral or reconciliatory act
- Forgiveness needs to be part of one's DNA, brought to the forefront of daily life in every moment
- Without cultivating forgiveness as an inner way of being, the skill is not available when harm occurs
- When forgiveness is embodied, it arises naturally and organically from the heart
**Embodying Harm**
- Most people have embodied harm through life experience rather than forgiveness
- Acts of forgiveness often emerge from the place of embodied harm
- Sources of embodied harm include experiences from others, strangers, spiritual communities, and society
**Prerequisites to Forgiveness: Understanding One's Own Suffering**
- Forgiveness begins with knowing how one suffers and what that suffering is
- Unawareness of one's own suffering creates the conditions for harm
- Key patterns identified as sources of harm:
  - A strong sense of right and wrong that separates self from other
  - Objectification of others, making harm easier
  - Controlling behavior as a response to suffering and self-protection
  - Maintaining a reputation through harmful words or actions
  - Arrogance and the belief that one is always right
**Motivation as the First Action Before Harm**
- Every action is preceded by a motivation
- Understanding one's motivation reveals how one suffers
- Motivation is the first action before harm occurs
**Ceremony as the Vehicle for Embodied Forgiveness**

- Zen practices such as chanting, bowing, and prostration bring the body into the practice
- The full moon ceremony in Zen, called Fusatsu, is described as a forgiveness ceremony addressing ancient karma across generations
- Ceremony allows forgiveness to extend backward and forward across generations
- Ritual and ceremony are described as powerful ways to attend to suffering of self and others together

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Forgiveness as a Path, Not an Event**
- Forgiveness is presented as a lifelong path and inner orientation, not a one-time act
- It requires preparation through self-reflection, understanding suffering, and recognizing one's own patterns of harm
**The Mirror of the Other**
- The exercise of listing qualities of a difficult person and then recognizing those same qualities in oneself is offered as a transformative practice
- Seeing the mountains in each other — recognizing shared terrain — makes peace possible
**Ceremony and the Body in Spiritual Practice**
- Dharma practice is not only a cognitive or intellectual exercise; it requires the body
- Sitting meditation is described as a seeing practice, not merely a sitting practice
- Ceremony integrates the whole self — body, senses, movement — into the practice of forgiveness
**Generational and Ancestral Forgiveness**
- The Fusatsu ceremony addresses karma from aeons past, not only personal actions
- Forgiveness practice needs to extend across generations, both backward and forward
- Understanding the historical and generational context of a person's behavior (as with Zenju's mother) does not excuse harm but opens the door to deeper understanding
**Inner Work and Social Action**
- Inner transformation and social justice work are not mutually exclusive but serve different functions
- Students are encouraged to engage in community organizations for social action
- The spiritual practice space is reserved for deep self-reflection and understanding of personal suffering
- Inner transformation affects those around us in ways that cannot always be measured or reported
**The Human Condition and Discomfort**
- Life is not comfortable; the human condition is inherently uncomfortable
- That discomfort is described as the juice of awakening
- Practitioners are encouraged not to suppress or bypass their humanity, including anger and pain
**Awakening Through Oppression and Identity**
- The book The Way of Tenderness is described as being about awakening, using race, sexuality, and gender as gateways
- Oppression is one gateway among many; others include death, illness, and family
- Internalized oppression is acknowledged as part of the practitioner's landscape
**Choice and Presence in Daily Life**
- In fast-paced or difficult environments, the practice is to remain in awareness and breath at all times
- Meditation is a seeing practice that applies to every moment, not only formal sitting
- Choice is emphasized as a key capacity developed through practice

### Stories
**The Roommate and the Exercise of the Two-Sided Face**
- Shared by Zenju Earthlyn Manuel, referring to herself and a former roommate
- Zenju went to a teacher in the Naikan tradition to complain at length about her roommate's faults
- The teacher told her she was arrogant; Zenju resisted this for about a year
- She eventually created an exercise: drawing a face with the roommate's qualities on one side and her own on the other
- She listed twelve qualities for the roommate and struggled to list even one or two for herself
- Upon reflection, she realized all twelve qualities of the roommate were also present in herself
- She placed the drawing on her altar and sat with it for months through chanting and stillness
- That roommate became a friend of 25 to 30 years; they still speak despite ongoing friction
**The Teacher Who Called Her Arrogant**
- Shared by Zenju Earthlyn Manuel, referring to a female teacher in the Naikan tradition
- Zenju was presenting her case against her roommate when the teacher simply said, "You're arrogant"
- Zenju continued to build her case, and the teacher repeated the assessment
- It took Zenju approximately one year to accept and understand what arrogance meant in her own life
**Zenju's Mother and Childhood Violence**
- Shared by Zenju Earthlyn Manuel, referring to her mother
- Her mother was born in 1910 in rural Alexandria, Louisiana, and had witnessed lynchings
- Her mother beat Zenju but not her sisters, believing Zenju could take it
- Zenju later spoke with her mother, who said, "I thought you could take it"
- Zenju's response was, "I couldn't"
- Her mother had never lived with Black people; she was raised from age eight by a Russian Jewish family in Louisiana
- Zenju attended Jewish schools as a result
- The violence at home and in desegregated schools caused Zenju to become increasingly silent as a teenager, which led her to writing and poetry
**The Sister and the Year of Disconnection**
- Shared by Zenju Earthlyn Manuel, referring to her older sister
- There was a period of significant conflict between Zenju and her older sister
- Zenju disconnected entirely for about a year
- When she returned, she felt an overwhelming outpouring of love toward her sister
- The relationship became the best it had ever been
**Jerome and the Affinity Group**
- Shared by Jerome during the Q&A
- Jerome described a difficult day involving a white affinity group and engagement with the effects of colonialism
- He asked how to begin to bless the effects of colonialism
- Zenju responded by redirecting toward inner work and trusting that inner transformation affects those around us
