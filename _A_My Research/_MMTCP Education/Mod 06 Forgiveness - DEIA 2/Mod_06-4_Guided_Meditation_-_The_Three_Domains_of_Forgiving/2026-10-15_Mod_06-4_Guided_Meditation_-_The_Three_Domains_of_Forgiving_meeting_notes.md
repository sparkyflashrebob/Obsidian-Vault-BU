### Outline
### Introduction to the Forgiveness Meditation Practice

- Participants are invited to close their eyes gently and ensure they are seated comfortably with their bodies at ease.

- Breath is encouraged to remain natural and easy throughout the practice.

- Attention is directed to the breath as a means of settling the body and mind into a relaxed state.

- The human experience is acknowledged as containing a significant measure of both joys and sorrows given to all people.

- Breathing is focused gently in the area of the heart as a way of opening into the practice.

### The First Direction of Forgiveness - Asking Forgiveness of Others

- The first of three directions of forgiveness involves asking forgiveness from those one has hurt or harmed.

- Practitioners are guided to recognize that there are many ways in which they have hurt, betrayed, or abandoned others, knowingly or unknowingly.

- These harmful actions are understood to arise from one's own pain, fear, anger, confusion, and ignorance.

- Participants are invited to visualize specific ways they have caused suffering to others and to feel the pain that resulted from those actions.

- A particular circumstance is chosen as a focus point for asking forgiveness, allowing the practitioner to feel the sorrow and regret they carry.

- The guided words offered are a direct appeal to those harmed, acknowledging the hurt caused out of fear, pain, anger, and confusion.

- Practitioners are encouraged to sense that forgiveness is possible and that the burden of unresolved harm can be released.

### The Second Direction of Forgiveness - Forgiving Oneself

- The second direction of forgiveness turns inward, recognizing that in harming others, one has also harmed oneself.

- Practitioners are guided to acknowledge the ways they have betrayed or abandoned themselves through thought, word, or deed.

- One particular instance of self-harm or self-betrayal is chosen as a focus for extending forgiveness inward.

- Touching this burden with mercy and tenderness is emphasized as the appropriate inner posture for self-forgiveness.

- The guided words offered affirm the intention to extend mercy and forgiveness to oneself for past actions taken out of pain, fear, hurt, and confusion.

- Resting in the kindness and tenderness that arises from self-forgiveness is encouraged as a natural outcome of this direction.

### The Third Direction of Forgiveness - Forgiving Those Who Have Caused Harm

- The third direction of forgiveness is extended outward toward those who have hurt or harmed the practitioner.

- It is acknowledged that every person has experienced betrayal, abandonment, or harm in some form.

- Practitioners are guided to picture and tenderly remember the hurts and sorrows they have carried as a result of others' actions.

- One specific instance of being hurt or betrayed is chosen as the focus, allowing the practitioner to feel the depth of sorrow carried from that experience.

- Forgiveness is framed as something that can be offered to the extent that the heart is ready, without forcing a premature release.

- The harmful actions of others are understood to have arisen from their own fear, pain, confusion, and anger, which provides a basis for extending compassion.

- The guided words offered express a turning of the heart in the direction of forgiveness and a willingness to release the burden of pain that has been carried too long.

### Reflections on Teaching the Three Directions of Forgiveness

- The practice is described as a tender and emotionally significant process that touches deep layers of the human experience.

- Teachers are encouraged to consider dividing the three directions into separate sessions to allow more time and depth for each.

- Offering participants quiet sitting time or small group sharing after the practice is suggested as a way to integrate the experience.

- The process is described as not only tender but also ennobling and powerful, reflecting the human capacity to choose one's spirit as one moves through the world.

- Something uplifting is identified in the recognition that forgiveness is a genuine possibility available to every human being.

- Participants are encouraged to receive the practice both for their own personal benefit and through the lens of the teachers they are and will become.

- Offering this practice to others is framed as a form of medicine that can be brought into the world through skilled and compassionate teaching.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to consider how to structure the three directions of forgiveness when offering the practice to others, including whether to present them together or separately.

- Thought is to be given to how much time to allocate to each direction and how to support participants in integrating the experience afterward.

- The practice is to be carried forward both as a personal contemplative tool and as a teaching offering to be shared with others in the world.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Three Directions of Forgiveness**
- Jack Kornfield structures the meditation around three distinct directions or domains of forgiveness
- Each direction is presented as a sequential step in the practice
- Participants are invited to choose one specific circumstance within each direction to work with
**First Direction — Asking Forgiveness of Others**
- Acknowledging the many ways one has hurt, harmed, betrayed, or abandoned others
- Recognizing that harm may have been caused knowingly or unknowingly
- Harm is understood to arise out of one's own pain, fear, anger, confusion, and ignorance
- Participants are guided to visualize specific people and circumstances
- The practice involves feeling sorrow and regret, then asking for forgiveness
- Repeated phrases: "I ask your forgiveness. Please forgive me."
- Sensing the possibility of being forgiven and releasing the burden
**Second Direction — Forgiving Oneself**
- Recognizing that in harming others, one has also harmed oneself
- Acknowledging self-betrayal and self-abandonment in thought, word, or deed
- Participants are guided to feel the sorrow carried from self-caused harm
- Touching the burden with mercy and tenderness
- Extending forgiveness to oneself, even beginning to forgive
- Repeated phrases: "I now offer myself forgiveness. I forgive myself."
- Resting in the kindness and tenderness that arises from self-forgiveness
**Third Direction — Forgiving Those Who Have Hurt Us**
- Acknowledging that every person has been betrayed and harmed by others
- Harm from others may have been caused knowingly or unknowingly, through abuse or abandonment
- Participants are guided to picture and remember specific hurts and sorrows
- Choosing one particular hurt or betrayal to work with
- Forgiveness is offered to the extent that the heart is ready
- The intention to forgive is itself recognized as a beginning
- Repeated phrases: "I now offer you forgiveness. I release you. I forgive you."
- Sensing the possibility of moving toward forgiveness and lifting the burdens of the heart
**Teaching Guidance for MMTCP Participants**
- Jack Kornfield addresses participants as current and future teachers
- Suggests the three directions can be divided and taught separately
- Recommends allowing more time for the practice when teaching
- Suggests options after the practice: sitting quietly, small group sharing, or reporting back to the full group
- Frames the practice as "medicine" to be offered to others and to the world
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Universal Nature of Harm and Forgiveness**
- Jack Kornfield emphasizes that all human beings have both caused harm and been harmed
- The practice is framed as universally applicable, not reserved for extreme circumstances
- Carrying unforgiveness is described as a burden that closes the heart and causes ongoing pain
**The Heart as the Seat of Forgiveness**
- The meditation consistently returns to the heart as the locus of forgiveness work
- Breathing into the area of the heart is used as an anchor throughout the practice
- Barriers and emotions held in the heart are acknowledged before forgiveness is extended
**Forgiveness as a Choice and a Direction**
- Forgiveness is presented not as a single event but as an intention and a direction one turns toward
- The phrase "to the extent that I'm ready" acknowledges that forgiveness is a process
- The act of choosing forgiveness is described as ennobling and uplifting
**Forgiveness as Spiritual Medicine**
- Jack Kornfield frames the practice as medicine for both the practitioner and those they will teach
- The capacity to choose one's spirit as one moves through the world is highlighted as a distinctly human quality
- The practice is presented as something that lifts burdens and ennobles the human spirit
**Forgiveness in the Context of Teacher Training**
- Participants are encouraged to experience the practice both personally and through the lens of a teacher
- The transmission of forgiveness practice to others is framed as a meaningful and powerful offering
