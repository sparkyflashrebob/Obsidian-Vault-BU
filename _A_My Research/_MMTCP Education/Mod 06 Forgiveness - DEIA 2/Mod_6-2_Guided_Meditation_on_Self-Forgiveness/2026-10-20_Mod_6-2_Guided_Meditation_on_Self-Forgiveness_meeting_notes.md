### Mod 6-2 Guided Meditation on Self-Forgiveness
📅 October 20, 2026 — 11:17 AM
👥 @Tara Brach (Guided Meditation Teacher/Facilitator)
Tags: Self-Forgiveness, Guided Meditation, Self-Compassion, Mindfulness, Inner Healing, Vulnerability, Spiritual Practice
### Outline
### Settling Into the Practice Through Physical Awareness and Breath

- Attention is drawn to finding a seated position that balances alertness with ease and comfort.

- Releasing obvious areas of tension in the body is encouraged as an entry point into the practice.

- Breath is directed to be felt at the heart, as though it could flow in and out of that space.

- Awareness of the present emotional mood in the heart is invited as a grounding step.

- Listening inward to one's intention is introduced as a way to open and free the heart before proceeding.

### Identifying an Aspect of Self That Feels Unforgivable

- Scanning one's life is guided as a way to bring to mind a behavior or aspect of self, past or current, that has felt unforgivable.

- A recommendation is made to avoid selecting something that feels deeply traumatizing, choosing instead a level of emotional charge that feels workable.

- Examples offered include tendencies toward judgment and control, ways one has hurt people who matter, or harm caused to one's own life.

- Addictive behavior is named as one possible area where self-forgiveness may feel particularly difficult.

- Leaning into the aversion is encouraged, asking what feels so wrong or bad about the behavior, emotion, or pattern of thinking.

### Exploring the Underlying Drivers of Unacceptable Behavior

- Attention is directed deeper into what is driving the part of oneself that acts in ways later condemned.

- Visualizing the mental scene in which harm was caused is offered as a way to sense both the behavior and its underlying motivation.

- Questions are posed about what needs are being satisfied or what fears are being soothed in behaviors such as addiction to food, nicotine, or alcohol.

- Consideration is invited as to whether wounding another person arose from hurt, insecurity, or a need to feel safe.

- Understanding the internal state present during the condemned behavior is framed as essential to the process of self-forgiveness.

### Witnessing Oneself With Compassion Through a Loving Perspective

- Seeing oneself through the eyes of the most understanding and loving friend, relative, deity, or awakened inner self is introduced as a practice.

- Recognizing that one was, in a sense, caught in a trap when acting in condemned ways is offered as a reframe that supports compassion.

- Feeling the underlying wants and fears directly in the body, heart, and mind is encouraged even while disliking the behaviors themselves.

- Holding the underlying vulnerability with compassion, as a wise and caring witness, is presented as the appropriate inner stance.

- Placing a hand on the heart is suggested as a physical gesture to deepen the sense of being in relationship with the vulnerable part of oneself.

### Offering Messages of Self-Forgiveness and Compassionate Communication

- Sensing what message or communication of self-forgiveness would feel most healing is invited as a personal inquiry.

- Classic phrases of self-compassion are offered, such as "I see I have caused myself suffering and I forgive myself now."

- Simpler expressions such as "it's okay" or "I care about the suffering" are presented as equally valid alternatives.

- A wordless, tender stream of inner energy is acknowledged as another form that self-forgiveness can take.

- Meeting whatever arises in the moment, whether fear, judgment, shame, grief, or numbness, with a message of forgiveness is encouraged.

- Even resistance to the practice itself is included as something that can be met with the word "forgiven."

### Sustaining the Practice and Resting in Wholeness

- Calling on the presence of someone considered truly loving and understanding is suggested as a support when feeling stuck.

- Reminding oneself that "it is my intention to forgive myself when I am able" is offered as an anchor during difficult moments.

- Scanning for any judgment arising about how one is performing the self-forgiveness practice is recommended, with that judgment also being met with "forgiven."

- Gradually, a sense of spaciousness that includes whatever is present is described as the natural unfolding of the practice.

- Resting in a truth or wholeness of being that is inherently tender and kind is named as the deeper orientation being cultivated.

### Next Steps

- Participants are invited to remain in silence or use a journal to capture any insights, openings, or challenges that emerge from the practice.

- Holding one's own being with forgiveness is established as the ongoing intention to carry forward beyond the session.


### Key Points, Subjects and Themes
- Settling into a state of alert ease through body awareness and breath
- Scanning one's life for behaviors or aspects of self that feel unforgivable
- Identifying the underlying fears, needs, and vulnerabilities driving condemned behaviors
- Witnessing oneself through the eyes of a loving, understanding presence
- Practicing self-compassion through classic phrases and wordless tender energy
- Extending forgiveness even to resistance, judgment, shame, and numbness
- Calling on an external loving presence to support the forgiveness process
- Holding the intention to forgive even when full forgiveness is not yet accessible
- Resting in a sense of wholeness and inherent tenderness of being
- Using journaling to capture insights, openings, and challenges from the practice
### Detailed Subject Matter
- **Body and Breath Awareness**
  - Participants are guided to sit alert and at ease
  - Attention is brought to areas of tension and invited to release
  - Breath is felt at the heart, as if flowing in and out of the heart center
- **Identifying What Feels Unforgivable**
  - Practitioners are asked to bring to mind a behavior, past or current, that has felt unforgivable
  - Guidance is given to choose something with a workable level of emotional charge, not something deeply traumatizing
  - Examples offered include being judgmental or controlling, harming people who matter, harming one's own life, and addictive behaviors such as food, nicotine, or alcohol
- **Exploring the Aversion**
  - Practitioners are encouraged to lean into what feels wrong or bad about the behavior
  - The question is posed: what is the worst part about this behavior, emotion, or way of thinking?
- **Understanding the Underlying Drivers**
  - Attention is directed toward what is driving the unacceptable behavior
  - Questions raised include: what needs are being satisfied, what fears are being soothed, what is happening internally when acting out
  - Examples of underlying states include hurt, insecurity, and the need to feel safe
- **The Loving Witness**
  - Practitioners are invited to witness themselves through the eyes of the most understanding, loving friend, relative, deity, or the most awake part of their own being
  - The metaphor of having a leg in a trap is used to describe the state of acting in condemned ways
  - Underlying vulnerability is to be held with compassion even while disliking the behaviors
- **Self-Forgiveness Phrases and Gestures**
  - Placing a hand on the heart is offered as a physical gesture of self-relationship
  - Classic phrases of self-compassion are offered, such as "I see I've caused myself suffering and I forgive myself now"
  - Simpler words such as "it's okay" and "I care about the suffering" are also offered
  - A wordless stream of tender energy is acknowledged as equally valid
  - The word "forgiven, forgiven" is offered as a repeatable phrase
- **Extending Forgiveness to All Arising States**
  - Forgiveness is extended to fear, judgment, shame, grief, and numbness
  - Even resistance to the practice itself is included in the forgiveness
- **Calling on External Support**
  - Practitioners are invited to call on the presence of someone considered loving and understanding
  - The compassion, mercy, and kindness of that presence is visualized as flowing inward
- **Holding Intention When Stuck**
  - The phrase "It is my intention to forgive myself when I am able" is offered for moments of difficulty
  - Practitioners are encouraged to notice any judgment about how they are performing the practice and to forgive that too
- **Resting in Wholeness**
  - The practice moves toward resting in a truth or wholeness of being described as inherently tender and kind
  - Silence or journaling is offered as a way to close the session and capture what emerged
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
- **Self-Forgiveness as a Heart Practice**
  - The heart is treated as the center of the practice, with breath and attention directed there throughout
  - Opening and freeing the heart is named as the overarching intention of the session
- **Compassion for the Vulnerable Self**
  - The teaching emphasizes that behaviors we condemn in ourselves arise from underlying vulnerability, fear, and unmet need
  - Holding that vulnerability with compassion, even while disliking the behavior, is presented as the path to forgiveness
- **The Trap Metaphor**
  - The image of having a leg in a trap is used to convey that harmful or condemned behavior is not a sign of fundamental badness but of being caught in pain or limitation
- **Forgiveness as Inclusive Space**
  - The practice expands to include not just the original wound or behavior but all states that arise during the practice itself, including resistance and self-judgment
  - This reflects a teaching that forgiveness is not a single act but an ever-widening space of acceptance
- **Intention as a Bridge**
  - When full forgiveness is not accessible, holding the intention to forgive is offered as a valid and meaningful practice in itself
  - This reflects a non-coercive approach to inner transformation
- **Wholeness as the Ground**
  - The session closes with the teaching that beneath all the layers of judgment and suffering, there is an inherent wholeness that is tender and kind
  - This wholeness is not something to be achieved but something to rest in
- **Community and Relational Support**
  - The session is framed as a shared practice, with the phrase "as we practice together" acknowledging the collective dimension of individual inner work

  - The concept of a workable level of charge is introduced as a safeguard
- **Resistance to the Practice**
  - Resistance to self-forgiveness is explicitly acknowledged as something that may arise during the session
  - Practitioners are guided to extend forgiveness to the resistance itself rather than treating it as a failure
- **Self-Judgment During Practice**
  - Judgment about how one is performing the self-forgiveness practice is named as a potential obstacle
  - The instruction to apply "forgiven, forgiven" to that judgment as well addresses this challenge directly
- **States of Numbness, Shame, and Grief**
  - Fear, judgment, shame, grief, and numbness are all named as states that may arise and are included within the scope of the forgiveness practice
- **Getting Stuck**
  - The possibility of getting stuck during the practice is acknowledged, and a specific phrase is offered as a resource for those moments
