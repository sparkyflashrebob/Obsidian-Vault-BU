### Outline
### The Universality of Betrayal as a Foundation for Forgiveness Teaching

- Framing forgiveness of others begins by reminding participants that betrayal is a universal human experience, with no one exempt from having been hurt by another.

- A shared human tenderness exists precisely because people betray one another both individually and collectively, and acknowledging this is part of understanding the human journey.

- This recognition leaves the heart tender and sad, yet also filled with a sense of wonder that people survive and move through such experiences.

- Albert Camus is quoted to illustrate that everyone carries within them places of exile, crimes, and ravages, and that the task is not to unleash these on the world but to transform them within oneself and others.

- The Bhagavad Gita is invoked to frame forgiveness as an act of heroism, stating that those who can return love for hatred and extend forgiveness are the truly brave.

### The Nobility and Courage Required to Forgive

- Jack Kornfield draws on his own experience with a volatile and abusive father to illustrate that forgiveness is a long and effortful process, not an immediate response.

- Witnessing Maha Ghosananda, known as the Gandhi of Cambodia, who lost nineteen family members to murder and led people back through the killing fields with chants of loving kindness, demonstrated that no matter how deep the betrayal, another possibility always exists.

- Maha Ghosananda's work showed that reclaiming land and reclaiming dignity in the heart are inseparable acts of forgiveness and courage.

- Sujata Baliga's story is presented as a powerful example of how deep personal suffering and hatred can be transformed through an encounter with teachings on compassion, specifically through her meeting with the Dalai Lama.

- Her subsequent work in restorative justice, moving from supporting victims to also working with perpetrators, illustrates the transformative potential of extending understanding beyond one's own pain.

- A colleague's experience entering a room with fourteen men convicted of sexual violence revealed that each of them had also been abused, many sexually, showing that suffering is passed down through generations in a chain rather than originating with a single individual.

- Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's observation is cited, noting that if one could read the secret history of enemies, enough sorrow and suffering would be found to disarm all hostility.

### Restorative Justice and Collective Forgiveness at a Societal Level

- Restorative justice is presented as a deeply inspiring model for addressing harm at both interpersonal and societal levels, with plans to explore it further in curriculum sections on conflict and conflict resolution.

- The South African Truth and Reconciliation process is offered as a model demonstrating that telling the truth about what really happened and allowing it to be fully received has a transformative effect, even if it does not resolve all problems.

- A national truth and reconciliation process is described as urgently needed in the United States to address the deep and unresolved legacy of slavery, native genocide, and ongoing systemic injustice.

- The proximity of the Civil War in generational terms is highlighted to underscore that these wounds are not distant history but living realities still shaping the present.

- Juneteenth is referenced to illustrate the scale of enslavement, noting that in Texas alone there were 250,000 enslaved people at the time of emancipation, representing more than a third of the state's population.

- Mass incarceration is identified as a continuation of slavery in another form, and the native genocide is described as a deliberate effort to erase millions of people, both representing unacknowledged collective harms.

- Conflicts in Cambodia, Palestine and Israel, Northern Ireland, Rwanda, and the former Yugoslavia are cited to show that people on opposing sides are often not fundamentally different, yet cycles of violence persist through inherited stories and histories.

- Communities in Rwanda and Cambodia are described as having had to live alongside those who killed their family members, demonstrating that forgiveness is not only noble but sometimes practically unavoidable.

- Without forgiveness, human beings remain chained to the past, perpetuating cycles of violence across generations until someone chooses to bear witness to suffering and declare that it stops with them.

### Key Principles for Teaching Forgiveness of Others

- Framing and explaining forgiveness correctly is identified as the essential first step in teaching it, with the most critical clarification being that forgiveness does not mean condoning what happened.

- Forgiveness is explicitly distinguished from forgetting, and participants are encouraged to affirm their determination to prevent the same suffering from recurring for themselves or others.

- The misconception that forgiveness is a form of weakness or indifference to harm is addressed directly, with the teaching emphasizing that it is neither passive nor an erasure of accountability.

- Understanding why forgiveness is valuable centers on its capacity to release the person forgiving from the grip of anger, hatred, hurt, and fear, shifting them from a reactive, fear-driven state to one of wisdom and compassion.

- Participants are invited to reflect on what it feels like in the body and nervous system to withhold forgiveness, making the cost of unforgiveness tangible and personal.

- Clarifying who forgiveness is for is presented as a third key framing element, with the teaching emphasizing that the person who caused harm may be entirely unaffected while the one holding resentment continues to suffer.

- Forgiveness does not require continued relationship with those who caused harm, particularly when safety is a concern, and protecting oneself remains a necessary and compatible part of the process.

- Abraham Lincoln's observation that mercy bears richer fruits than strict justice is cited to ground forgiveness not merely in legal or moral frameworks but in a vision of the kind of world one wishes to create.

### The Complexity of Forgiveness Within Ongoing Relationships

- Alvaro's story is used to illustrate that forgiveness does not require abandoning love, but it does require seeing people clearly and trusting them only to be who they are.

- Choosing not to allow those who have caused harm to colonize one's heart or life is presented as an act of self-reclamation, even when legal or protective measures are still necessary.

- A dialogue with Sri Nisargadatta Maharaj is recounted in which a man complaining about an unloving mother is told simply to love her anyway, and when he protests that she would not allow it, the response is that she could not stop him.

- This exchange illustrates the core teaching that the freedom to love and forgive is an inner capacity that cannot be taken away by external circumstances or the behavior of others.

### Forgiveness as a Practice and Training

- Forgiveness is described as a practice and a training, comparable to learning a sport or an instrument, requiring repetition and gradual development rather than a single decisive act.

- The Buddhist teachings of impermanence and emptiness are invoked to support the idea that renewal is always possible, with the body itself constantly regenerating as a natural metaphor for the heart's capacity to do the same.

- The traditional image of raindrops filling a water jar drop by drop is used to convey that consistent practice gradually fills the heart with clarity and peace.

- A description from a birding text is used as a metaphor for the delicate approach required in forgiveness practice, suggesting that one must begin gently, without forcing, and allow the process to unfold naturally.

- Beginning with what is easiest, rather than the most difficult hurt, is recommended as the entry point into forgiveness practice, mirroring the approach used in loving kindness meditation.

- Normalizing the difficulty of forgiveness is identified as an important part of teaching it, affirming that feeling anger, grief, and the sense that something is unforgivable are all part of the process rather than signs of failure.

- When full forgiveness feels impossible, the teaching suggests holding the inability to forgive itself with tenderness and compassion, using whatever small intention toward forgiveness is available.

- Alexander Solzhenitsyn's reflection that the line dividing good and evil runs through every human heart is cited to dissolve the illusion that harm is committed only by others, opening the door to genuine empathy and forgiveness.

### Pedagogical Sequencing in Teaching Forgiveness

- The pedagogy of forgiveness can be structured in distinct sections, including self-forgiveness, asking forgiveness from those one has harmed, and extending forgiveness to those who have caused harm.

- Beginning with asking forgiveness from others before moving to self-forgiveness or forgiving others is recommended because it opens participants to recognizing their own capacity for betrayal, which softens the heart toward those who have harmed them.

- Tara Brach shares the example of a woman who had just discovered her husband's long-term infidelity to illustrate that premature forgiveness can be counterproductive and that the first workable response is often simply to be with the anger and hurt.

- Many people report having forgiven something when in fact they have skipped the essential step of fully presencing with their own pain, resulting in a forgiveness that is not embodied or authentic.

- Returning to self-compassion and feeling held within one's own experience is identified as the necessary foundation before any genuine movement toward forgiving another becomes possible.

- A saying from an African play is cited, describing vengeance as a lazy form of grief, to illustrate that fixating outward on blame is a way of avoiding the inner work of being with one's own hurt.

- The first step in the forgiveness process is therefore described as making a U-turn inward, meeting the place of hurt with kindness before attempting to extend understanding outward.

- Deepening attention when locked in blame requires actively engaging with the other person's experience, a process referred to in psychodrama as role reversal, in which one genuinely senses what it is like to be the other.

- Knowing the personal circumstances of someone who caused harm, including their suffering and struggles, is described as making it very difficult to sustain hatred, illustrating how understanding dissolves the construction of an enemy.

- Truth and reconciliation processes at a community level are described as operating on the same principle as person-to-person forgiveness, both requiring the genuine question of what the experience is like for the other.

### Next Steps

- The pedagogy of forgiveness is to be taught in a sequenced manner, beginning with asking forgiveness, moving through self-forgiveness, and then extending forgiveness to others.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Framing Forgiveness for Others**
- Jack Kornfield opens by extending the teaching from self-forgiveness to forgiveness of others, including both asking for and extending forgiveness
- The first pedagogical step is reminding people of the nobility of forgiveness
- Everyone has been betrayed — there is no exemption from this human experience
- There is a shared human tenderness that comes from the fact that we betray one another individually and collectively
- This shared experience leaves the heart tender, sad, and yet in wonder that we survive
**Why Forgiveness Is Valuable**
- Forgiveness releases us and the world around us from the grip of anger, hatred, hurt, and fear
- It shifts us from the reptilian brain — the most primitive fear response — and allows access to wisdom and the wise heart
- It allows us to move from being in exile
- Participants are invited to reflect on what it is like to live without forgiving — in the body and nervous system
**What Forgiveness Is Not**
- Forgiveness does not mean condoning what happened
- It does not mean forgive and forget
- It is not a weakness or an ignoring of what happened
- One may still stand up and prevent the harm from continuing
- One may make the inner determination to do all in their power to prevent the suffering from recurring
**Who Forgiveness Is For**
- Forgiveness is primarily for oneself, not for the person who caused harm
- The person who harmed you may be entirely unaffected by your unforgiveness
- Forgiving someone does not require continuing a relationship with them, especially if that person is dangerous
- It is done for the sake of one's own heart, well-being, and freedom
**Pedagogy of Teaching Forgiveness**
- Frame and explain forgiveness clearly before leading practice
- Invoke the spirit of forgiveness through stories
- Describe it as a practice and training — like sport or music — done again and again
- Normalize the difficulty: it is not sentimental or weak
- Start where it is easy, not with the most difficult situation, just as in metta practice
- Build gradually, dozens of times
- When anger, grief, and hurt arise and forgiveness feels impossible, hold that inability with tenderness and whatever shred of forgiveness is available
- Pedagogy may tease apart self-forgiveness, asking forgiveness, and extending forgiveness as separate practices
- Starting with asking forgiveness from those we have harmed is recommended before moving to self-forgiveness or forgiving others, as it opens awareness that we too have betrayed others
**Premature Forgiveness — Tara Brach**
- Forgiveness is often not the first workable response
- Premature forgiveness skips necessary steps
- The first step is to be with the anger, hatred, and hurt
- One must find their way back to self-compassion before authentic forgiveness becomes possible
- Jumping too quickly to forgiveness skips the process of presencing with one's own heart
- True forgiveness must be embodied and authentic
**Vengeance and the U-Turn**
- Vengeance is described as a lazy form of grief — a fixation outward on fear or hurt
- The first step is to make a U-turn and be with the hurt place, holding it with kindness
- Only after this inner work can forgiveness be extended outward
**Seeing the Secret Suffering**
- A key process in deepening attention when locked in blame is to look more closely at what is happening for the other person
- In psychodrama this is called role reversal — sensing what it is like for the other
- This is one of the most powerful trainings offered: stepping out of the egocentric perspective
- Truth and reconciliation processes at a community-wide level mirror this person-to-person process of asking, "What is it like for you?"
**Restorative Justice**
- Jack Kornfield highlights restorative justice as an inspiring model
- Sujata Baliga, a Harvard-trained lawyer who had been sexually abused, encountered the Dalai Lama and redirected her work from prosecuting perpetrators to restorative justice
- She eventually worked with both victims and perpetrators
- A colleague with a similar history was directed to work with perpetrators — 14 men convicted of sexual violence — and came to see them as 14 boys who had all been abused themselves
- This revealed the chain of suffering passed from one generation to another
**Collective and National Forgiveness**
- Jack Kornfield calls for a national truth and reconciliation process in the United States, modeled on South Africa
- Telling the true story of what happened and letting it sink in is described as transformative
- The US is described as still fighting the Civil War due to deep cultural denial about the suffering caused by slavery
- Juneteenth is referenced — June 19th, when General Granger declared freedom for 250,000 enslaved people in Texas alone
- The history of lynching, apartheid segregation, and mass incarceration are named as continuations of this history
- Native genocide is also named as a deliberate historical act that contradicts American exceptionalism
- Examples of necessary coexistence and reconciliation are drawn from Cambodia, Rwanda, Northern Ireland, Palestine and Israel, and former Yugoslavia
- Without forgiveness, human beings are chained to the past and cycles of violence continue
**Renewal and Practice**
- Buddhist teachings on impermanence and emptiness support the idea that everything is in a process of re-creation
- The body renews itself constantly — 100 billion new red blood cells daily, skin particles, liver cells — and the heart can renew itself too
- The traditional image of raindrops filling a water jar drop by drop illustrates how repeated practice gradually fills the heart with clear water

### Stories
**Jack Kornfield's Father**
- Jack Kornfield shares his own experience of having a difficult, violent, and mentally ill father who was abusive, volatile, and a wife batterer
- It was a long process for Kornfield to see his father not only from a place of hurt and reactivity but as someone doing the best he could with who he was
- This shift came through years of practice and training
**Mahagosannanda — The Gandhi of Cambodia**
- Jack Kornfield describes spending time with Mahagosannanda, referred to as the Gandhi of Cambodia
- Mahagosannanda had 19 family members murdered and his temple and surrounding villages destroyed
- For 15 years he led people back through the killing fields and jungles, chanting loving kindness, so they could reclaim their land and their dignity
- This experience helped Kornfield realize that no matter how deep the betrayal, another possibility always exists
**Sujata Baliga — Restorative Justice**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield; subject is Sujata Baliga, a friend and colleague
- Baliga had been terribly and sexually abused as a young woman, went to Harvard, and intended to become a lawyer to prosecute perpetrators
- She was making herself sick with hatred and bitterness until an encounter with the Dalai Lama turned her life around
- She ultimately moved into restorative justice, working with both victims and perpetrators
- A long piece about her was written in the New York Times Magazine
**Colleague Working with Perpetrators**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield; subject is an unnamed woman colleague with a similar history to Baliga
- She had been working with victims for healing and was directed — as the Dalai Lama had suggested to Baliga — to work with perpetrators
- She entered a room with 14 men all convicted of some form of sexual violence toward women
- As she heard their stories, she came to see not 14 men who had done harm but 14 boys who had all been abused themselves, many sexually
- This revealed to her that it was not just personal but a chain of suffering passed from one generation to another
**Alvaro and His Siblings**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- When Alvaro's father died, two of his six siblings used lies and greed to try to gain control of the family construction company, nearly robbing Alvaro of his inheritance
- These were family members — parents of beloved nieces and nephews — with whom he had shared every family gathering
- After a year, Alvaro realized he could trust them only to be themselves and did not want them to colonize his heart or his life
- He instructed his lawyer to use every legal means to protect his inheritance but chose not to give up loving them
- By seeing them clearly he regained trust in himself and learned he could care for himself while still loving them in a new way
**Roberto DiVincenzo — The Golfer**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield; source is the book The Art of Forgiveness, Loving Kindness, and Peace
- Roberto DiVincenzo was a celebrated Argentine golfer, described as the Tiger Woods of golf in Latin America
- After winning a tournament, he was approached in the parking lot by a young woman who congratulated him and told him her child was seriously ill and near death
- Moved by her story, he endorsed his winning check over to her
- The following week a golf official informed him the woman was a fraud — not married, no sick child — she had deceived him
- DiVincenzo's response: "Why, that's the best news I've heard all week"
- DiVincenzo died in the past year prior to the session
**Tara Brach's Student — Betrayed Wife**
- Shared by Tara Brach
- A woman approached Tara after class having discovered her husband had been having affairs for a long time without telling her
- She was feeling hatred and anger and asked how to forgive him
- Tara's first response was: "Don't even try" — not yet
- The woman first needed to be with her anger and hatred and find her way back to self-compassion before authentic forgiveness could be possible
**Tara Brach — Tenant Rights Organizing**
- Shared by Tara Brach about her own experience in college
- She was involved in tenant rights organizing; a recurring problem was slumlords co-opting lead organizers with minor concessions
- A woman organizer Tara had grown close to was co-opted
- Because Tara knew so much about this woman's life — her son in jail, her husband in debt, her uterine cancer — she found she could not hold on to her anger and hatred when the betrayal happened
- Knowing the other person's suffering made it impossible to make her into an enemy
- Tara shares this story to illustrate the importance of truly knowing another person's suffering as part of the forgiveness process
**The Amish Community — Pennsylvania School Shooting**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- A man entered an Amish community school in Pennsylvania and shot a number of young girls; the perpetrator was killed
- Immediately, the Amish community reached out to the wife and family of the perpetrator, offered forgiveness, and brought the whole community together
- When a journalist asked whether they had held a meeting to decide on forgiveness, the Amish elder responded: "A meeting? This is who we are."
