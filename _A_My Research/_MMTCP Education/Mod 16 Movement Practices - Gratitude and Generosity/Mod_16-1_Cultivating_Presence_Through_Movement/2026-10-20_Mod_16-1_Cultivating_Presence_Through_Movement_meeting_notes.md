📅 October 20, 2026

### Outline
### Quality Over Quantity in Contemplative and Movement Practice

- Prioritizing the quality of practice over the duration is presented as a foundational principle, with even a small amount of mindful practice being more valuable than a longer, unfocused one.

- Creating a sense of sacred space during yoga, Qigong, or Tai Chi is encouraged as a way to establish a tone of strength and intentionality before practice begins.

- Having energy in practice is described not always as a matter of accumulating or appropriating energy, but often as a matter of releasing it.

- Clarity is identified as the primary goal of wisdom, and embodied practices are positioned as the means through which that clarity becomes accessible.

- Principles underlying movement arts such as Qigong, yoga, and Tai Chi are emphasized as more essential than the aesthetic or technical forms of the movements themselves.

- Simple gestures such as radiant body breathing or standing meditation are offered as sufficient vehicles for connecting with presence when practiced with relaxed intention.

### Presence Awareness as a Dual and Interconnected Quality

- Presence awareness is defined as having two distinct dimensions, the first being mindful, kind attention brought to the body and its activities.

- The second dimension is described as a non-dual field awareness, a quality of consciousness that extends beyond individual perception into a shared, living field.

- A framework of three truths is introduced, expanding on the traditional two truths of relative and absolute being to include a third truth of genuine interconnectedness with all of life.

- Compassionate awareness is described as a natural outflow of mindfulness practice, arising organically as one develops a felt sense of connection with other beings.

- Non-duality is explicitly clarified as not being the opposite of duality, but rather as something that includes and transcends it, offered as a contemplative inquiry for participants.

### Embodied Practice as a Gateway to Presence and Liberation

- Embodied movement practices are described as creating conditions in which the flow of life can be felt moving through the physical body.

- Mindfulness in the context of movement is characterized as holding space for experience to open naturally, rather than forcing or manufacturing a particular state.

- Resting in things exactly as they are, without needing to change or create anything, is presented as a genuinely liberating quality of embodied presence practice.

### Freshness as a Measure of Meditative Engagement

- Meditating only as long as the practice remains fresh in awareness is offered as a guiding principle for sustaining genuine quality of attention.

- Practitioners are given permission to release the expectation of fixed meditation durations such as 45 minutes or two hours, in favor of responsiveness to the living quality of the session.

- Time in contemplative practice is noted to expand and contract naturally, with even brief periods capable of feeling timeless or infinite.

- Neuroscience is referenced in support of the idea that practitioners develop increasing tolerance and capacity through consistent, quality-focused engagement over time.

### Grounded Expansive Awareness in Qigong and Embodied Arts

- Expansive awareness cultivated through Qigong is distinguished from spaced-out or unfocused awareness, being described instead as deeply grounded.

- Relaxed intention directing movement is reiterated as the core principle connecting embodied practice to presencing awareness.

- Inner listening and learning to trust oneself are identified as essential outcomes of sustained engagement with embodied and mindfulness arts.

### Two Modes of Knowing and the Role of Intuitive Intelligence

- At least two distinct ways of knowing are identified, the first being cognitive, reasoning-based knowledge developed through inquiry, training, and intellectual engagement.

- The second mode is described as a direct, intuitive knowing that arises from meditative and embodied practice and often does not present itself through words.

- Gut feeling is cited as a common cultural expression pointing toward this deeper, non-verbal intelligence that practitioners learn to trust and nourish.

- Trusting this natural intelligence both relationally and interiorly is described as something that strengthens and develops through consistent mindfulness practice.

- Embodiment arts are ultimately framed not as disciplines of form but as practices of heart and spirit that deepen trust in one's own being at both relative and absolute levels.

### The Interconnected Nature of Being and the Purpose of Mindfulness Training

- The question of identity is explored through the distinction between expressions of who one is, such as wisdom and compassion, and the deeper truth of being all of life.

- Interconnectedness is presented as an inclusive reality that encompasses all beings rather than referring only to individual personhood.

- Training as mindfulness, meditation, or dharma teachers is framed as participation in an evolutionary arc oriented toward healing and bringing wholeness to individuals and the planet.

- The responsibility that comes with access to these teachings is described as joyful rather than burdensome, reflecting the rare and recent availability of such integrated wisdom traditions.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to continue their personal embodied practices with an emphasis on quality and freshness of attention rather than fixed durations.

- Integrating the principle of relaxed intention directing movement into ongoing Qigong, yoga, or Tai Chi practice is recommended as a way to deepen presence awareness.

- Developing trust in intuitive, direct knowing alongside cognitive understanding is identified as a key orientation for practitioners moving forward in their training.

- Holding the three truths framework, relative being, absolute being, and interconnectedness, as a living contemplative inquiry is offered as an ongoing focus for participants.

### Stories
**The Tai Chi Teacher's Lesson**
- Shared by Teja Bell, referring to his Tai Chi teacher
- When students asked the teacher about how much practice was needed, the teacher responded that a little bit of bad Tai Chi is better than no Tai Chi
- Teja Bell describes learning something important that day: it is not about getting the time in but about the quality brought to the endeavor
**Lama Wangdor and the Freshness of Meditation**
- Shared by Teja Bell, referring to his meditation master Lama Wangdor, a Tibetan Lama
- Teja Bell was very enthusiastic about meditation and meditated a great deal
- Lama Wangdor told him he meditated too much
- When Teja Bell asked how long he should meditate, Lama Wangdor answered: as long as it stays fresh
- Teja Bell describes this as a very important lesson learned that day
**The Zen Master's Question**
- Shared by Teja Bell, referring to his Zen master
- Upon their first meeting, the Zen master leaned forward and asked, "Who are you?"
- Teja Bell responded with something along the lines of "I am wisdom and compassion"
- The Zen master replied that those are expressions of who you are, not who you are
- The Zen master then said, "You? Are all of life," meaning not the personal self but the inclusive, interconnected being
- Teja Bell notes the Zen master knew he was a martial artist, referencing the Rinzai tradition
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Distraction during concentration practice is acknowledged as a natural and common occurrence, illustrated with the example of being distracted by thoughts of Thanksgiving dinner
- The tendency to force oneself into long meditation periods such as 45 minutes, two hours, or even half an hour is identified as a challenge to be released
- Meditating too much without attention to freshness is noted as a pitfall, as shared through the story of Lama Wangdor's guidance to Teja Bell
- The distinction between expansive awareness and spaced-out awareness is raised as relevant to maintaining grounded presence during embodied practice
