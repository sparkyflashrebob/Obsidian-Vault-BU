### Outline
### Introduction to Seated Dao Yin Practice

- Seated Dao Yin is a form of Qi Gong rooted in contemplative traditions practiced over many centuries.

- Offered as both a complement and a legitimate alternative to standing Qi Gong, it is described as deeply beneficial for practitioners who may find standing forms inappropriate.

- Practitioners are encouraged to use whatever seated position suits them best, whether on a cushion or in a chair.

### Foundational Alignment Principles for Seated Practice

- Alignment begins with establishing a stable and settled base from the waist downward, in harmony with the natural flow of gravity toward the earth.

- The midriff band is kept open and relaxed, with a gentle sense of expansion or stretch in that area.

- Along the length of the spine, the upper body is held in a natural vertical alignment that is open, clear, and relaxed.

- Adjustments are encouraged so that each practitioner finds the alignment most appropriate for their individual body.

- A sense of natural lift and openness is cultivated, described as being like a flower turning toward the sun.

### Breath Awareness and Settling

- Natural abdominal breathing is introduced as the foundational breath practice, where the breath is not confined to the chest but allowed to settle into the abdominal field.

- With each in-breath, a natural expansion is felt in the abdomen, emphasizing relaxation and settling rather than volume or effort.

- Softening the jaw, relaxing the space around the eyes, and allowing the tongue to rest naturally against the upper palate are all described as supportive elements of proper alignment and ease.

- A lightly lifted sensation at the back of the head during relaxed jaw alignment is offered as an indicator of appropriate posture.

### First Dao Yin Movement - The Spinal Spiraling Staircase

- This movement involves a sequential rotation of the spine beginning at the base, progressing through the mid-section, and completing at the head and neck.

- The exhalation accompanies the spiral turn to one side, while the in-breath returns the practitioner to the centered neutral position before moving to the other side.

- One hand is drawn back with fingertips pointing toward the inner thigh, the shoulder relaxed, and the elbow pointing outward as the starting position for the movement.

- Practitioners are advised to work at approximately 70 percent of their range of motion, deliberately underdoing the movement rather than reaching maximum extension.

- Mindful presence and kindness of attention are emphasized over achieving a particular range of motion or physical outcome.

- The spiraling motion along the full length of the spine is described as a clearing, opening, and releasing action.

- A recommended repetition of four to six turns per side is suggested for those wishing to extend their practice.

### Second Dao Yin Movement - Opening and Closing the Door

- This movement is described as opening and closing the front and back doors of the heart field, engaging both the sternum area at the front and the space between the shoulder blades at the back.

- Releasing tension in the upper mid-back and lubricating the spine are identified as primary physical benefits of this practice.

- Hands are raised with the backs of the hands spiraling toward each other, the chin dropping slightly, and the back of the neck relaxing as the exhalation accompanies the folding inward.

- On the in-breath, the hands open to the sides with palms pressing lightly outward, expanding the front gateway of the heart field and opening the chest and sternum.

- Modifications are encouraged for practitioners who experience strain in the shoulders or back when raising the arms, with the instruction to adjust the height and range to suit individual needs.

- Practitioners are reminded to adjust the speed of the movement to match their own natural breath rhythm throughout the sequence.

- After completing the repetitions, hands are brought to rest in the practitioner's chosen meditation gesture, followed by a period of stillness to feel whatever arises in thought, feeling, or emotion without intervention.

### Third Dao Yin Movement - The Opening Gesture

- This movement is designed to open the interior of the body, particularly the abdominal field, while gently lengthening the spine and encouraging natural uprightness.

- Contribution to the health of the intervertebral spaces and the relatively unobstructed flow and circulation of chi through the body are described as key energetic and physical benefits.

- The sequence begins with an exhalation as the palms press inward at the level of the heart and lungs, followed by an in-breath as the hands rise into a U-shape with upright palms.

- Natural breathing is maintained in the raised position, with shoulder blades consciously relaxed downward and the sides of the body allowed to open.

- Fingers are then folded over thumbs into what is called a containment mudra, which is described as a gesture that holds the energy cultivated during the movement.

- Hands are brought to rest on the upper thighs with elbows lightly extended outward, and a gentle downward press accompanies the in-breath to support spinal lengthening.

- Ease and effortlessness are emphasized throughout, with practitioners reminded not to press or strain but to allow the gesture to work naturally.

- Three full repetitions of the opening gesture are guided, each followed by a period of natural breathing to feel the lengthening and internal spaciousness created.

### Closing Mindfulness Integration

- After completing the three Dao Yin movements, practitioners are guided to settle into stillness and simply feel the present moment without judgment, preference, or the need to intervene.

- Sensation, thought, and emotion are acknowledged as passing moment by moment, and practitioners are invited to meet whatever arises with kindness and equanimity.

- Taking time to settle and feel after any of the seated or standing practices is described as a valuable way to integrate the embodied benefits of mindfulness practice.

- Embracing the fullness of human life with compassion is offered as the closing intention of the session.

### Next Steps

- Practitioners are encouraged to use the three seated Dao Yin movements as a complete sequence or to select individual movements or pairs based on personal preference and need.

- Regular practice of these forms is recommended, including their use before, after, or during meditation sessions, as well as during everyday seated activities such as working at a computer.

- Extending the number of repetitions per side and taking additional time to settle after each movement are suggested as ways to deepen personal practice.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Seated Dao Yin — Overview and Context**
- Seated Dao Yin is a form of seated Qigong with roots in contemplative traditions spanning centuries
- It serves as both a complement and an alternative to standing Qigong
- Described as a legitimate and deeply beneficial form of practice in its own right
- Suitable for practitioners for whom standing practice is not appropriate
**Essential Alignment for Seated Practice**
- The base of the body — from the waist downward — should feel stable and settled in harmony with gravity
- The midriff or waist band should be open, relaxed, and may carry a sense of expansion or stretch
- The upper body along the spine should be open, clear, relaxed, and naturally vertically aligned
- The jaw should be relaxed; the space of the eyes soft, even when closed
- The back of the head lightly lifts when alignment is correct
- The tongue rests naturally in the mouth, likely touching the upper palate
- Alignment is described as being like a flower opening to the sun
**Natural Abdominal Breathing**
- Breath is not confined to the chest but settles into the abdominal field
- The in-breath produces a natural expansion in the abdomen
- The emphasis is on settling and dynamic relaxation, not on volume of breath
- Breath rhythm guides the pace of all movement sequences
**First Dao Yin — Spinal Spiraling Staircase**
- Moves rotation of the spine starting at the base, progressing to the middle, then to the head and neck
- Exhalation accompanies the turn; in-breath returns to center neutral
- One hand draws back with fingertips toward the inner thigh, shoulder relaxed, elbow pointing outward
- Practiced at approximately 70% of range of motion — underdoing is encouraged
- Described as a clearing, opening, and release along the full length of the spine
- Recommended repetitions: four to six times each side
**Second Dao Yin — Opening and Closing the Door**
- Opens and closes the front door of the heart field (sternum and chest) and the back door (space between shoulder blades)
- Hands spiral inward with backs of hands approaching each other; chin comes down slightly; back of neck relaxes
- Exhalation accompanies the folding inward; shoulder blades spread and round
- In-breath opens the hands to the sides with a light outward press, opening the front gateway of the heart
- Particularly beneficial for releasing tension in the upper mid-back
- Can be practiced before, after, or during a meditation session, and while working at a computer
- Described as contributing to lubrication of the spine
**Third Dao Yin — The Opening Gesture**
- Begins with an exhalation pressing palms inward at the heart and lung field
- In-breath lifts the hands upward into a U-shape, opening the body's channels
- Hands then move forward with fingers folded over thumbs into a containment mudra, which holds energy
- Hands settle onto the knees; elbows come out lightly; a gentle downward press on the upper thighs
- In-breath draws the hands back; natural breathing follows
- Contributes to lengthening the spine and health of the intervertebral space
- Encourages unobstructed flow and circulation of chi through the body
- Described as effortless — no pressing or efforting, only ease
**Settling and Integration**
- After each practice, time is taken to feel what is present in the body, breath, and awareness
- No particular state is prescribed — practitioners are encouraged to feel what is genuinely there
- Thoughts, feelings, and emotions are allowed to be present without intervention
- Settling after practice is described as a way to integrate the embodied value of mindfulness practice

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Physical Considerations and Modifications**
- Standing Qigong may not be appropriate for all practitioners; seated Dao Yin is offered as a full alternative
- Practitioners are consistently reminded to listen to their body first before any movement
- Range of motion is to be underdone — approximately 70% — to avoid strain
- Raising the hands high during Opening and Closing the Door may strain the shoulders or back; modification to a lower position is encouraged
- All forms are to be adjusted to what is appropriate for the individual's body and condition
- The Opening Gesture is stated to be appropriate regardless of the condition of the spine
**Tension and Release**
- Accumulation of tension in the upper mid-back is specifically identified as something the Opening and Closing the Door practice addresses
- The practice can be used during computer work or prolonged sitting to counteract tension buildup
**Mental and Emotional Dimensions**
- Practitioners are guided not to intervene with whatever arises in thought, feeling, or emotion during settling periods
- Judgment is identified as something to let go of during mindfulness moments
- Equanimity and kindness are presented as the appropriate mental orientation throughout practice
