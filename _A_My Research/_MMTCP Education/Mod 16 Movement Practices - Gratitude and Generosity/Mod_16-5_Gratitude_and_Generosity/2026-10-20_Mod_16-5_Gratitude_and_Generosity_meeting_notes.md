### Outline
### Overview of the Training Program's Purpose and Scope

- Foundational mindfulness teachings form the basis of the training, with recent sessions expanding into topics such as impermanence, grief, and conflict.

- Empowering participants to teach wisdom and compassion is a central goal, applicable across diverse settings including schools, clinics, and businesses.

- Beyond introductory classes, the program is designed to offer teachings that participants can draw upon and build on throughout their entire careers as teachers.

- A useful framing for the overall curriculum is "the life arts of happiness," drawing on modern neuroscience to explain what genuinely brings well-being.

- Shifting participants from a small, fear-based sense of self toward an experience of interbeing and interconnectedness with the world is identified as a core transformational aim.

- Gratitude and generosity are introduced as two additional natural dimensions of the awakened heart, complementing previously covered themes of loving kindness, joy, compassion, equanimity, and forgiveness.

### Gratitude as a Foundational Practice

- Gratitude is framed as beginning with a direct sense of the light and mystery of simply being alive and being supported by life in unseen ways.

- An E.E. Cummings poem, recited by an astronaut while orbiting the earth, is offered as an example of the perspective that allows one to recognize the extraordinary nature of existence.

- Teaching gratitude calls for a language of love, connectedness, appreciation, and abundance, rather than language that may trigger guilt or judgment.

- Neuroscience supports the value of gratitude, showing that living with it leads to better health, greater social connection, increased resilience, and reduced anxiety.

- A shift in consciousness from poverty mentality and victim mentality toward appreciation is described as moving "from whining to worship," without denying the reality of grief or injustice.

- Expressing appreciation directly to another person is highlighted as a simple but powerful act that builds a foundation of respect, care, and interconnectedness in relationships, workplaces, and communities.

- Gratitude is also presented as having a fierce dimension, in which difficulties, challenges, and hardships are recognized as the very experiences through which the heart grows wiser and more compassionate.

- Common barriers to gratitude, including trauma, poverty, unworthiness, and guilt, are acknowledged as real obstacles that practitioners and teachers must be willing to examine honestly.

- Traditional Buddhist morning gratitude prayers and Native American gratitude rituals are cited as examples of how gratitude practice appears across spiritual traditions and can serve as profound mindfulness exercises.

- A gratitude prayer from Jack Kornfield's book "The Art of Forgiveness, Loving Kindness and Peace" is offered as a practical teaching resource, expressing thanks for ancestors, the earth, health, service, and teachings received.

### Practical Gratitude Practices for Teachers and Students

- A weekly gratitude walk is suggested as a simple personal practice in which one moves through their environment and consciously notices things to be grateful for.

- Keeping a gratitude journal is recommended as a regular reflective practice to cultivate an ongoing orientation of appreciation.

- A gratitude jar placed in a classroom, from which entries are periodically read aloud, is offered as an accessible group practice.

- Sending a brief daily gratitude message to a colleague is described as a one-minute practice that uplifts the heart and strengthens professional relationships.

- Three reflective questions are proposed for deepening gratitude practice: what one is currently grateful for, what else one could be grateful for, and what opportunity life is presently offering for which one can be grateful.

- Teachers are encouraged to embody these practices personally before teaching them, as gratitude in particular cannot be authentically transmitted without direct lived experience.

### Generosity as an Expression of the Awakened Heart

- Generosity is presented as naturally connected to gratitude and as a direct expression of the awakened heart, practiced not out of obligation but for the joy it brings.

- Expanding one's circle of identification beyond the individual self is described as the foundation of generosity, with Mother Teresa's teaching that "you draw your family circle too small" offered as a guiding principle.

- Buddhist teachings are cited to convey the transformative power of generosity, suggesting that if one truly understood its liberating effect, no meal would pass without offering some to others.

- Children are described as naturally generous, with this impulse identified as an innate quality that education and culture can either nourish or suppress.

- Three levels of generosity are outlined in traditional teachings: tentative giving, brotherly or sisterly sharing, and royal giving, in which one offers the very best they have with delight in another's happiness.

- A Mediterranean communal wine-pouring tradition is used to illustrate how generosity creates community and communion even when no material gain is exchanged.

- Generosity is also framed as a practice of letting go, described as an art that human beings must learn and that brings transparency, freedom, and joy.

- The importance of pairing generosity with self-care is emphasized, noting that codependency and self-neglect are not expressions of true generosity and that being generous to oneself is not in contradiction with giving to others.

- Acting immediately on an impulse to give, rather than deliberating over it, is offered as a powerful personal practice that Jack Kornfield has maintained for twenty-five years without regret.

- Scarcity is identified as a lie, with the observation that global hunger persists not due to lack of resources but due to greed, fear, and the illusion of separation.

### The Role of Deliberate Training in Cultivating Gratitude and Generosity

- Both gratitude and generosity are described as natural expressions of a fully awake heart, arising spontaneously when one is not caught in habitual fear and grasping.

- Conditioning is identified as the primary force that blocks these natural impulses, which is why deliberate training through specific practices is necessary.

- Tara Brach introduces a guided reflection in which participants recall someone whose kindness they have felt, mentally whisper that person's name, and offer sincere thanks as a way of directly evoking gratitude.

- Meister Eckhart's teaching that "thank you" is a sufficient prayer for an entire lifetime is cited to underscore the depth and completeness of genuine gratitude.

- Moving from a momentary state of gratitude or generosity to an enduring trait requires not only experiencing the feeling but also fully embodying and expressing it, then taking time to register its goodness.

- Evolution and neuroscience are both cited as evidence that generosity produces positive affect in the brain, reinforcing the behavior and confirming that it aligns with one's deeper nature.

- The story of Tetsugen, a seventeenth-century Zen practitioner who twice gave away funds collected for a major publishing project in order to relieve famine and epidemic, is shared to illustrate that acts of generosity can surpass even great material achievements in their spiritual significance.

- An anonymous saying that "to be kind we have to swerve regularly from our path" is offered as a reminder that deliberate generosity requires flexibility and a willingness to step outside the habitual drives of daily conditioning.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to integrate gratitude and generosity practices into their own daily lives as a prerequisite for teaching them authentically to others.

- Including gratitude as a dimension woven into existing mindfulness and compassion curricula is identified as a meaningful way to enrich ongoing teaching work.

- Drawing on the handout materials from "The Art of Forgiveness, Loving Kindness and Peace" is recommended as a resource for structuring gratitude teachings.

- Experimenting with accessible classroom formats such as gratitude jars, gratitude walks, journaling, and partner appreciation practices is encouraged as a way to bring these teachings to diverse audiences.

### Stories
**Russian Astronauts Returning to Earth**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. Astronauts were stranded in a space station due to mechanical problems with the return capsule. After a long and uncertain wait, they returned safely and landed in Kazakhstan. Upon exiting the capsule, their overwhelming gratitude for being back on Earth — able to walk, breathe, and be among people — is used to illustrate the beginning of gratitude: sensing the light and mystery of being alive.
**Trudy in the Refugee Camps on the Darfur Border**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to his wife Trudy. Trudy traveled to refugee camps on the Chad-Darfur border with a group called IACT, an anti-genocide organization. Unlike most NGOs, they asked the women in the camps what they wanted. The women requested soccer for their children's joy and collaboration, and kindergarten so children could learn and women could forage. The opening of each new classroom tent was celebrated with dance, music, and ceremony by the women of the camp, expressing profound gratitude for a safe place for their children.
**The Russian Refugee at the American Supermarket**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. A friend who came to the United States from Russia in the early 1980s, before Glasnost and Perestroika, was taken to a local supermarket in San Francisco. Overwhelmed by the abundance of food from around the world, he was then taken to a second supermarket two blocks away and began to weep. The story illustrates how abundance surrounds us and how a shift in perspective can reveal it.
**Baldy the Cat in Watts**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, from a novel by Mary Gaitskill. A woman named Veronica, in a difficult neighborhood in Watts, decided to feed a stray cat known as Baldy. Men in the neighborhood who initially seemed skeptical gathered to watch. When the cat buried his head in the food with complete satisfaction, one of the men crouched down to hold the can so the cat could reach it better. Every day after that, the men gathered to watch Baldy eat. The story is described as one of the few times those men got to see a righteous need completely satisfied.
**Jack Kornfield's Daughter and the Homeless Children in Asia**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. During a sabbatical in Asia, his daughter, then six years old, encountered homeless children begging on the street. After being told they were homeless, her immediate response was: "We have money, let's get them a house." This is used to illustrate the natural generosity present in children.
**Iroquois Ritual of Generosity for Children**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, attributed to Iroquois tradition. A young child is brought into the center of a community circle, given something beautiful to drink, and then someone outside the circle cries out in thirst. The child is encouraged to carry the drink to that person. The ritual is repeated with food. This is presented as a model for teaching generosity from an early age.
**The Mediterranean Wine-Pouring Tradition**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. At a communal table along the Mediterranean, each person finds a modest bottle of wine before their plate. By tradition, each person pours wine not into their own glass but into their neighbor's, who returns the gesture. In economic terms nothing changes, but community and communion are created where there were none before.
**Tetsugen and the Three Sets of Sutras**
Shared by Tara Brach. Tetsugen was a devoted Zen practitioner in 17th-century Japan who set out to publish the sutras in Japanese, requiring 60,000 wood blocks. After ten years of collecting donations, a famine struck and he gave away all the funds to save people from starvation. He collected again, and then an epidemic spread and he gave away those funds as well. On his third round of collecting, after twenty years, he completed the printing blocks, which can still be seen in Japan today. The Japanese tell their children that Tetsugen made three sets of sutras, and that the first two invisible ones surpass even the last.
**Tara Brach's Son and the Chocolate Easter Bunny**
Shared by Tara Brach. Her son, at age four or five, had received a large chocolate bunny for Easter. When friends came over and wanted some, his first response was to refuse. Tara told him it was his rabbit and that was why he got to share it. After a moment of reflection, he lit up and went around sharing it with real delight and graciousness. The story illustrates how deliberate encouragement can activate the natural joy of generosity even when the limbic system initially grasps and fears.
**Woman in Mindfulness Class and the Homeless Man**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. In a class on social responsibility and mindfulness in Oakland, a woman shared that she had been giving money to a homeless man at the subway entrance every day for months but had never truly looked at him. She realized she had been afraid that if she looked in his eyes, she would not know how to make a loving connection while also taking care of herself. The story is used to illustrate the importance of pairing generosity with a beautiful intention and with self-awareness.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Trauma, poverty, unworthiness, and guilt are identified as common blocks to experiencing or expressing gratitude
- Loyalty to suffering is named as a pattern that keeps people from shifting into gratitude
- The fear of being too generous or losing oneself in another's need is raised through the story of the woman and the homeless man — specifically the fear of not knowing how to make a loving connection while maintaining self-care
- Codependency is cautioned against in the context of generosity; self-generosity is described as equally important and not in contradiction with generosity toward others
- The limbic system's tendency to grasp and fear is acknowledged as a natural obstacle to generosity, requiring deliberate training to overcome
- Grief, conflict, and injustice are acknowledged as real and not to be bypassed; gratitude practice is not presented as a denial of these realities
