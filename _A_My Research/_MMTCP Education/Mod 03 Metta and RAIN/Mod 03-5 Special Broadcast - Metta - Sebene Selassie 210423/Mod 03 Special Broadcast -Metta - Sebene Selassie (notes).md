### Mod 03 Special Broadcast – Metta – Sebene Selassie

#### Outline

#### Introduction to Metta and Its Relationship with Mindfulness

- Metta, or loving kindness, is described as an essential quality that co-arises with mindfulness, meaning the two cannot exist independently of one another.
- Mindfulness is defined as the capacity to witness experiences with clarity, while metta is the capacity to meet those experiences with kindness and care.
- Curiosity is identified as the most fundamental expression of mindfulness, while care is identified as the most fundamental expression of metta.
- Together, these two qualities are presented as foundational to creating a culture of belonging for students, communities, and the broader world.
- Metta is compared to the warmth of the sun, without which mindfulness functions only like a flashlight, providing light but unable to nourish or grow anything.

#### The Three Honorings as a Metta Practice

- Sebene Selassie opens teachings with three honorings, described as rememberings that help ground intentions and situate the teacher in right relationship with what is being offered.
- These three honorings cover native land, Asian lineages, and ancestors, and are presented as expressions of care for place, for the teachings, and for oneself and those gathered.
- The Pali word "sati," commonly translated as mindfulness, carries an etymology of remembering, which connects the act of honoring to the attunement of awareness in the present moment.
- Land acknowledgement is explained as a practice of recognizing that all gatherings occur on the ancestral lands of Indigenous peoples, wherever participants may be located.
- This practice is noted as being more established in settler colonial states such as Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, where it appears in schools, sports events, and political gatherings.
- Connecting to land is presented as a powerful practice found across wisdom traditions worldwide, relevant even in non-settler colonial contexts.
- Systems of oppression are described as fundamentally rooted in the exploitation of land, with land acknowledgement offered as a way to begin healing those historical harms.
- Honoring Asian lineages is presented as a way to acknowledge that modern mindfulness teachings originate in Buddhist traditions, particularly in the context of ongoing violence against Asian and Asian Pacific Islander communities.
- Excluding Asian and Asian American Buddhists from conversations about these teachings is identified by Taiwanese American scholar Funi Xu as a form of cultural appropriation, with lineage honoring offered as a form of repair.
- Sebene's own lineage is connected primarily to the Thai forest tradition, particularly through teachers who studied with Ajahn Chah.
- Honoring ancestors is described as a universal practice found across cultures worldwide, offering an opportunity to acknowledge that all people carry both the resilience and the suffering of those who came before them.

#### No Part Left Out as the Central Theme of Self-Metta

- The title of the talk, "No Part Left Out, The Metta of Belonging," is drawn from a poem by 10th-century Japanese poet Izumi Shikibu, in which the moon symbolizes illuminated awareness and wholeness.
- The poem reads "Watching the moon at dawn, solitary mid sky, I knew myself completely, no part left out," and is presented as a reflection on self-metta.
- Leaving parts of oneself out of practice, whether consciously or unconsciously, is identified as a barrier to fully seeing and caring for the corresponding parts of students.
- A "deep welcome" practice, attributed to co-teacher Brian Lesage, is introduced as an expression of metta that explicitly invites all parts of a person into a practice space, including sadness, anger, confusion, biases, regrets, and joy.
- Practitioners are invited to reflect on which parts of themselves they have not brought into their practice and to offer those parts a phrase of care such as "I welcome you" or "I care for you."
- Welcoming previously unacknowledged parts of oneself is described as deeply healing and as the place where metta and compassion meet.

#### Metta as the Foundation for Compassion

- Metta and compassion are distinguished as related but not identical expressions of an awakened heart, with metta serving as the foundation from which compassion, joy, and equanimity can arise.
- Many people come to practice because of suffering, and the ability to meet oneself with care and compassion is identified as a prerequisite for meeting the suffering of others.
- The RAIN practice, introduced by Tara Brach, is recommended as a powerful tool for cultivating self-compassion and is described as something that, with sustained practice, can become an automatic response to difficulty.
- Practicing metta toward oneself enables teachers to be more available and present for students who bring challenging or painful experiences to class.

#### Teaching from Scars Not Wounds

- Sharing personal experiences in teaching is identified as a powerful pedagogical tool for embodying metta, but a distinction is drawn between what is appropriate to share and what requires further personal healing.
- Lutheran minister Nadia Bolz-Weber's phrase "teach from scars, not wounds" is cited to illustrate that open or recently scabbed wounds are best tended privately, while healed scars can be offered as meaningful teaching material.
- Sebene's own cancer experience is given as an example of something that took years of practice, psychotherapy, somatic therapy, and body work before it could be shared authentically in a teaching context.
- Public speaking anxiety is offered as a further example of a wound that was transformed into a scar through sustained practice, self-kindness, and a growing understanding that the anxiety often stemmed from a genuine desire to offer something of value.
- Practitioners are invited to reflect on what might currently be a wound too fresh to share, as well as what might already be a scar that could benefit students by modeling self-metta.

#### Metta and the Shadow Self

- A caution is raised that metta can be misused as a form of spiritual bypassing, where phrases of loving kindness are deployed to keep suffering at a distance rather than to genuinely open to it.
- Metta is described as requiring intimacy, and its connection to mindfulness is emphasized as essential so that the clear light of awareness can illuminate hidden or frozen material for the warmth of metta to reach.
- The concept of the shadow is introduced to describe unconscious material, including unconscious biases related to race, gender identity, sexual orientation, and caste hierarchies, which exist across all cultures globally.
- These unconscious parts are described as not being one's fault but as being one's responsibility, requiring the same aware and loving attention as more consciously held experiences.
- Practitioners are reminded that because these biases are unconscious, they will not be aware of them until they are surfaced through practice or other work, and that curiosity and care are the appropriate responses when they are revealed.

#### Metta and Cancel Culture

- The term "cancel culture" is acknowledged as a loaded phrase that encompasses a wide range of phenomena, from legitimate accountability processes to more confused or harmful dynamics.
- Metta is presented as beginning with the self, particularly when one is experiencing the effects of being held accountable or witnessing others go through similar processes.
- Healthy boundaries around social media and news consumption are offered as a practical expression of self-care and metta, with a distinction drawn between being informed and being inundated.

#### Metta Versus Loving Kindness as Terminology

- The phrase "loving kindness" is identified as a Victorian-era translation that, like "equanimity," can feel clunky or unfamiliar to practitioners who have no prior context for it.
- Teachers are encouraged to find the words, phrases, or expressions that allow them to genuinely embody the practice rather than relying on terminology that does not resonate personally.
- For Sebene, metta is most meaningfully understood as "the heart that cares," a description that resonates in the body rather than functioning as a purely cognitive concept.

#### Accessing Metta When Love Feels Unavailable

- The classical metta practice is described as beginning with oneself, which is often the most uncomfortable starting point due to gender or cultural conditioning that orients care outward toward others.
- When love or care feels inaccessible, practitioners are encouraged to begin with the easiest available access point, such as a beloved pet, a single kind memory, or a trusted friend.
- Connection to nature, including the warmth of the sun or the experience of being in a natural environment, is offered as an alternative entry point when human connection feels difficult.
- Christy Peoples describes the experience of knowing love exists within but feeling it is locked away, and Sebene responds by affirming that this quality of care is actually the most natural human experience, often obscured by fatigue and the demands of daily life.
- Cultivating metta is compared to opening a treasure-filled box in an attic, requiring time, patience, and the willingness to step away from busyness in order to access what is already present.

#### Metta and Grief

- Grief is identified as a natural process that modern culture has largely disconnected from through the loss of traditional mourning rituals.
- A distinction is drawn between grief as an internal experience and mourning as the ritual process through which grief is expressed and released.
- Ethiopian wailing traditions are described as an example of culturally embedded mourning practices that provide a structured and communal container for moving through loss.
- The absence of such rituals in contemporary culture is identified as a significant gap, and practitioners are encouraged to find or create their own mourning processes to allow grief to move through rather than remain held.

#### Next Steps

- Further study of the three honorings is recommended through chapter six of Sebene Selassie's book "You Belong."
- Ongoing personal work with the RAIN practice is recommended as a way to deepen self-compassion over time.

### Detailed Subject Matter

**Metta and Mindfulness as Co-Arising Qualities**

- In classical teachings, mindfulness and metta always co-arise; one cannot exist without the other
- Mindfulness is the capacity to witness clearly; metta is the capacity to meet experience with kindness
- Mindfulness at its fundamental level is a quality of curiosity; metta at its fundamental level is a quality of care
- Metta is likened to the warmth of the sun; mindfulness alone is like a flashlight — it can illuminate but cannot nourish
- Together, mindfulness and metta create a culture of belonging for students and communities

**No Part Left Out — The Central Theme**

- Drawn from a poem by Izumi Shikibu, a 10th-century Japanese poet
- The moon in Japanese poetry symbolizes illuminated awareness and wholeness — nothing excluded
- If teachers leave parts of themselves out of practice, they unknowingly leave out parts of their students
- The theme applies both to practitioners and to teachers in distinct ways

**The Three Honorings**

- Sebene begins most teachings with three honorings as an expression of metta and right relationship
- Honoring Native Land: Land acknowledgement situates the teacher in place and connection to earth; Sebene acknowledges the unseated Territory of the Lenape peoples, known as Brooklyn, New York
- Honoring Asian Lineages: Acknowledges that mindfulness teachings originate in Buddhist traditions, particularly relevant amid ongoing violence toward Asian and Pacific Islander communities; references Taiwanese American scholar Funi Xu on cultural appropriation
- Honoring Ancestors: Every culture has ancestor relationship practices; ancestors survived suffering and resilience is inherited; imagining ancestors offering support is part of Sebene's metta practice

**Deep Welcome**

- Co-teacher Brian Lesage uses a "deep welcome" practice at the start of sessions
- A general "welcome" may not convey that all parts of a person are truly welcome
- The deep welcome explicitly names what might not feel welcome: sadness, anger, confusion, doubts, joy, passions, biases, prejudices, regrets, tears, laughter, triumphs
- Teachers cannot offer a deep welcome to others without first offering it to themselves

**Wounds vs. Scars in Teaching**

- "No part left out" does not mean displaying unresolved trauma in the teaching seat
- Reference to Lutheran minister Nadia Bolz-Weber: teach from scars, not wounds
- A wound that is recently scabbed or open should be tended privately; sharing it prematurely may harm both teacher and students
- Scars — healed experiences — are powerful teaching tools
- Sebene's cancer experience took years to integrate before she could speak about it in teaching
- Public speaking anxiety is offered as a less intense example of a wound becoming a scar over time

**Metta and Compassion**

- Metta and compassion are both expressions of an awakened heart but are not identical
- Metta is the foundation; compassion arises naturally from metta when suffering is encountered
- Many come to practice because of suffering; if teachers cannot meet themselves with care, it is hard to meet others
- The RAIN practice (developed by Tara Brach) is recommended as a tool for self-compassion

**Unconscious Bias and Shadow Work**

- Parts of the self that have been left out to the point of unconsciousness are called "the shadow"
- Includes unconscious racism, unconscious biases around sexual orientation, gender identity, and caste
- These conditioned patterns exist globally — colorism and systems of domination are worldwide
- These parts are not our fault but are our responsibility
- Mindfulness and metta together bring aware, loving attention to what is unconscious

**Metta and Cancel Culture**

- "Cancel culture" is a loaded phrase covering a spectrum from accountability to confused activity
- Metta begins with self: if one is being held accountable or witnessing others being held accountable, care and kindness toward self is the starting point
- Sebene recommends being boundaried around social media and news intake — distinguishing between being informed and being inundated

**Metta vs. Loving Kindness — Language and Translation**

- "Loving kindness" is a Victorian-era translation of the Pali word "metta"
- The translation, like "equanimity," can feel clunky or unfamiliar
- Practitioners are encouraged to find words or phrases that help them embody the practice
- For Sebene, metta is "the heart that cares" — an embodied, not merely cognitive, experience

**Accessing Love and Metta When It Feels Unavailable**

- The classical metta practice begins with self, which is often the most uncomfortable starting point
- Entry points include: imagining a pet, recalling a kind memory, connecting to nature, feeling the warmth of the sun
- Christy described the experience of love feeling like a locked box — accessible but hard to reach
- Sebene responded with the importance of faith and trust, patience, and carving out ease rather than pressure in practice
- What we pay attention to grows

**Grief and Mourning**

- Modern culture is largely disconnected from rituals of mourning
- Grief is natural and can be moved through; mourning rituals facilitate that movement
- Sebene witnessed this through traditional Ethiopian mourning practices after her mother's death — including communal wailing
- In Ethiopia and many cultures, wailing is a performative offering; mourners can even be hired
- Without rituals of mourning, people can become stuck in grief
- Ambiguous grief was raised by a participant; Sebene acknowledged grief as an underexplored area she wishes to develop further

### Spiritual and Societal Insights

**Metta as a Societal Healing Force**

- Metta is not a passive or weak quality; it can be strong or fierce when that is what is most kind
- The cultivation of metta and mindfulness together is essential for creating cultures of belonging in communities and in the world
- Land acknowledgement is connected to healing the exploitation of earth and the harms of colonization, slavery, and extractive capitalism
- Honoring Asian lineages is an act of repair for the extractive relationship many modern practitioners have with Buddhist teachings
- Ancestor honoring reconnects practitioners to resilience, cultural heritage, and the totality of ancestral experience — including both suffering caused and suffered

**Metta as a Practice of Inclusion**

- Metta practice requires bringing the full self — including shadow, bias, and unconscious material — into awareness
- Leaving parts of the self out of practice means those parts will likely be left out when caring for students
- The deep welcome is a societal as well as personal practice: it names what culture often excludes — anger, confusion, prejudice, regret

**Mindfulness and Metta in the Context of Systemic Harm**

- Systems of oppression are fundamentally about the exploitation of land and people
- Africans were enslaved expressly to exploit land; colonization and slavery are about theft of land and resources
- Extractive capitalism continues to poison the planet
- Colorism and racial and caste hierarchies exist globally, not only in North America
- Unconscious biases shaped by culture can lead to harm in communities and in the world; they require the light of mindfulness and the warmth of metta to be seen and released

**Faith, Trust, and the Long Arc of Practice**

- Faith — or trust — is an underemphasized quality in modern secular mindfulness spaces
- Cultivating metta requires patience, energy, determination, and the other paramis (perfections)
- The qualities of care and love are described as the most natural experiences, yet fatigue and the demands of modern life obscure them
- Stepping away from busyness and giving oneself time — in nature, in connection, in ease — is itself a metta practice

**Grief as a Spiritual and Cultural Practice**

- Grief is a natural process; mourning rituals are the spiritual and cultural technology for moving through it
- The disconnection of modern culture from these rituals is a form of collective loss
- Reconnecting to cultural or newly created mourning rituals is an act of care for self and community

### Stories

**The RAIN Practice Becoming Automatic**

- Shared by Sebene Selassie about her own experience
- After years of formally practicing RAIN on retreats and in classes, first learned from Tara Brach, Sebene was riding her bike in Brooklyn on the way to lead a sit
- She was upset about a difficulty with a friend and pulled over at the corner of Bergen and Smith streets
- She stood on the sidewalk, felt what was happening, and after a few moments realized she was spontaneously doing RAIN without going through formal steps
- She was so pleased she temporarily forgot about the original problem

**Missing the Interview with Tara on Retreat**

- Shared by Sebene Selassie about herself
- On retreat at Seven Oaks during an IMCW retreat, Sebene mixed up the interview schedule and missed her meeting with Tara Brach
- She went into a shame spiral; retreat heightened her sensitivities
- Tara came up to her in the food line, recognized what was happening, and said "Don't blame yourself. Don't go into shame spirals."
- Sebene reflected that Tara could offer that kindness because she had likely developed care toward herself through her own experience of shame spirals

**Cancer Scars and Teaching**

- Shared by Sebene Selassie about herself
- Sebene has many scars on her body from cancer surgeries and treatments
- She used to see them as imperfections or blemishes
- It took years after finishing treatment — processing physical and emotional trauma through practice, psychotherapy, body work, and somatic therapies — before she could speak about her cancer experience in teaching
- She now honors her scars as powerful experiences that allow her to bring care and compassion to her teaching

**Public Speaking Anxiety**

- Shared by Sebene Selassie about herself
- Sebene was terrified of public speaking, which she noted was not a great quality for a Dharma teacher
- There were times when introducing teachers or giving openings she performed so poorly that people came up to commiserate afterward
- She worked with the anxiety by bringing kindness and compassion to it, taking deep breaths, honoring what she felt, and learning about the anxiety
- She came to understand it was often rooted in wanting to do well and offer something of value
- Caring for it did not make it disappear but allowed it to become a scar rather than a wound

**Ethiopian Mourning Rituals After Her Mother's Death**

- Shared by Sebene Selassie about her own experience and her family
- When Sebene's mother died, she was fortunate to have mourning rituals connected to traditional Ethiopian culture
- Through her aunties and relatives, she was able to enter processes and spaces that were powerful and helpful for moving grief
- Ethiopian culture includes a practice of communal wailing; mourners can even be hired in many places around the world
- Her mother's best friend, who lives in the Bronx, would open the door and immediately begin wailing each time Sebene visited for the year after her mother's death
- This would allow Sebene to go into crying and release, followed by sitting, talking, laughing, and telling stories about her mother
- Sebene described this as a beautiful process she was gifted by her culture

**The Locked Box — Christy's Personal Reflection**

- Shared by Christy Peoples about her own experience
- Christy described knowing that love is present within her but experiencing it as if it were in a locked box, hard to access
- She noted that practices of being in nature and feeling love through the natural world have helped her move toward that reservoir
- She asked how metta practice might help unlock that box or tap that reservoir
