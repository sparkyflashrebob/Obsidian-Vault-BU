### Mod 03 — Metta: The Practice of Lovingkindness

📅 October 16, 2026

👥 Main Teacher: Jack Kornfield

Tags: Metta, Lovingkindness, Compassion, Self-Love, Spiritual Practice, Buddhism, Mindfulness, Interconnectedness, Grief, Fear, Self-Judgment, Healing, Teaching Practice

#### Outline

#### Introduction to Metta Through Poetry and Storytelling

- Evoked through Ellen Bass's poem set at Portland airport gate C22, the practice of metta is introduced as something already known within the human heart.
- Described in the poem is a middle-aged couple kissing with profound tenderness long after other passengers have departed, drawing the attention of everyone in the terminal.
- The poem culminates in a reflection on how every person was once gazed upon with unconditional wonder at birth, regardless of what followed in life.
- Stories and poems are identified as carriers of something larger than their literal content, serving as vehicles to awaken the longing for love that exists beneath everyday awareness.

#### Why Metta Matters as a Foundational Practice

- Loving-kindness is presented not as an optional spiritual add-on but as the deepest expression of human nature, rooted in the understanding that consciousness and love are inseparable.
- Archbishop Tutu's observation about African communal language is cited to illustrate that the isolated individual is a fiction, and that human beings are fundamentally interconnected.
- Jack Kornfield's teacher Ajahn Chah is quoted as saying that the Dharma is the heart, pointing to love as the central truth of all spiritual teaching.
- Spiritual life is reframed around the idea that the goal is not to perfect oneself but to perfect one's love.

#### The Nature of Love as Strength Rather Than Weakness

- Love is frequently misunderstood in contemporary culture as weakness, vulnerability, or a loss of control, making it especially challenging to teach.
- Martin Luther King Jr. is cited as an example of love expressed as soul force, meeting physical violence not with hatred but with the capacity to endure suffering and appeal to conscience.
- The power of love is illustrated through examples such as mothers lifting cars off children, demonstrating that love can be a source of extraordinary strength.
- Winning freedom through love is described as a process that liberates both the oppressed and the oppressor by appealing to the heart and conscience.

#### Self-Love and the Difficulty of Receiving Love

- Sharon Salzberg's book Real Love is referenced to highlight that self-love is widely prescribed but rarely explained in practical terms.
- A personal account from a woman named Nora describes the moment she realized she had never considered herself lovable and therefore could not truly receive love from others.
- Growing in love is described as a process of returning home to one's true nature rather than acquiring something foreign or new.
- Self-love is identified as among the most difficult forms of love to cultivate because the self is the most convenient target for one's own judgment and hatred.

#### The Eleven Blessings of Loving-Kindness Practice

- A traditional Buddhist list of eleven blessings arising from the practice of loving-kindness is introduced as a way to set the stage and generate interest before formal instruction.
- Among the blessings described are sweeter dreams, easier sleep, improved health, protection by angels and devas, and being welcomed everywhere one goes.
- Additional blessings include a sweeter voice, more pleasant thoughts, animals sensing one's loving presence, and a serene countenance.
- Presenting these blessings is noted as an effective way to engage even intellectually oriented students by pointing to something nourishing and desirable in the practice.

#### Setting the Stage for Teaching Metta

- Beginning a metta teaching session by evoking the spirit of love through poems, stories, or reflections is identified as an essential pedagogical skill.
- Teachers are encouraged to collect their own poems, stories, and readings that genuinely open and touch the heart, rather than relying solely on borrowed material.
- Richard Selzer's story "The Kiss" is read aloud, depicting a surgeon who witnesses a young husband twist his own lips to accommodate the crooked mouth of his post-operative wife, recognizing in that moment the presence of something divine.
- The loving heart is described as uniquely capable of meeting the full measure of human sorrow and beauty in a way that no other approach can replicate.

#### Blocks to Metta Practice and How to Work with Them

- One of the most common obstacles is the practice feeling mechanical or dry, with no emotional resonance arising despite repeated recitation of loving-kindness phrases.
- Sharon Salzberg's experience of accidentally calling herself a klutz and then adding "but I love you" is shared as an illustration that the practice is working even when it feels rote, because seeds are being planted.
- Grief is identified as a significant block, as attempting to send love to someone in deep pain can feel superficial, requiring a shift from loving-kindness phrases to compassion-oriented language such as "may your pain and sorrow be eased."
- Compassion is defined as what love becomes when it encounters pain and sorrow, described as a quivering of the heart that says "yes, I am touched by this."
- When the practice feels dry and numb, the recommended approach is to turn loving-kindness toward the very experience of dryness itself rather than trying to fix or escape it.
- Frank Ostaseski's account of a hospice patient with terminal stomach cancer illustrates how backing off, creating space, and allowing the person to rest in love can ease suffering when direct engagement is too intense.
- Self-judgment and shame are named as pervasive blocks, with Oscar Wilde quoted to the effect that it is the imperfect, not the perfect, who are most in need of love.
- A guided technique for working with shame involves asking participants to identify the age at which they first felt unworthy, then visualize holding that inner child with tenderness and asking permission before approaching.
- Expectations about how metta should feel are identified as another obstacle, with the heart's natural opening and closing compared to the seasonal rhythms of flowers, tides, and the phases of the moon.
- Fear of vulnerability, loss of self, or loss of strength is noted as a block, addressed by emphasizing that a complete circle of loving-kindness must include oneself and that love can be fierce and intelligent rather than passive.

#### Practical Guidance for Teaching Metta

- Starting the practice with whoever is most easily loved, whether a person or an animal, is recommended as a way to access the natural feeling of metta before expanding outward.
- After opening loving-kindness toward a beloved, participants are invited to imagine that person gazing back at them and wishing them the same wellbeing in return.
- Placing a hand on the heart is offered as an optional gesture to deepen the felt sense of the practice, particularly when compassion phrases are added.
- Over time the circle of metta is expanded from benefactors and loved ones to friends, family, community, the globe, all beings, and eventually difficult people.
- For challenging figures such as dictators, phrases like "may you be free from hatred, may you find peace" are suggested as a way to genuinely wish for conditions that would reduce harm to others.
- Metta can also be practiced informally in everyday settings such as airports, buses, or airplanes by silently wishing wellbeing to those nearby for just a few minutes.
- Multiple metta scripts are noted as available in the course handouts, and participants are encouraged to find phrases that speak from their own heart and to invite students to customize their own.

#### The Power of Love in the Face of Collective Suffering

- Maha Gosananda's actions in the Cambodian refugee camps following the Khmer Rouge genocide are described as a demonstration of love's capacity to speak a truth larger than collective sorrow.
- Despite Khmer Rouge threats that anyone entering the makeshift temple would be killed upon return to Cambodia, 25,000 refugees streamed into the central square to sit together.
- Chanting the verse "hatred never ceases by hatred, but by love alone is healed," Gosananda led the gathered crowd in a collective recitation that moved people to tears after years of silence.
- Love is characterized not as a small or sentimental thing but as an act of bravery with enormous power to shift the conditions of human life.

#### Next Steps

- Trauma-informed approaches to teaching will be addressed in later modules, building on the understanding of how suffering and love intersect.
- Participants are invited to experiment with informal metta practice in daily life settings as a way of integrating the teaching beyond formal seated sessions.

### Detailed Subject Matter

**Why Teach Metta**

- Metta is taught because it reflects the true nature of human beings — consciousness and love itself
- Human beings are fundamentally interconnected; the isolated individual is described as a fiction
- Archbishop Tutu's example: in Africa, even one person replies "We are well," because individual wellbeing is inseparable from communal wellbeing
- Ajahn Chah, when asked "What is the Dharma?" replied: "The Dharma is the heart"
- The aim of spiritual life is not to perfect oneself but to perfect one's love

**Setting the Stage for Teaching Metta**

- Teachers are encouraged to evoke the spirit of Metta through poems, stories, and personal accounts before formal instruction
- Teachers should collect their own stories and poems that open and touch the heart
- Evoking Metta creates a resonance that allows students to "draft behind" the teacher

**The 11 Blessings of Lovingkindness**

- Sweeter dreams and easier sleep, waking contented
- Improved health and physical wellbeing
- Protection and love from angels and devas
- Being loved by men and women
- Weapons and poisons will not harm you
- Lost things will be returned
- Being welcomed everywhere
- More pleasant thoughts
- Animals sensing and loving you
- A serene countenance
- Seeing Buddha nature in every being encountered

**How to Teach Metta — Practical Guidance**

- Begin with whoever is most easily loved — one or two people, or even a pet
- After opening Metta toward a beloved, invite the student to imagine that person gazing back with the same wishes
- Invite students to place a hand on their heart if comfortable
- Phrases are an intention; students should find phrases that speak from their own heart and may customize them
- Multiple Metta scripts are provided in handouts, including scripts by Tara and others
- Ensure students sit comfortably before beginning
- Expand the circle gradually: from benefactors and loved ones, to self, to friends, community, the globe, all beings, and eventually difficult people
- For difficult people such as cruel leaders: phrases like "May you be free from hatred, may you be free from greed, may you find peace" are offered
- Metta can be practiced informally — on planes, buses, in airports — for two to three minutes at a time

**Blocks and Barriers to Metta**

- Mechanical feeling and lack of resonance: practice can feel dry and nothing seems to happen; the instruction is to love the dryness itself, bringing lovingkindness to the exact experience of aridness
- Grief and sorrow: when grief arises, switching from Metta to compassion practice is recommended; compassion phrases such as "May you be held in compassion, may your pain and sorrow be eased, may your heart be at peace" are used
- Dryness and numbness: rather than trying to fix or change the experience, bring lovingkindness to it as it is
- Self-judgment and shame: students are guided to identify the age at which they first felt the self-judgment, visualize themselves as that child, and direct compassion toward that inner child; using a childhood photograph as a reminder is suggested
- Expectations: wanting specific emotional experiences such as blissful or gushing feelings; the heart naturally opens and closes in cycles, like flowers or tides, and this is to be accepted
- Fear of vulnerability: fear of losing oneself, one's ground, or one's strength; love is reframed as strong and fierce, not weak
- The circle of Metta is incomplete if the practitioner is excluded from it; self-love is essential to prevent compassion from collapsing into co-dependence

**Compassion as Distinct from Metta**

- Compassion is described as what happens to love when it meets pain and sorrow
- It is the quivering of the heart in response to suffering
- There are different times to move between lovingkindness and compassion depending on what arises

**Love as Strength and Social Force**

- Martin Luther King after his church was bombed: meeting physical force with soul force and love, refusing to hate, refusing to obey unjust laws, and appealing to the heart and conscience of opponents
- Napoleon's observation: the sword is always beaten by the spirit
- Love is described as an act of bravery with enormous power

### Spiritual and Societal Insights

**Love as True Nature**

- The teaching holds that love and consciousness are the fundamental nature of human beings, not something to be acquired but something to be returned to
- The phrase "returning home" is used to describe growing in love

**Interconnectedness**

- The African communal response "We are well" illustrates that no human being exists in isolation
- The solitary, isolated human being is described as a fiction
- This interconnectedness is presented as the foundation for why Metta practice is both natural and necessary

**Self-Love as the Most Difficult Practice**

- Self-love is identified as harder than loving others because the self is the most convenient person to hate
- Many people have never considered themselves lovable and therefore cannot fully receive love from others
- The inner child visualization is offered as a bridge to self-compassion for those who find direct self-love inaccessible

**The Heart's Cycles**

- The heart opens and closes naturally, like poppies closing at dusk or tides moving in and out
- The goal is not to maintain a permanently open heart but to love both the opening and the closing

**Love in the Face of Collective Trauma**

- Maha Gosananda's work in Cambodian refugee camps demonstrates love as a force that speaks a truth larger than collective sorrow
- The chant "Hatred never ceases by hatred, but by love alone is healed" was offered to 25,000 refugees who had survived the Cambodian Holocaust
- The story illustrates that love and Metta are not passive or weak but can meet the full measure of human suffering

**Love and Justice**

- Martin Luther King's use of "the weapons of love" is highlighted as a deliberate and powerful framing
- Love that says no to injustice, no to self-harm, and no to harm of others is presented as among the most powerful expressions of love
- Rosa Saldana's declaration that her community would proudly call itself the Wetback Church illustrates taking all of humanity into the heart, including the despised and rejected

**Planting Seeds**

- The practice of Metta is compared to water drops wearing away stone or raindrops filling a water jar — gradual, cumulative, and transformative
- The Rabbi's teaching: words are placed on the heart so that when the heart breaks, they fall in

### Stories

**The Airport Kiss — Ellen Bass poem**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield as an opening evocation of Metta

**Jillian and Her Mother with Dementia — Frank Ostaseski, The Five Invitations**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, sourced from Frank Ostaseski, founder of Zen Center Hospice
- Jillian, who worked in publishing, brought her mother with dementia home; the mother scattered Jillian's beloved books including Buddhist texts across the floor, announcing she would give them to her dentist; the attendant responded with equanimity, saying she would pack and unpack the books daily if it gave the mother a sense of control, and that she simply loved being with her

**The Kiss — Richard Selzer, surgeon and essayist at Yale University**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, sourced from Richard Selzer
- A surgeon describes standing beside a young woman whose facial nerve was severed during tumor removal, leaving her mouth twisted; her young husband says he likes it and calls it cute, then bends to kiss her crooked mouth, twisting his own lips to meet hers; the surgeon reflects on encountering the gods in mortal form

**Jack Kornfield's Brother at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield about his own experience
- Kornfield spent many weeks at Dana-Farber near Harvard Medical School with his brother during a long illness before his death; he observed families, nurses, respiratory therapists, and attendants all gathered in care, describing the hospital as a temple of love despite being a place of suffering

**Sharon Salzberg Dropping the Tray**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, sourced from Sharon Salzberg's book Real Love
- During an intensive Metta retreat, Salzberg found the practice mechanical; on her way downstairs she dropped a tray, shattering glass, and instinctively said to herself "Sharon, you're such a klutz — but I love you," then recognized the practice was working

**Jack Kornfield's Friend Who Died After Surgery**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield about his own experience
- A close friend had minor surgery, came home, and died in her sleep three days later; her husband, who shares Kornfield's birthday and birth year, was devastated; Kornfield found he could not practice Metta for his friend because the grief was too raw, and instead shifted to compassion practice

**The Man with Terminal Stomach Cancer — Frank Ostaseski, Zen Center Hospice**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, sourced from Frank Ostaseski
- A man with terminal stomach cancer was in severe pain; attempts to open to the sensations directly were too intense; Frank placed his hand near the man's feet, then a foot or two away from the body, and the man relaxed; from that place the man said "rest in love" and thereafter used that phrase alongside his morphine pump when pain arose

**Father Greg Boyle and the Wetback Church — Homeboy Industries, Los Angeles**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield, sourced from Father Greg Boyle, author of Tattoos on the Heart
- Boyle arrived at a church to find it spray-painted with the slur "wetback church"; he apologized to the congregation and offered to have gang youth remove the graffiti; Rosa Saldana, a small woman who rarely spoke, stood and declared that the graffiti should not be removed, and that if people were cast out and despised for being mojados, they would proudly call themselves the Wetback Church

**Maha Gosananda in the Cambodian Refugee Camps**

- Shared by Jack Kornfield about his own experience alongside Maha Gosananda
- After the Cambodian Holocaust in which nearly a quarter of the population was killed by the Khmer Rouge, Kornfield and Maha Gosananda worked in UN refugee camps on the Thai-Cambodian border; Gosananda requested permission to open a Buddhist temple in the camp; the Khmer Rouge underground threatened death to anyone who attended; 25,000 people came anyway; Gosananda chanted "Hatred never ceases by hatred, but by love alone is healed" and the crowd wept and joined in, having not heard these words since the temples were burned

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice

- Metta practice can feel mechanical and dry, with nothing seeming to happen, described as being lost in a desert without water
- Grief and sorrow can arise during Metta and make the practice feel superficial or impossible; switching to compassion practice is recommended in these moments
- Self-judgment and shame are identified as significant barriers, often rooted in early childhood experiences
- Expectations of blissful or emotionally rich experiences can block engagement with the practice
- Fear of vulnerability, loss of self, loss of ground, or loss of strength can prevent practitioners from opening to love
- The heart naturally opens and closes in cycles; practitioners may experience periods where the heart feels closed like a stone
- For people with deep childhood injury, even directing compassion toward the inner child may require gentle permission-seeking before proceeding
- Physical discomfort during seated practice is noted as a barrier; practitioners are advised to ensure bodily comfort before beginning
- Intense physical pain, as in the case of the hospice patient with terminal stomach cancer, can make direct attention to sensation overwhelming; creating more space and distance is offered as a skillful response
