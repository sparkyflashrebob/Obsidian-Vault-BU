# MMTCP Mod 03 - Self-compassion and RAIN JK-TB - Oct, 15

### Self-compassion and RAIN
📅 October 15, 2026
👥 Main Teachers: Tara Brach, Jack Kornfield
### Outline
### The Second Arrow and the Pervasiveness of Unworthiness

- Difficult emotions are often accompanied by a secondary layer of self-judgment, referred to in Buddhist tradition as the "second arrow," where individuals feel bad about themselves for experiencing the original emotion.

- Feeling bad about an emotion can collapse into a deeper belief of "I am bad," creating a compounded layer of suffering that operates below conscious awareness.

- This sense of unworthiness is described as highly pervasive among students and clients, making it a critical layer for teachers and therapists to recognize and address.

- A dying woman's final words — "all my life I thought something was wrong with me" — are cited as a powerful illustration of how the trance of unworthiness can persist across an entire lifetime.

- Research from a palliative caregiver who sat with over 10,000 dying people revealed that the greatest regret of the dying was not living true to themselves, but instead living according to others' expectations or limited self-concepts.

### The Two Wings of Awareness in Mindful Presence

- Mindful presence is described through the metaphor of two wings, the first being mindfulness, which recognizes what is happening, and the second being compassion, which begins with allowing.

- Recognition, when deepened through sustained attention, gradually unfurls into wisdom, while allowing, when cultivated intentionally, opens into compassion.

- When individuals are stuck in difficult emotions, the wing of compassion does not arise spontaneously but must be deliberately and incrementally cultivated.

- The arc of transformation in both therapy and meditation teaching is consistently marked by a felt sense of kindness or tenderness toward one's own pain, which Tara Brach identifies as the pivotal juncture of healing and awakening.

- Compassion for the life that is immediately present is described as the heart of Buddhist practice, emphasizing that transformation begins with turning toward one's own experience rather than away from it.

### The Hand Model of the Brain and Emotional Reactivity

- Dan Siegel's hand model of the brain is introduced as a practical teaching tool to help students understand what happens neurologically when they become emotionally triggered.

- In the model, the wrist and lower palm represent the brain stem, the thumb folded into the palm represents the limbic system, and the four fingers curled over represent the cortex, with the fingertips specifically representing the prefrontal cortex.

- The prefrontal cortex is responsible for higher functions such as reasoning, empathy, and mindfulness, and it plays a key role in down-regulating the limbic system's fight-flight-freeze responses.

- When the brain is integrated, incoming stressors can be processed with perspective and equanimity, but under conditions of chronic stress or a deep sense of unworthiness, the prefrontal cortex becomes disconnected, resulting in what is called "flipping the lid."

- Flipping the lid describes a state in which a person loses access to empathy, humor, moral reasoning, and perspective, and may act in ways they would not otherwise choose.

- Simply recognizing that one has flipped their lid, even through the use of this metaphor, begins the process of reintegrating the brain.

- A vicious cycle is described in which a core belief of being bad or unlovable increases the likelihood of flipping the lid, and subsequent reactive behavior then reinforces and deepens that belief.

### Moving from Fight-Flight-Freeze to Attend and Befriend

- The central inquiry of the session is how to move from a state of disconnected reactivity, characterized by fight, flight, or freeze, to a more integrated quality of presence described as "attend and befriend."

- The Buddhist story of the Buddha and Mara is used to illustrate this shift, in which the Buddha responds to the presence of Mara, the embodiment of greed, hatred, and delusion, not with fear or resistance but with direct acknowledgment and an invitation to have tea.

- Saying "I see you, Mara, let's have tea" is presented as a metaphor for meeting difficult emotions with recognition and openness rather than reactivity, which creates a moment of freedom from identification with those emotions.

- Guiding students to "have tea with Mara" is framed as the practical goal of teaching the RAIN practice.

### Introduction to the RAIN Framework

- RAIN is an acronym standing for Recognize, Allow, Investigate, and Nurture, and it is presented as a structured method for bringing mindful and compassionate presence to difficult emotions.

- The original version of RAIN was introduced by Michelle McDonald into the insight meditation community approximately 18 to 20 years prior, and the version being taught in this session differs by explicitly incorporating the wing of compassion.

- Recognize and Allow are described as a pair corresponding to the wing of mindfulness, while Investigate and Nurture are described as a pair corresponding to the wing of compassion.

- Recognizing involves clearly seeing and naming what is present, while allowing involves making room for that experience without immediately trying to change or escape it.

- Deepening recognition through investigation allows wisdom to emerge, while deepening allowing through nurturing activates embodied compassion.

### The Practice of Saying Yes as the Allow Step

- Allowing is taught through the practice of saying "yes" to present experience, where "yes" does not mean agreement or approval but rather an acknowledgment of the reality of what is arising.

- The habitual response to unpleasant experience is described as a form of "no," in which the body contracts, the mind resists, and there is an implicit belief that something is wrong with the situation, the other person, or oneself.

- A guided meditation is offered in which participants are invited to bring a moderately difficult recent situation to mind, first exploring the felt sense of resistance and contraction, and then shifting to an attitude of allowing or "yes."

- Participants are asked to notice the difference between the two orientations, with many reporting greater spaciousness with "yes" and contraction with "no."

- It is acknowledged that some participants found "no" to feel more comfortable or even better in certain moments, and this is validated as an important nuance rather than a failure of the practice.

- There are circumstances in which saying "no" is wise and appropriate, particularly when dealing with traumatic material or when the nervous system is not yet resourced enough to safely open into the body.

- Practicing the allowing step with traumatic content is explicitly cautioned against, as opening into the body without sufficient resourcing can contribute to re-traumatization.

- Viktor Frankl's observation that between stimulus and response there is a space, and that in that space lies freedom, is cited as a way of articulating what the Allow step of RAIN makes possible.

### Investigate and Nurture Through a Clinical Case Example

- A case example is shared of a woman whose mother was dying of terminal breast cancer, and who was the sole local caregiver despite having a history of neglect and narcissism from her mother.

- The woman felt deep anger toward her mother but was also ashamed of that anger, believing it made her a bad person, which exemplifies the second arrow dynamic.

- Investigation began with the presenting emotion of anger, following the principle of starting where the student is, regardless of whether that entry point seems like the most obvious gateway.

- Investigation is described as a "U-turn," in which attention is redirected from outward projection onto others back inward to the felt sense of emotion as lived energy in the body.

- Inquiry questions used during investigation include asking what the person is believing in the moment of the emotion, what they are unwilling to feel, and where the feeling lives in the body.

- Bringing awareness to the body posture and facial expression associated with an emotion is described as a way of deepening embodiment, since the vagal nerve helps activate the full-body experience through these physical channels.

- As the woman allowed her anger to fully express itself inwardly, it eventually exhausted itself and gave way to a deep sense of emptiness, aloneness, and grief, revealing the core wound beneath the anger.

- The key investigative question of "what does this place most need right now" is identified as a powerful pivot point that transitions naturally from investigation into nurturing.

- Nurturing was delivered through the woman placing her hand on her heart, saying "I'm sorry" and "I love you," and directing care toward the image of her younger self in the bath, whom she had abandoned through her anger.

- Over subsequent weeks, repeated rounds of RAIN allowed her to increasingly rest in a sense of wholeness, and she eventually became able to perceive her mother's own vulnerability, insecurity, and loneliness.

### The Alchemy of Self-Compassion

- For compassion to be genuine and embodied rather than abstract, there must first be direct contact with the actual vulnerability that is present, described as the "ouch" of lived experience.

- Common ways of avoiding self-compassion include minimizing one's pain by comparing it to others' suffering or by deciding that one deserves to feel bad, both of which prevent full contact with vulnerability.

- Investigation serves the function of enabling full contact with vulnerability, while nurturing provides the active response that completes the compassionate gesture.

- The part of the brain correlated with compassion is located near the motor cortex, which governs activity, supporting the understanding that compassion has an inherently active quality rather than being purely receptive.

- Nurturing phrases such as "I love you," "I'm listening," "I'm not leaving," "it's okay," and "it's not your fault" are offered as examples of language that communicates presence and care to the suffering part of oneself.

- Using one's own name during self-compassion practice is noted as a technique supported by research on effective compassion delivery.

- Imagery can be a powerful vehicle for nurturing, including calling on a deceased loved one, invoking a future self who embodies full wisdom and kindness, or imagining being surrounded and held by a loving presence or light.

- When imagining an external source of love and nourishment, the boundary between self and other can dissolve, allowing a return to a larger sense of being that is not confined to the small self.

### The Shift in Identity After RAIN and the Nature of Ongoing Practice

- After completing the RAIN process, resting in the quality of presence that remains is described as essential, because it is in that resting that a shift in identity becomes available.

- Following RAIN, the practitioner or student may discover that they are no longer identified with the self that was angry, afraid, or ashamed, but rather with the presence, tenderness, and loving awareness that held those states.

- This shift in identity is described as the central blessing of the practice, not a permanent resolution but a recurring recognition that becomes more accessible over time.

- A common misunderstanding about RAIN is that completing it once should result in permanent freedom from reactivity, and this is gently corrected by framing the practice as cumulative and iterative.

- The metaphor of cloth being dyed indigo is used to describe how repeated immersion in loving awareness gradually saturates one's being with that quality, so that it becomes more familiar than the expressions of the small self.

- Rumi's poem "Wax" is quoted to evoke the liberation that comes from recognizing one's own luminous nature and releasing the habitual self-constriction that obscures it.

### Skillful Teaching Moves for Nurturing and Non-Identification

- Jack Kornfield adds that invoking common humanity at the right moment in a class setting is a powerful skillful means, such as asking how many people have felt betrayed or confused and inviting a show of hands.

- This technique shifts consciousness away from the personal and isolated experience of suffering toward a recognition that these experiences are universally human, reducing the tendency to take difficulty personally.

- Another teaching move involves inviting a student who is carrying significant pain to stand at the front of the class while the rest of the group, already primed through loving-kindness practice, gazes at them and silently wishes them well.

- The student standing at the front is encouraged to keep their eyes open and actively receive the compassionate attention of the group, which teaches them experientially that great difficulty can be held within a spirit of loving awareness.

- Both of these moves are described as context-dependent and should be deployed at the right moment rather than used routinely in every class.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to experiment with the hand model of the brain in their own teaching and to assess whether it resonates with their students as a tool for understanding emotional reactivity.

- Exploring the difference between resistance and allowing through the yes meditation is recommended as an ongoing personal and pedagogical practice, with attention to when "no" is the wiser response.

- Further exploration of trauma-sensitive applications of RAIN is planned, with a dedicated session on trauma to address the specific resourcing needed before opening into the body in traumatic contexts.

- Continued practice of the full RAIN process is framed as a long-term commitment to cultivating loving awareness, with the understanding that each round of practice deepens the capacity to rest in that awareness as one's home.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Second Arrow**
- When strong emotions arise, a second layer often accompanies them: feeling bad about ourselves for having the emotion
- This is called the second arrow in the Buddhist tradition
- The first arrow is dealing with difficult circumstances; the second arrow is self-judgment for having the reaction
- This produces the sense: "I feel bad, I am bad"
- Teachers will frequently encounter this layer of unworthiness in students
**The Trance of Unworthiness**
- The sense of feeling unworthy or that something is wrong with oneself is described as pervasive
- A dying woman's last words were: "All my life I thought something was wrong with me"
- A palliative caregiver who sat with over 10,000 dying people reported that the greatest regret of the dying was not living true to themselves, but living according to others' expectations and limited self-concepts
**The Two Wings of Awareness**
- The wing of mindfulness: recognizing what is happening; as recognition deepens it unfurls into wisdom
- The wing of compassion: begins with allowing, then gradually deepens into tenderness
- When stuck, the wing of compassion needs to be intentionally cultivated
**The Hand Model of the Brain (Dan Siegel)**
- The wrist and lower palm represent the brain stem
- The thumb folded into the palm represents the limbic area
- Together, brain stem and limbic regulate arousal and the fight/flight/freeze response
- The fingers folded over represent the cortex
- The front fingers represent the prefrontal cortex, which enables mindfulness, empathy, and down-regulation of the limbic system
- When integrated, the prefrontal cortex helps contextualize threatening messages
- When stressed or in the trance of unworthiness, a person can "flip their lid," losing contact with the prefrontal cortex
- Simply recognizing that one has flipped their lid begins to reintegrate the brain
**The Vicious Cycle**
- Feeling worthless or unlovable makes a person more likely to flip their lid
- Flipping the lid leads to behavior that reaffirms the sense that something is wrong
- This creates a deepening spiral of reactivity and self-judgment
**The Buddha and Mara**
- When Mara appeared, the Buddha's attendant Ananda would panic
- The Buddha would calmly say "I see you, Mara. Let's have tea"
- This direct, non-reactive acknowledgment of difficulty is contrasted with reactivity that creates identity with the difficulty
- This story is frequently recalled by students as helpful in practice
**RAIN: Recognize, Allow, Investigate, Nurture**
- Originally offered by Michelle McDonald to the Insight Meditation community approximately 18–20 years prior
- Tara Brach's version explicitly includes the wing of compassion
- R and A (Recognize and Allow) correspond to the two wings: seeing clearly and making room
- I (Investigate) is the deepening of recognition into wisdom through inquiry
- N (Nurture) is the deepening of allowing into active compassion
- RAIN is practiced as pairs: Recognize/Allow and Investigate/Nurture
**The "Yes" Meditation**
- Allowing is described as saying "yes" to the reality of what is present, not agreement with content or a wish for it to stay
- Tara Brach practiced this during a month-long retreat while dealing with separation, custody concerns, anxiety, sadness, and a sinus infection
- The "yes" began mechanically, became humorous, then opened into spaciousness and tenderness
- Participants were guided to contrast the bodily experience of "no" (contraction, resistance) with "yes" (spaciousness, allowing)
- "No" is not always wrong; there are times when setting limits is wise and compassionate, especially with traumatic material
**Investigation**
- Investigation is described as a U-turn: moving from projecting outward onto the world to bringing attention inward to the felt sense in the body
- Useful inquiry questions include: What's here? What am I believing? What am I unwilling to feel? Where is it in the body? What does this place need?
- Inviting facial expression and body posture to match the emotion helps with embodiment and accessing dissociated material
- Investigation brings full contact with reality and the underlying vulnerability
**Nurturing**
- Nurturing follows investigation and responds actively to the vulnerability contacted
- Methods include: placing a hand on the heart or cheek, using phrases such as "I love you," "I'm listening," "I'm not leaving," "It's okay," "It's not your fault," using the person's name
- Imagery can be used: imagining a deceased loved one offering comfort, calling on a future self, imagining being surrounded by light or love
- Nurturing by imagined others acts as a bridge that dissolves the sense of a separate self and reconnects to a larger sense of being
**The Shift After RAIN**
- After completing RAIN, resting in the quality of presence that remains reveals a shift in identity
- The practitioner is no longer identified as the self that was angry, afraid, or ashamed
- What remains is recognized as presence, tenderness, and loving awareness
- This is not a one-time shift; it requires repeated practice
- Described using the metaphor of cloth being dyed indigo: each dipping holds the color more until the cloth is saturated
**Skillful Teaching Moves (Jack Kornfield)**
- Asking the group "How many of you have felt this?" normalizes experience and introduces common humanity
- Inviting a person carrying difficulty to stand at the front while the group directs loving-kindness toward them teaches the capacity to hold difficulty with compassion
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**On the Universality of Unworthiness**
- The sense that "something is wrong with me" is described as one of the most pervasive experiences across people
- It operates below the line of awareness and hooks identity when unrecognized
- It is not a personal failing but a conditioned pattern that can be met with awareness
**On Living True to Oneself**
- The greatest regret reported among the dying was not living true to themselves
- This is framed not only as a concern of the dying but as a living issue for many people and their students
**On Normalizing Emotional Experience**
- Framing difficult emotions in terms of evolution and neuroscience makes them less personal
- Helping students leave an encounter feeling that their emotion is not their fault is described as a gift
- Common humanity practices shift consciousness from personal suffering to shared human experience
**On the Nature of Compassion**
- The heart of Buddhism is described as compassion, and the heart of compassion is compassion for the life that is right here
- Compassion requires contact with vulnerability; without it, compassion remains abstract
- The part of the brain correlated with compassion is near the motor cortex, indicating that compassion has an active quality
**On Identity and Awakening**
- The juncture that signals awakening and healing is when there is a felt sense of kindness or tenderness toward one's own pain
- RAIN facilitates a shift from identification with a reactive small self to recognition of oneself as loving awareness
- The more the practice is repeated, the more loving awareness becomes the familiar home rather than the reactive states
**On Trauma and the Limits of Allowing**
- Opening into the body and saying "yes" is not appropriate with traumatic material without prior resourcing
- Doing so can contribute to re-traumatization
- Resourcing must come first when working with trauma

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Strong emotions such as fear, anger, grief, and shame are identified as primary challenges practitioners and students encounter
- The second arrow — self-judgment for having difficult emotions — compounds the original difficulty and is described as pervasive
- The trance of unworthiness, operating below conscious awareness, hooks identity and perpetuates a vicious cycle of reactivity and self-condemnation
- Flipping the lid — losing access to the prefrontal cortex under stress — results in behavior that people later cannot imagine having done, deepening shame
- Dissociation from the body is noted as a common challenge; embodiment practices such as facial expression and body posture are offered as ways to access what is present
- Trauma is specifically flagged as requiring a different approach: opening into the body and practicing allowing without prior resourcing can contribute to re-traumatization
- Tara Brach describes her own experience at retreat of arriving with anxiety, sadness, custody concerns, and a sinus infection, illustrating that teachers themselves face these challenges
- Tara Brach describes a period at the Forest Refuge of being caught in a core sense of having let people down, requiring active nurturing practice to move through
- The tendency to minimize one's own pain — by saying "I deserve this" or "others have it worse" — is identified as a way people move away from self-compassion and avoid contacting vulnerability
