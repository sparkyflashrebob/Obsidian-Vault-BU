# MMTCP Mod 04 Working with Pain - Oct, 15

### Working with Pain — Mindfulness-Based Pain Teaching
📅 October 15, 2026
👥 Main Teachers: Tara Brach, Jack Kornfield
### Outline
### Universal Nature of Pain and Cultural Conditioning

- Pain is one of the most frequently asked-about topics in mindfulness teaching, with students consistently seeking guidance on how to work with it effectively.

- Every group, without exception, encounters pain, making it essential for teachers to understand and address it thoroughly.

- All humans share a deep conditioning to dislike and fear pain, as it functions as a signal to pay attention and potentially take action.

- Western culture has significantly exacerbated the natural reaction to pain by framing it as something bad, unnatural, and in need of control.

- This cultural framing creates a heightened fear response that can cause the world to shrink, the mind to obsess, and the body and heart to contract away from full living.

### The Core Teaching of Pain Versus Suffering

- One of the most foundational teachings across wisdom traditions is that pain is inevitable, while suffering is optional.

- Physical bodies will inevitably experience pain, pleasure, wanting, fearing, and the full range of changing inner weather systems.

- Whether suffering arises depends entirely on how one relates to what is happening, rather than on the experience itself.

- When a person is trapped in unpleasantness, hating it and feeling oppressed by it, their identity contracts and shapes their entire sense of self.

- Pain, like any arising experience, can serve as a profound portal of awakening that grabs attention and opens the door to deep wisdom and compassion.

- Presenting pain as a spiritual gateway in an overly cheerful way is not effective, as it fails to meet people where they are in their experience.

### The Mechanics of Resistance to Pain

- Resistance to pain manifests on multiple levels simultaneously, including physical tensing, emotional aversion, mental proliferation, and behavioral responses.

- Anything that falls below the line of awareness during a pain response snags one's identity, causing a person to become identified as a physically oppressed self.

- Unnoticed mental judging and proliferating around pain further entrenches the sense of being victimized or defeated by the experience.

- Suffering arises specifically when identity contracts around pain, not from the pain sensation itself.

- The human negativity bias makes it very difficult to hold pain within a larger perspective, and the mind quickly moves toward catastrophic meanings.

- Positive affirmations or reframes that bypass the actual experience are also identified as ineffective coping strategies.

- A useful equation for teaching is that pain multiplied by resistance equals suffering, illustrating that the degree of resistance directly determines the degree of suffering.

- Contracting away from pain causes fatigue, anxiety, increased physical unpleasantness, and a critical lack of attunement to what one truly needs.

### The Practice of Allowing Presence with Pain

- The foundational attitude for working with pain is one of allowing presence, which means meeting each arising experience with a quality of inclusion rather than rejection.

- A practical mantra of "you belong" or "this too" can be applied to each layer of experience as it arises, including the physical sensation, the fear, and the grief beneath it.

- Applying this attitude of belonging to each wave of experience dissolves the identity hooks that keep a person contracted as a judging or oppressed self.

- Through this practice, one can begin to re-inhabit the body in a new way, moving with greater sensitivity, care, and intimacy rather than at a gross level of sensation.

- Chögyam Trungpa's teaching of meeting one's edge and softening captures the ongoing nature of this practice, as it must be relearned with each new wave of pain or illness.

- Anne Morrow Lindbergh's passage describes the practice as going with the pain, opening the body to it as a vessel, and allowing it to fill and recede like a tide.

- This approach creates a sense of inner freedom in which the pain belongs to the body and to life itself, rather than being a personal affliction.

### Guidelines for Teaching Mindfulness with Pain

- One practical guideline is to shift language from the word "pain" to "sensations," as the latter avoids the fixed, adversarial connotation and invites curious investigation.

- Describing experience as a constellation of changing sensations encourages the practitioner to become an engaged witness who notices movement, shift, and impermanence.

- Scanning for resistances at all levels is a key practice, as the act of noticing resistance alone begins to free identity from being hitched to the sense that something is wrong.

- Learning to stay with discomfort in a wise and courageous way, rather than immediately flinching away, is an important skill developed through practice.

- When pain or sensations are intense, it is helpful to locate areas of the body that feel pleasant or neutral and to sense a softening around the area of difficulty.

- Imagining space inside the painful sensations, much like the space within an atom, can help the sensations feel less solid and more workable.

- Mark Nepo's image of a tree falling into open spring water versus cracking ice illustrates the quality of spacious, soft attention that allows sensations to move rather than resist.

- Titrating and pendulating are techniques in which a practitioner establishes a safe or neutral anchor in the body, moves briefly into the zone of difficulty, and then returns, gradually building the capacity for mindful presence with intense sensation.

- Loving-kindness practice can serve as a powerful complement to pain work, with the phrase "rest in love" offering a container of warmth around difficult experience.

### The Role of Self-Compassion and Accompaniment in Pain

- When accompanying someone in pain, the most healing offering is often a simple communication of presence and care, expressed through phrases such as "I care about your suffering."

- Self-compassion is identified as central to working with pain, as pain frequently generates a sense of separation and a feeling that something is wrong with oneself.

- Acknowledging "there is hurting here" and offering genuine care to the place of pain can shift the relationship from suffering to unpleasantness without the added layer of self-judgment.

- A teacher's own capacity to shift from resistance to inclusion is described as contagious, creating an atmosphere in which others can relax and open to what is moving through them.

- Knowing that pain belongs, and communicating that knowing, gives both teacher and student the heart space to be present with the full range of human experience.

### Pain as a Messenger and the Felt-Sense Prayer

- An anonymous poem called the Felt-Sense Prayer personifies pain as a messenger carrying good news, asking to be heard rather than suppressed, ignored, or condemned.

- Pain is described in the poem as the most recent note in a long symphony, representing the most visible branch of roots that have been challenged over time.

- The poem frames pain as guiding a person back to tender places within themselves where they can be held with compassion and honesty.

- Practical changes such as diet, sleep, exercise, and conscious breathing are presented as possible invitations from pain rather than demands from an enemy.

- Success in relating to pain is measured not by its eradication but by the shift in the internal landscape from which it emerges.

- Pain is ultimately described as a friend tugging at one's sleeve, energizing the capacity for self-care and leading toward the mysterious core of being where insight and wisdom are naturally available.

### Why Sitting with Discomfort Matters in Practice

- Students often ask why they should sit with discomfort rather than simply moving, and teachers are encouraged to find their own genuine answer to this question.

- One answer is that there may come a point in life when movement is not possible, and having cultivated the capacity to be with discomfort prepares a person for those moments.

- Another answer is that teachers who have not opened to the full range of experience will not be able to be genuinely present with students or loved ones who are suffering.

- Ajahn Neb's practice of having students do nothing unless necessary for an entire day reveals how many movements throughout the day are made purely to relieve the discomfort woven into human incarnation.

- This practice is not intended to assign fault but to help students recognize that discomfort is a natural part of having a human body, and that life involves a constant dance with it.

- Staying with intense pain while titrating and pendulating builds a trust that something within the process wants to heal itself, even when full physical healing is not possible.

- Lauren Slater's observation highlights that the healing connections within and between people, which soothe terror and support wholeness, are often overlooked in favor of quick symptom relief.

- Voltaire's framing that medicine primarily consists of supporting the patient while nature does the healing points to the body's own deep capacity for self-regulation and return to balance.

### Discernment Around Moving Versus Staying with Pain

- A practical question that arises frequently in teaching is how to know when it is wise to move away from pain versus when to stay and continue exploring it.

- Noticing the habitual pattern of constantly adjusting and moving away from discomfort is identified as the first and most important level of awareness to develop.

- For practitioners whose tendency is to gut it out, the wiser guidance may be to move mindfully and bring more self-care to the experience.

- For practitioners who habitually move away from discomfort after only a few seconds, the invitation is to stay for even ten seconds and notice what arises in that brief window.

- Even ten seconds of staying with discomfort while breathing can produce a radical awakening to the realization that there is room for the experience, and ultimately room for the full arc of life including dying.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to continue developing their own personal practice of working with pain as a foundation for guiding others effectively.

- Exploration of titrating and pendulating techniques is planned for further discussion in the context of trauma work.

- Participants are invited to observe their own patterns of moving toward or away from discomfort as an ongoing inquiry both on and off the cushion.

- The group intends to deepen its understanding of how mindfulness and loving-kindness together create the conditions for both personal healing and effective teaching around pain.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Pain as Universal Human Experience**
- Every group, without exception, encounters pain
- Students benefit from being reminded that not liking pain and fearing it is universal conditioning
- Pain is a signal — it calls attention and may indicate something needs care
- Western culture treats pain as bad, unnatural, and something to be controlled
**Pain vs. Suffering**
- Core teaching from wisdom traditions: "Pain is inevitable, suffering is optional"
- Suffering arises from how we relate to pain, not from pain itself
- When identity contracts around pain, the world gets small — mind obsesses, body and heart contract
- Being trapped in unpleasantness, hating it, feeling oppressed by it — this is suffering
**The Equation: Pain × Resistance = Suffering**
- Resistance to pain amplifies suffering
- Contracting away from pain causes fatigue, anxiety, increased physical unpleasantness, and self-harm
- Resistance creates a lack of attunement to what we truly need, undermining self-care
- Resistance operates on multiple levels: physical tensing, emotional aversion, mental proliferation, behavioral reaction
**Levels of Resistance**
- Physical: body bracing and tensing against sensation
- Emotional: "I don't like this, I don't want this"
- Mental: catastrophizing, projecting into the future, proliferating stories
- Behavioral: seeking quick fixes, compulsive problem-solving
- Anything below the line of awareness snags identity — we become identified as an oppressed or victimized self
**Mindfulness Approach to Pain**
- Seeing resistance is the beginning of freeing from it
- Simply noticing resistance — without trying to change it — allows more of awareness to inhabit the space
- Shift language from "pain" to "sensations" — sensations are changing, dynamic, less fixed
- Become an engaged witness: embodied, interested, caring, not detached
- Scan for resistances at all levels
- Learn to lean in rather than flinch away — not in a machismo way, but with wisdom and courage
**Titrating and Pendulating**
- Establish a place in the body that feels safe, pleasant, or neutral
- Move attention into the difficult zone in small increments (titrating)
- Pendulate back to the safe/neutral place
- This allows increasing mindful presence to what has been pushed away without becoming overwhelmed
- Particularly relevant for intense pain and trauma
**Arousing Resources / Supports**
- Locate places in the body that are pleasant or neutral
- Soften around the pain rather than against it
- Use imagery or words that evoke spaciousness around discomfort
- Sense space inside the sensations themselves — like the space within an atom
- Allow sensations to move and shift rather than be fixed
**Self-Compassion and Loving-Kindness**
- Self-compassion is at the heart of working with pain
- Pain often brings a sense of separation and something being wrong with oneself
- Offering care to the place of pain: "Darling, I care about this suffering"
- Loving-kindness can be directed toward specific areas of the body
- "Rest in love" as a mantra within a field of loving-kindness
- When accompanying someone in pain: "I'm here, I care, I'm with this"
**The Body as Messenger**
- Pain is a messenger with good news — it guides back to tender places in oneself
- Pain may ask for changes: diet, sleep, exercise, conscious breathing, seeking vaster reality
- Success is not measured by eradication of pain but by a shift in the internal landscape
- The body has its own intelligence and innate capacity for self-regulation and healing
**Teaching Considerations**
- Know the student's tendency: do they gut it out or constantly move away?
- For those who gut it out — encourage mindful self-care and movement
- For those who constantly move — encourage staying for even ten seconds
- Radical awakening can happen in ten seconds of staying: "I can handle this. There's room."
- Pain as a gateway — introduce gently depending on student's openness and availability
- Contagion of presence: when a teacher knows "this belongs," it helps others open too
**Healing and Medicine**
- The body itself does the healing; medicine and surgery create conditions for it
- Something deep in our being wants to regulate and come into wholeness
- Healing is not always quick — it connects with a mysterious presence within
- Even when pain cannot be completely removed, a wise relationship with it is possible
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Western Cultural Conditioning Around Pain**
- Western culture frames pain as bad, unnatural, and in need of control
- This conditioning greatly exacerbates the suffering response
- The negativity bias makes it hard to hold pain in a larger, more spacious way
- The internet and information culture can amplify health anxiety and catastrophizing
**Pain as Portal to Awakening**
- Pain grabs attention in a way that can catalyze deep wisdom and compassion
- It is one of the most potent gateways to spiritual realization — though this should not be announced cheerfully to someone in acute suffering
- As identity hooks dissolve through the practice of "you belong," a deeper sense of self emerges
- The Heart Sutra teaching: "Form does not differ from emptiness, emptiness does not differ from form" — pain belongs to the ocean of experience
**The Teaching of "You Belong" / "This Too"**
- Whatever arises — physical unpleasantness, fear, grief — can be met with "you belong"
- This mantra creates space, presence, and tenderness
- Identity hooks dissolve; one moves from being an oppressed self or a judging self to inhabiting life more fully
- Re-entering the body becomes possible — with sensitivity, intimacy, and care
**Impermanence and the Changing Nature of Sensation**
- Sensations move and shift; they are not fixed
- Witnessing this change directly reveals the nature of impermanence
- Pain is "empty" — not "my pain," just sensation arising in awareness
- In sensing that "it belongs," the whole sense of who we are shifts
**The Role of the Teacher / Caregiver**
- Teachers may be asked to sit with people in pain, people who are dying, and their own passing body
- Shifting from resistance to inclusion in one's own being is contagious — it creates an atmosphere of openness
- Managed care and quick symptom amelioration are contrasted with the "mysterious alchemy" of healing connections between people
- The art of medicine, as Voltaire noted, is primarily about supporting the patient while nature heals
**Human Incarnation and Discomfort**
- Discomfort is woven into human incarnation — we are constantly in a dance with it
- Ajahn Neb's practice of doing nothing unless necessary reveals how many movements are made simply to relieve discomfort throughout a day
- This is not a fault or something wrong — it is part of having a human body
- Learning to be with discomfort prepares one to be present with others in their suffering

### Stories
**Tara Brach — Her Own Experience with Chronic Pain and Illness**
- Tara was a highly physical person, a self-described "jock" and later a "yoga jock," proud of her flexibility
- About 14 years prior to the teaching, her hyperflexibility was identified as a genetic tendency toward loose connective tissue, causing repeated injury
- She experienced chronic pain and kept overdoing physical activity, injuring herself repeatedly across biking, swimming, and other sports
- Her body became "the enemy" — a disappointment, a separate object causing trouble
- She engaged in constant mental obsessing about how to fix it and judged herself as the one hurting herself
- She attended a six-week retreat during the first dramatic year of illness
- At retreat, she began using the mantra "you belong" or "this too" for every wave of experience — physical unpleasantness, fear, grief
- This practice dissolved identity hooks; she became less identified as judge or oppressed self
- She began re-entering her body with sensitivity, intimacy, and care — moving slowly, listening, inhabiting sensation more subtly
- She notes this is an ongoing process — each new wave of sickness or pain requires meeting the edge again and softening
**Tara Brach — Back Pain During the Teaching Retreat**
- The day before the session, Tara's seating position caused her to feel close to a back spasm
- She observed her body bracing against it, her emotional aversion, her mental catastrophizing about four more days of the retreat
- She also observed the behavioral proliferation: planning to get ibuprofen and arnica rub
- Because she was watching it with some space, and because of the quality of consciousness in the room, she did not get stuck
**Tara Brach — Friend with Psoriasis**
- A friend had psoriasis on both arms
- He conducted a loving-kindness experiment, offering metta to only one arm
- The psoriasis went away on that one arm
- Noted as an experiment with an N of one
**Tara Brach — Dan Gottlieb**
- Dan Gottlieb is a paraplegic and radio host on the East Coast and a dear friend of Tara's
- He goes through a lot of physical suffering
- In his own process, he tracks what is happening and finds the only thing that truly makes a difference is acknowledging "ouch, this hurts" and offering deep care to the place of pain
**Jack Kornfield — Ajahn Neb's Retreat Practice**
- Ajahn Neb is described as a great Thai teacher featured in the book Living Dharma
- Her practice was to have students sit still and do nothing unless they had to, for a full day or longer
- Students would wake up, lie comfortably, then notice the urge to pee — discomfort arising
- They would go pee, then feel cold feet and cold bottom — more discomfort
- They would get dressed to relieve that, then feel the body ache from sitting, then hunger, then the unpleasantness of leftover food on a plate
- Throughout the day, students would observe how many movements are made simply to relieve the discomfort woven into human incarnation
- The purpose was not to find fault but to reveal that this is part of having a human body — we are in a constant dance with discomfort
**Jack Kornfield — Story of Sitting with Pain and the Bedside of a Dying Person**
- Jack raises the question of why one should sit with discomfort rather than simply move
- He offers that there may come a point in life when one cannot move, or when one is sitting at the bedside of someone dying
- If a teacher has not opened to this range of experience themselves, they will not be able to be present in an open-hearted way with another person's suffering
