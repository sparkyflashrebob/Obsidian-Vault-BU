# MMTCP Mod 04 - Mindfulness of Emotions and Feelings - Oct, 15

### Mindfulness of Emotions and Feelings
📅 October 15, 2026
👥 Jack Kornfield (Main Teacher), Tara Brach (Co-Teacher)
### Outline
### The Second Foundation of Mindfulness and the Nature of Feelings

- Mindfulness of feelings is identified as the second foundation of mindfulness, following mindfulness of the body.

- Awareness of feelings begins with recognizing whether an experience is pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral, without judgment or reactivity.

- Praise and blame, gain and loss, pleasure and pain, joy and sorrow, and birth and death are described as opposites that weave together to form the experience of human life.

- Habitual reactivity to pleasant feelings involves grasping and wanting more, while unpleasant feelings tend to trigger fear, resistance, and avoidance.

- Primary feelings, drawn from Buddhist psychology, give rise to secondary feelings and emotions, forming a continuous stream of experience within consciousness.

### How People Sense and Experience Feelings

- People perceive feelings through different channels, including the mind, the heart, or the body, and often only one of these channels is accessible to them.

- Becoming more mindful of feelings requires expanding one's capacity to sense emotions as they arise across multiple channels.

- A large proportion of feelings operate unconsciously, shaping decisions and behavior without deliberate awareness.

- Supreme Court Justice William O. Douglas is cited as observing that approximately 90% of decisions at the highest judicial level are based on feelings, with rational thought serving primarily as justification.

- Teaching mindfulness of feelings helps people become less entangled in reactivity and grasping, which are identified as primary sources of suffering.

### Normalizing Feelings as a Teaching Practice

- A foundational step in guiding students is to normalize feelings and reassure them that whatever they are experiencing is simply a feeling and a natural part of being human.

- Acknowledging feelings without labeling them as good or bad reflects the spirit of loving awareness and is described as enormously helpful to students beginning to open up emotionally.

- Consciousness is described as being mediated by a wide range of emotional states, with Buddhist psychology identifying 52 distinct states and broader lists cataloguing up to 500 emotions.

- Emotions are not generally part of formal education, making mindfulness practice a rare opportunity for people to develop a conscious relationship with their inner emotional life.

- Pema Chodron is quoted to affirm that both negative and positive emotions are exactly what is needed to be fully human, fully awake, and fully alive.

### Limbic Resonance and the Role of Loving Awareness

- Human nervous systems are described as tuned to one another through a phenomenon previously called limbic resonance or mood contagion, whereby emotional states are transmitted between people.

- Just as the strings of one violin resonate with another being played nearby, people resonate with the emotional states of those around them and with the broader culture.

- Loving awareness is presented as capable of holding all emotional states, including those amplified by cultural fear or collective distress.

- Mindfulness is clarified to include clear comprehension and appropriate response, not merely passive observation of inner experience.

- Mary Oliver's poem is referenced to illustrate that properly attended feelings, whether delightful or devastating, are only a beginning that can inspire wise and engaged action.

### Helping Students Connect with Their Feelings

- A first step in guiding people toward emotional awareness is teaching them to know and actually experience what they feel, rather than remaining disconnected from their inner life.

- Directing attention carefully to the body is described as a practical method for helping students who tend to live primarily in their minds to access their emotional experience.

- Writing a letter to oneself after meditation is offered as a technique for articulating feelings that are hidden from ordinary awareness.

- Naming feelings with honesty and clarity is shown to bring a person alive to their inner experience, increasing trust that awareness can tune into emotional states.

- As emotional openness grows, suffering may initially increase as previously unseen pain, compassion, and vulnerability become more visible.

- Questions such as "Where do you feel that in your body?" and "What images or stories come?" are offered as practical tools for guiding students who feel numb or disconnected.

- Inviting images and stories alongside bodily sensation can help feelings become more fully present and accessible to awareness.

### The Hindrances as Emotional Patterns in Meditation

- Buddhist psychology provides an articulate framework of hindrances, described as obstacles to clarity rather than problems requiring elimination.

- Desire or grasping is identified as the first hindrance, characterized by wanting particular experiences, states, or outcomes and becoming blind to what is actually present.

- Resistance is the second hindrance, involving aversion, fear, anger, and the pushing away of experience when it does not match expectations.

- Restlessness is another hindrance that arises during practice and can be worked with by naming it gently and allowing loving awareness to become spacious enough to tolerate it.

- Sleepiness and doubt are also listed as hindrances, with doubt manifesting as self-judgment, questioning one's worthiness, or distrust of teachers and practices.

- Tracking feelings as they are, without trying to change them, is presented as the appropriate response to all hindrances.

### Working with Difficult Emotions Including Fear, Shame, Grief, and Rage

- Difficult emotions such as fear, shame, sorrow, longing, guilt, anxiety, jealousy, and rage are described as naturally arising during meditation and in life.

- Karlfried Graf Dürckheim is quoted to argue that genuine spiritual development requires seeking out those who help one risk and endure difficulty rather than those who offer comfort and reinforce the old self.

- Only by repeatedly exposing oneself to difficulty and even the possibility of annihilation can that which is indestructible be found within, and this is described as the spirit of true awakening.

- Staying present with strong emotions through loving awareness and affirming that they are part of being human is described as truly liberating.

- Positive emotions such as joy, rapture, love, delight, ease, and contentment can also be unfamiliar and difficult to tolerate, as many people are more accustomed to suffering than to ease.

- Ajahn Chah's instruction to sit fully in the midst of rage without escape is offered as an example of how a teacher's trust and fearlessness can give a student the confidence to remain present with intense emotion.

### Respect, Trust, and the Capacity to Hold Emotions

- Emotions are described as requiring respect for their power, their wounds, and their presence, and the appropriate response is to bow to them and hold them with loving awareness.

- Teaching others to work with emotions requires transmitting a sense of trust and confidence that the space of awareness and compassion is large enough to hold everything.

- Frank Ostaseski's account of accompanying a family through the death of their seven-year-old son illustrates how entering grief fully, rather than avoiding it, can release a fierce and transformative love.

- When people in grief ask what they can do, what they need first is the teacher's courage, trust, and capacity to be present as a model of what is humanly possible.

- Reflective questions about one's family history with emotion, what was allowed or forbidden, and what remains outside one's comfort zone are offered as a starting point for deepening personal emotional awareness.

### Expanding the Window of Tolerance Through Mindfulness

- Mindfulness practice directly expands what neuroscience calls the window of tolerance, enlarging the heart's capacity to be present for the full range of human emotion.

- Tara Brach reflects that sustained practice reduces the lag time between being caught in an emotion and recognizing it with awareness, allowing for a quicker and gentler return to presence.

- A question attributed to a well-known sage is offered as a powerful opening practice: "What are you unwilling to feel?" as most unconscious moments involve some form of avoidance.

- Dana Faulds' poem "Allow" is shared to convey that resisting life's experiences sweeps one away, while allowing them carries one to higher ground through grace.

- Much of the contracted sense of self is organized around what one refuses to feel, and guiding students toward allowing decreases this contraction and reveals a new sense of being.

### Holding Emotions in Group Settings and in Society

- When strong emotions erupt in a group setting, the teacher's role is to remain present, name what is happening, and model the possibility of staying with it before responding or trying to fix anything.

- Inviting the group to take a breath and be present for one another before reacting is described as an enormous gift and a form of modeling what is possible.

- The capacity to hold collective emotion with kindness and confidence is described as increasingly necessary not only in teaching groups but in societies around the world where feelings are erupting without the human skill to meet them wisely.

### The Impermanence of Feelings and the Nature of Emotional Tracking

- Feelings, when observed closely and named with mindfulness, are found to be alive and ever-changing rather than fixed or permanent states.

- What appears to be a solid emotion lasting all day or all week is revealed, upon careful attention, to be a succession of shifting waves moving through mind, body, and heart.

- Tracking emotions moment to moment shows that each feeling gives way to another, such as sadness shifting to wishing, then to fear, then to confidence, and back again.

- No feeling is described as final, as each one is leading toward some greater understanding.

- Molly Ivins is quoted to encourage fighting for justice with joy and outrage alike, illustrating that the full range of feelings can inspire engaged and meaningful action.

### Next Steps

- Further curriculum stages will build on emotional awareness to address working with difficulty, the RAIN process, and diversity and inclusion.

- Participants are encouraged to continue journaling and reflective practices, including exploring their personal and family history with emotion and identifying what remains outside their comfort zone.

- Future sessions will address how to shift from unhealthy emotional patterns toward more healthy feelings such as lovingkindness, well-being, and gratitude, once the foundational work of acknowledgment and presence is established.

- The emphasis going forward is on developing the courage and capacity to remain present for the full range of human emotion before moving toward any modification or transformation of those states.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Second Foundation of Mindfulness**
- After mindfulness of the body, the second foundation is mindfulness of feelings
- The practitioner becomes aware of feelings in the feelings — sensing them deeply
- Initial awareness distinguishes: this is a pleasant feeling, this is an unpleasant feeling, this is a neutral feeling
- This witnessing stance is unhooked from judging, reactivity, or trying to change experience
- The pairs of opposites — praise and blame, gain and loss, pleasure and pain, joy and sorrow, birth and death — weave together to create human incarnation
**Primary and Secondary Feelings**
- Primary feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral) give rise to secondary feelings and emotions
- There is a river of feelings just as there is a river of physical sensations, thoughts, and perceptions
- Buddhist psychology identifies 52 different states; a list of 500 emotions exists as a reference
- The letter C alone yields: calm, capable, cheerful, captivated, cautious, chagrined, challenged, childish, clever, cold-hearted, combative, comfortable, compassionate, confident, confused, conscientious, competitive, contemptful, contrite, courageous, conservative, cowardly, cranky
**How People Sense Feelings**
- Some people sense feelings through the mind, associated with thought
- Some sense feelings through the heart — anger, fear, love, longing, delight
- Many sense feelings primarily in the body
- Some can access all channels; often people have only one channel open
- Becoming more mindful of feeling requires expanding the capacity to sense feeling as it arises
- Most feelings are unconscious
**Feelings and Decision-Making**
- Supreme Court Justice William O. Douglas stated that 90% of decisions at the Supreme Court level are made on the basis of feelings, with the remaining 10% being rationalization
- This reflects how feelings are the basis of how humans relate to the world
**Normalizing Emotions**
- The first step in teaching mindfulness of feelings is to normalize them
- Reassuring students that whatever they feel is just a feeling — human and normal
- This acknowledgment prevents people from being caught, entangled, and reactive
**Habitual Reactivity**
- The usual habit with pleasant feelings is grasping: wanting more, wanting it to stay
- The usual habit with unpleasant feelings is resistance: fear, turning away, distraction
- Both patterns, when unacknowledged, produce reactivity or a kind of sleep
**Limbic Resonance and Mood Contagion**
- The nervous system is tuned to others through limbic resonance or mood contagion
- Like violin strings resonating with another violin, humans resonate with one another and with culture
- Cultural fear spreads through this primitive wiring
- Mindfulness and loving awareness can hold all of this
**The Hindrances**
- The hindrances are named so because they hinder clarity, not because they must be eliminated
- First hindrance: desire or grasping — wanting, scanning for what is desired, becoming attached to perception
- Second hindrance: resistance — not liking, fear, anger, pushing away experience
- Third hindrance: restlessness — becoming mindful of it, naming it, allowing loving awareness to be large enough to tolerate it
- Fourth hindrance: sleepiness — normalized as natural and human
- Fifth hindrance: doubt — doubting oneself, the practice, the teachers, one's worthiness
**Difficult Emotions**
- Fear, shame, sorrow, longing, guilt, anxiety, addiction, jealousy, hurt, anger, and rage all arise in practice
- Jealousy involves a construct of ideas with underneath a feeling of not being enough and wanting something else
- Repression of difficult emotions knots people up and keeps them from being fully alive
- Staying present with strong emotions with loving awareness is described as truly liberating
**Positive Emotions**
- Many people can be loyal to their suffering; positive emotions can themselves be difficult
- Joy, rapture, love, delight, ease, bliss, and contentment may be unfamiliar or uncomfortable
- Contentment may not have been consciously experienced in months or years for some people
**Teaching People to Know What They Feel**
- The first step is helping people actually experience what they feel
- Useful questions: Where do you feel that in your body? What images come? What stories come?
- Images and stories can invite feeling to come more fully
- Writing a letter to oneself after meditation can help articulate what is hidden from ordinary awareness
- Naming feelings brings them into clarity and builds trust that awareness can tune into feelings
**Expanding the Window of Tolerance**
- Mindfulness directly expands what neuroscience calls the window of tolerance
- The heart expands to be present for the full range of emotions
- The teacher's confidence and trust that awareness can hold everything is contagious
- Transmitting trust that the space of awareness and compassion is big enough to hold it all is essential
**Emotions as Impermanent**
- Feelings do not last as long as they seem — they move like waves through mind and body
- Sitting with sadness, one notices it shifts: wishing arises, then hating, then fear, then confidence, then sadness again
- Feelings are alive and ever-changing in body, mind, and heart
- No feeling is final; they lead to greater understanding
**Shifting to Healthier Emotional States**
- After acknowledging feelings, it becomes possible to shift to more healthy feelings
- Shifting thought and feeling toward lovingkindness, well-being, self-care, inner peace, confidence, or gratitude is possible
- The caution is not to rush to fix or make feelings go away before they have been fully acknowledged
**Respect for Emotions**
- Emotions want respect for their power and for whatever wounds they carry
- The gesture of bowing to emotions — saying yes, this too, held with loving awareness — is central
- The story of soldiers taking a knee before a mosque illustrates how a gesture of respect can transform a volatile situation

### Stories
**The Scary Ride with Ajahn Chah**
Jack Kornfield shares a personal story. He and his teacher Ajahn Chah were taken by a young man with a new Toyota pickup truck on a long, fast drive on a narrow mountain dirt road to a temple on the Cambodian border. The road had drop-offs and oncoming buses and logging trucks. Jack became very nervous and afraid. He looked over and saw Ajahn Chah's knuckles were white. When they arrived safely and the engine turned off, Ajahn Chah turned to Jack and said, "Scary ride, wasn't it?" — illustrating that even a master simply acknowledged the feeling as it was, without judgment.
**Tom and His Wife's Parkinson's Diagnosis**
Jack Kornfield shares the story of a student named Tom, who had been practicing meditation for several years. Tom tended to be spaced out and emotionally flat. When directed to focus on his body during retreat, he noticed anxiety. He then began obsessing about his wife, who had recently been diagnosed with Parkinson's. Jack suggested Tom write a letter to himself after meditation over several days. What emerged were his fears for his wife and their future, his ongoing insecurity, memories of the same insecurity from childhood and a first marriage that ended badly. When Tom read the letter aloud, he came alive — feeling his inner experience with greater honesty and clarity. As he opened, his sense of suffering increased, but compassion welled up alongside it, and a kind of relief broke through. He became more loving, more direct with his wife, and less afraid.
**Ajahn Chah and the Rage**
Jack Kornfield shares a personal story from his time as a monk with Ajahn Chah. Jack began experiencing waves of anger and rage, partly rooted in his family conditioning — his father was violent and abusive. Jack had vowed never to be like him and suppressed the trauma. When rage began to surface, he went to Ajahn Chah. Ajahn Chah responded without fear, instructing Jack to go back to his hut during the hot season, close the windows, put on all his robes, and sit in the middle of the rage for as long as it lasted. Ajahn Chah's trust and lack of fear gave Jack the sense that it was possible to sit in the midst of it.
**Frank Ostaseski and the Death of Jamie**
Jack Kornfield shares a story from Frank Ostaseski's book The Five Invitations. Ostaseski received a desperate call from a family whose seven-year-old son, Jamie, had just died from cancer at home. He went immediately. Upon arriving, he leaned down and kissed the boy on the forehead. The parents broke into tears — not from fear of the corpse, but because nobody had touched the boy since he died; they feared the grief that touching him might unleash. Ostaseski explained that almost every culture has a ritual of washing the body of those you love. The family gathered rosemary, sage, and lavender, made warm water, and began slowly washing their son. The mother examined every scratch and bruise lovingly. When she reached Jamie's toes, she counted them as she had done on the day he was born. The grief filled the room like an enormous ocean. The parents would look over at Ostaseski with beseeching, tear-filled eyes asking if they could survive this. He would nod and encourage them to continue. As the mother entered the grief fully, a fierce fire of her own love burst forth in the room.
**The Soldiers at the Mosque in Najaf**
Jack Kornfield shares a story about a small group of American soldiers in Iraq who were surrounded by an angry crowd during the war. Rather than opening fire, the officer said, "Take a knee." They all turned their rifles to the ground in front of a great mosque in the holy city of Najaf. The crowd became quiet. The soldiers withdrew without anyone being hurt. The gesture of respect transformed the situation — used as an analogy for how emotions want respect.
