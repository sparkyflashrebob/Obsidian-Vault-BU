### Outline
### Welcome and Opening Reflection

- Participants from approximately 75 countries were welcomed to the Mindfulness Meditation Teacher Certification Program broadcast hosted by Sounds True.

- A brief guided moment of stillness was offered, inviting attendees to settle, breathe, and bring loving kindness to themselves and others.

- Jack Kornfield expressed his enthusiasm for the journey of becoming teachers of the heart and acknowledged the demanding nature of the certification program.

### The Role of Anxiety in Personal and Professional Growth

- Anxiety arising during significant undertakings was reframed as a signal of impending growth rather than a sign of being on the wrong path.

- A metaphor of a dashboard warning light was used to describe how fear and anxiety indicate that a person is approaching the edge of their known self.

- Transformation through training was described as a well-documented and beautiful process observed across hundreds and thousands of program participants.

### Overview of the Program's Progression and Emotional Focus

- The program had moved from foundational teachings on presence, death, and the body toward working with emotions, loving kindness, hindrances, and mindful awareness.

- Mindfulness, loving awareness, compassion, and investigation were identified as the core tools for holding and opening to human experience in a more integrated way.

- Global conflicts, the unequal distribution of vaccines, and widespread suffering were acknowledged as deeply relevant to the practice, emphasizing that practitioners are not separate from the world around them.

### Emotions as the Root of Global and Personal Suffering

- Conflicts and injustices observed around the world were described as being driven by emotions, illusion, ignorance, fear, and confusion.

- The work of awakening and understanding emotional freedom was presented as critically important given the current state of the world.

- Vivek Murthy, the U.S. Surgeon General, was cited as having noted that more than half of what presents in clinics and hospitals is emotionally based rather than purely physical.

### Creating a Safe Space for Emotional Holding in Teaching

- One of the most important capacities a teacher can develop is the ability to create a safe space of mindful loving awareness that can hold students' emotions when they become overwhelmed or triggered.

- The purpose of this space is not to fix emotions but to acknowledge them with understanding so that students can respond wisely.

- Suppression of emotions was distinguished from genuine peace, using the example of anger arising during meditation practice after years of unconscious suppression.

- Sitting with anger fully, listening to its stories, feeling it in the body, and learning not to fear it was described as critical to liberation from trauma and suffering.

### The Buddha's Teaching on Recognizing Emotional Energies

- The archetypal story of the Buddha under the Bodhi tree was referenced, in which the armies of Mara representing fear, desire, temptation, and aggression were met with clear recognition rather than resistance.

- Saying "I see you, Mara" was presented as a model for how spacious freedom arises when emotional energies are acknowledged rather than fought.

- Teachers were encouraged to meet students' fears, grief, loss, and anxiety with this same quality of spacious, non-reactive presence.

### Presence Over Problem-Solving in Teaching and Caregiving

- Hospice work was used to illustrate that the role of a teacher or caregiver is often simply to be present rather than to resolve or fix what is arising.

- Holding a dying person's hand and acknowledging all emotions including anger, fear, bargaining, denial, and grief without attempting to eliminate them was described as a profound act of care.

- The RAIN framework was introduced, with recognition and acknowledgment as the first step, followed by investigation of bodily sensations and the stories emotions tell.

### Cultural Differences in Emotional Expression and the Teacher's Role

- People experience and express emotions differently based on cultural programming, bodily habits, and learned permissions around feeling.

- Observations from a retreat in Switzerland illustrated how participants from different countries demonstrated markedly different emotional styles, from demonstrative to reserved to performance-oriented.

- The teacher's role was clarified as not to fix these differences but to recognize them and teach students to hold all of their experience with loving awareness, including numbness and the inability to feel.

### Introduction of the DEIA Training as a Foundation of the Program

- Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Accessibility training was introduced as an integral and foundational component of the certification program, not an add-on.

- Jack Kornfield and Tara Brach were described as deeply committed to this work, having undertaken their own training with leading facilitators to understand the structures of racism, caste, and unconscious conditioning.

- Racism was identified as the core wound of American society, with the acknowledgment that parallel forms of discrimination and marginalization exist in every country represented in the program.

- The DEIA training was framed as the practice of compassion and loving awareness applied to some of the most difficult dimensions of human culture.

### The Purpose and Value of Affinity Groups Within the Program

- Separating participants into affinity groups based on race and background was explained as a practice that creates necessary space for deeper sharing and learning.

- Historical precedent was drawn from women's retreats and men's retreats, both of which were initially met with resistance but proved profoundly valuable for participants.

- Participants in affinity groups were described as supporting not only their own group but also those in other groups by engaging honestly with their own experience and conditioning.

- The Buddhist teaching of "O nobly born, remember who you are" was invoked as the underlying spirit of this work, pointing toward the inherent dignity of every human being.

### Working with Numbness and Resistance to Difficult Emotions

- Ashley shared that conflict with her mother-in-law had brought up anger, hurt, and sadness, but that she had been experiencing emotional numbness and avoidance rather than direct feeling.

- Resistance to feeling was reframed not as an obstacle to be bypassed but as something to be honored and included within the field of loving awareness.

- Guiding Ashley to locate resistance in her body, she identified it in her chest and named it primarily as fear, specifically the fear of being seen as an angry or bad person.

- Bringing tenderness toward the part of herself that had learned this fear long ago allowed the resistance to soften and shift into underlying sadness and grief.

- Acknowledging the sadness with compassion produced a felt sense of the emotion being shared and lighter, no longer carried alone.

- The insight that arose was that loving awareness can be brought to difficult emotions without requiring full immersion in them, and that feeling them does not make a person bad or cause harm.

### Questioning the Language of Healthy and Unhealthy Emotions

- Amy raised a concern about the use of the word "unhealthy" in the program's reading materials, noting that it carried a sense of judgment toward certain emotions.

- The dualistic framing found in some Buddhist teachings of promoting healthy states and avoiding unhealthy ones was acknowledged as a common but limited approach.

- No emotion was described as inherently bad, including rage, anger, fear, and terror, with the distinction being that harm arises from acting on emotions or holding beliefs associated with them rather than from the emotions themselves.

- Emotions were described as simply feelings, and the teacher's role was reaffirmed as creating a field of mindful loving awareness that can hold all of them without exclusion.

- From that held space, a wise response can emerge, rather than avoidance or reactivity.

### Teaching Across Differences of Race and Lived Experience

- Jennifer Wang raised the question of how a teacher should respond when a student asks about racial suffering that the teacher has not personally experienced.

- Pretending to fully understand an experience one has not lived was identified as unhelpful, while honest acknowledgment of one's own limits alongside genuine curiosity about the student's experience was offered as a more respectful approach.

- Spiritual bypass was named as a risk when teachers respond to racial suffering by simply directing students to sit with it without first engaging with the substance of what has been shared.

- Inviting the group to contribute their own knowledge and experience was presented as a powerful teaching move that distributes wisdom across the room rather than centering the teacher as the sole authority.

- Bringing the group's shared material into a collective sitting practice was described as a meaningful final step, distinct from using meditation as a way to avoid difficult content.

### Practicing Loving Kindness Toward Those Causing Harm

- Mark shared deep grief and difficulty extending loving kindness to people in his family and in positions of power who were justifying violence in the Israel-Palestine conflict.

- Ram Dass's practice of placing images of political figures he disagreed with on his altar alongside saints and teachers was offered as an example of refusing to cast anyone out of one's heart while still opposing their actions.

- Adapted metta phrases such as "may you be free from hatred and fear, may you find peace in yourself" were described as phrases that can be offered to anyone, including those causing great suffering.

- Offering these phrases was described as shifting one's internal orientation away from seeing others as enemies, which is what those promoting division want, without requiring agreement with or support for harmful actions.

- Mark reflected that recognizing the humanity of those spreading harmful ideology, understanding that they too inhabit a suffering body, had been helpful in moving from anger toward a deeper sadness about their condition.

- The enemy was ultimately identified not as any individual but as ignorance and delusion, with the capacity to stand up for justice while still holding the humanity of those one opposes.

### Holding Resistance and Harmony Together as a Teacher

- Lavanya Devdas described how a history of colonization in her background causes her to feel resistance and a sense of imposition when group consensus forms around a suggestion, even when she knows the intention is not oppressive.

- This resistance was validated and affirmed as important, with the observation that colonization harms not only the colonized but also the colonizers and all those shaped by its legacy.

- Lavanya described a felt tension between the gut's call to honor the oppressed part of herself and the heart's pull toward group harmony, with both arising simultaneously.

- Allowing both responses to be held with loving awareness and respect, without trying to reconcile or eliminate either, produced a felt sense of their interconnection.

- The wisdom that emerged was that both the gut and the heart are part of a larger self, and that honoring both opens the space for a deeper and more genuine harmony to manifest.

- Giving voice to pain, naming oppression, and acknowledging harm were described as practices that, rather than disrupting harmony, can create a different and more trustworthy kind of it.

### Closing Reflections on Love as the Foundation of Practice and Teaching

- The secret underlying all of the program's practices was named as love, with mindfulness and love described as inseparable.

- The DEIA work was framed as an expansion of the capacity to love those who are different and those one has been conditioned to see in limited ways.

- Those from marginalized backgrounds were encouraged to nourish themselves, care for themselves, and carry dignity as an offering to others.

- Those from dominant cultural backgrounds were invited to awaken to the pain of separation and learn what it truly means to honor one another.

- Becoming a teacher was described as a practice that brings up everything it is supposed to, and the richness of the journey ahead in the remaining program was affirmed.

### Next Steps

- The program was described as continuing to deepen over the remaining year and three quarters, with further teachings building on the emotional and relational foundations established in this session.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Emotions and Mindfulness Practice**
- Jack Kornfield opened by situating the session within the broader MMTCP curriculum, noting the program had moved from foundational teachings on body and death to working with emotions, loving kindness, hindrances, and difficulties
- He emphasized that the world's conflicts — in Myanmar, Palestine, Israel, Venezuela, Colombia, India, Brazil, Pakistan — are driven by emotions, fear, confusion, and illusion, making emotional literacy critical
- He stated that more than half of what comes through US clinics and hospitals is emotionally based, citing Vivek Murthy, Surgeon General of the United States
**The Role of the Teacher with Emotions**
- The teacher's primary role is to create a safe space of mindful loving awareness that can hold students' emotions when they are triggered or overwhelmed
- The point is not to fix students but to acknowledge and be present with what arises
- Kornfield described hospice work as a model: no emergencies, no fixing, simply being present with love — like a midwife for the spirit leaving the body
**RAIN Practice**
- Recognize and acknowledge the emotion present
- Investigate — sense what it feels like in the body, what stories it tells
- Trust that a deep lesson comes with all emotions
- Resistance itself should be included in the field of loving awareness, not bypassed
**Healthy vs. Unhealthy Emotions**
- Kornfield expressed discomfort with the language of "healthy" and "unhealthy" emotions used in course readings
- He stated no emotion is bad in itself — rage, anger, fear, and terror are not bad; what is destructive is acting on them with associated beliefs
- The field of mindful loving awareness can hold all emotions; from that place a wise response becomes possible
**Cultural Differences in Emotional Expression**
- Kornfield described observing different cultural styles at a European retreat — Italian participants being demonstrative, English participants more reserved, German participants focused on doing it right
- Teachers must recognize these differences and teach students to hold their experience with loving awareness, including numbness and the inability to feel
**DEIA Training as Foundation**
- Kornfield and Tara Brock have undertaken DEIA training with facilitators Crystal, Kitsie, Conda, and Kate
- DEIA is described as integral to an awakened heart and to the practice of mindfulness teaching
- Racism is described as the core wound of US society; parallel suffering exists in every country
- Separate affinity groups for people of color and white participants are explained through the analogy of women's retreats and men's retreats — spaces that allow deeper truth-telling and solidarity
- The DEIA work is framed as Dharma practice: bringing mindful loving awareness to one of the great questions of humanity
**Metta Practice Extended to Those Causing Harm**
- Kornfield described Ram Dass keeping pictures of Dick Cheney and Donald Trump on his altar alongside saints and spiritual figures
- Ram Dass's principle: "I don't have to agree with them. I don't even have to support what they do. But I won't throw them out of my heart."
- Kornfield offered adapted metta phrases for those causing harm: "May you be free from hatred and ill will. May you be free from fear. May you be free from confusion. May you find peace in your heart."
**Teaching Across Difference — Race and Caste**
- Jennifer Wang raised the question of how a teacher responds to a student's racial suffering when they do not share that background
- Kornfield advised honesty: acknowledge you don't have their experience, share your own experience of not being treated well, and meet the student from that place
- He cautioned against "just sit with it" as a potential spiritual bypass
- He suggested inviting the group to share what they know, then bringing the material into practice — sitting with the heartbreak, confusion, and pain that has been named
**Colonization and Embodied Resistance**
- Lavanya Devdas described how colonization lives in her body as a tightening and resistance when something feels imposed, even in a well-intentioned group
- She described a conflict between honoring her gut resistance and maintaining group harmony
- Kornfield validated her resistance as important and not to be given up
- Through guided inquiry, Lavanya identified that her heart and gut are both interconnected and part of a bigger self; voicing both brought a sense of openness

### Stories
**Ajahn Chah and the Teaching on Anger**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to his teacher Ajahn Chah
- Kornfield, meditating as a monk, was surprised to find anger arising despite his practice; he had suppressed it due to a violent and abusive father
- Ajahn Chah told him: "Good. Now you're going to learn about anger." He instructed Kornfield to go to his hut in the hot season, close the windows, wrap himself in his robes, and sit with the anger like a fire — listening to its stories, feeling it in the body, experiencing the pain of it
- Kornfield described it taking a long time to learn not to be afraid of anger and to be present for it, recognizing it carries intelligence but also tremendous pain, hurt, and fear
**The Hospice Patient — Jane**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- A woman called Jane, age 41, with two children aged 10 and 11, dying of metastatic cancer
- A hospice nurse told Kornfield: "There are no more emergencies. No matter what happens, we don't call the doctor, we don't call the hospital, we don't call the ambulance."
- The work was to be present for Jane's anger, fear, bargaining, denial, grief at leaving her children, and guilt — holding her hand, being with her with love, with no fixing possible
**Ram Dass and the Altar**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Ram Dass
- Ram Dass kept an altar with 150 pictures of saints and spiritual figures — Mother Mary, Buddha, Krishna, Kali, Mother Teresa, Amma, Gandhi — alongside pictures of Dick Cheney, Donald Trump, and other political leaders
- When Kornfield asked how he could do that, Ram Dass said: "My work is to love everybody. I don't have to agree with them. I don't even have to support what they do. But I won't throw them out of my heart."
**Women's Retreats and Men's Retreats**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- Approximately 40 years prior, Kornfield helped facilitate the first women's retreats in his community after years of mixed retreats
- Some men were scared or felt it was against the Dharma of inclusion; one man tried to sneak in dressed in women's clothing
- The women's retreats proved profoundly important — women needed space to tell their stories, be sisters to one another, and share the possibilities and struggles of their lives
- Men's retreats followed, where men discovered they were rarely in close, honest company with other men outside of sports or business; deep questions about fatherhood, masculinity, money, sexuality, and justice opened up
**Peace Work in Palestine and Israel**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- Kornfield described spending time in Palestine and Israel working with former combatants and bereaved mothers, and with the Sulha organization bringing Palestinian and Israeli teens together
- Former combatants said: "We will no longer fight one another. We're no longer going to see each other as enemies. We have to stop killing our children. We have to be brothers."
- He also referenced peace work in Burma and Cambodia
**Teaching at Apple and Tech Conferences**
- Shared by Jack Kornfield
- Used as a teaching moment when audio problems interrupted the session
- Kornfield described experiencing tech problems while teaching senior Apple staff and at a large Silicon Valley tech conference, normalizing the experience for student teachers
