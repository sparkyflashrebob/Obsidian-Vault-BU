### Outline
### Introduction to Embodiment as the Foundation of Mindful Awareness

- Embodiment is described as the very ground of all mindfulness teaching, serving as a direct portal to wisdom and full aliveness.

- Awakening in the body is presented as the pathway through which practitioners can directly perceive the nature of reality, including how things change and the formless presence behind that change.

- Mindful awareness of bodily sensations and senses is identified as the source from which love, compassion, and aliveness naturally arise.

### Guided Meditation on Bodily Presence and Collective Heart Space

- Participants are invited to turn attention inward, soften with the out-breath, and open to the field of sensations present in the body.

- Awareness is directed toward tingling, vibrating, pressure, weight, lightness, and flow, with attention to how everything is constantly changing.

- A background presence that is aware is highlighted as existing beneath the changing flow of sensations.

- Attention is then brought to the heart space, inviting participants to sense numbness, sensitivity, or vulnerability as they feel into a collective heart.

- The meditation extends outward to hold with care those affected by anti-Asian violence, particularly during the pandemic, and more broadly all those who have been targeted or violated in society.

### The Default Brain and the Tendency to Leave the Body

- Human brains have a default setting that, when attention is unoccupied, continuously scans the future or past, solves problems, and generates plans and worries.

- Stress and anxiety accelerate this mental activity, pulling awareness further from present-moment bodily experience.

- Poet and philosopher John O'Donohue is quoted to illustrate that bodies inherently belong to life and spirit, while it is the mind that makes life feel homeless.

- Teaching about embodiment is framed as a homecoming, an invitation for students to return to what is already here.

### Trauma, Safety, and the Challenge of Feeling the Body

- For many students, feeling the body does not feel like home and can be traumatizing, unsafe, or deeply unpleasant.

- Real mindfulness of the body can only be cultivated when a person feels safe enough to begin the practice.

- Guidance must offer a range of options to respect individual sensitivities, such as keeping eyes open, using a downcast gaze, or finding a grounded posture.

- Students should always be encouraged to trust their own intuition and redirect attention if being invited into the body is not working for them.

### Common Challenges Students Report with Embodiment Practice

- The most frequently reported difficulty is being perpetually lost in thought or unable to feel sensations across large areas of the body.

- Some students report being able to touch into bodily awareness but unable to sustain it, while others become judgmental and reactive when they do stay.

- Feeling overwhelmed upon entering the body is identified as a signal of trauma, requiring approaches that create greater safety.

- Resting wakefully in the changing flow of sensation without resistance is described as rare, making it essential to explore what makes sustained embodiment so difficult.

### The Body as Wilderness and the Inquiry into Self

- Entering the body is framed as entering the wilderness, a realm that is raw, intense, changing, and beyond the control of the doing self.

- Fear, love, the intensity of aliveness, and mystery all reside in this wilderness, making it both challenging and essential to inhabit.

- An inquiry into "Who am I?" within the wilderness reveals that sustained presence in the body strips away fixed identity, as the controlling self has no role there.

- Discomfort, unfamiliarity, and anxiety that arise in this space are reframed as portals to freedom and to discovering who one is beyond the constructed self.

### Informal Practices for Returning to the Body During Daily Life

- Choosing specific daily activities such as walking, washing dishes, folding laundry, eating, or drinking tea offers opportunities to check in with bodily experience.

- Recognizing the signs of having drifted into a trance and using that recognition as a cue to return, even briefly through a few breaths, is presented as a second informal approach.

- Students are encouraged to establish a personal anchor for presence, such as the breath or sensations in the hands, to support regular pausing and returning.

### Four Key Signals of Trance as Opportunities for Embodiment Practice

- Obsessive thinking, including repetitive cycles of fear, worry, and rehearsal, is identified as the first signal and an invitation to return to the body.

- The question "What am I unwilling to feel?" is offered as a powerful inquiry to accompany each return from trance.

- Habitual self-judgment and the negativity bias of perceiving that something is wrong represent the second signal, calling for an interruption through bodily awareness.

- Compulsive self-distraction and numbing behaviors, most commonly checking emails or texts unnecessarily, form the third signal, with the suggestion to pause one in four times and feel the body instead.

- Speeding through daily activities driven by an undercurrent of time scarcity is the fourth signal, with the invitation to slow down and discover that moving at half the pace yields twice the experience.

### The Cost of Disconnection and the Unlived Life

- Cutting off from the body's fear simultaneously cuts off access to the body's love and wisdom.

- Carl Jung's observation that one of the greatest influences on offspring and on oneself is the unlived life of the parent is cited to illustrate the depth of what is lost through chronic disembodiment.

- The unlived life refers not only to unexplored pursuits but more profoundly to passions, tender-heartedness, fears, and loneliness that have never been touched, expressed, processed, or transformed.

- Teaching students about the ways they leave the body is ultimately an act of love, an encouragement to come back to life, and for those with trauma, to do so slowly and with great care.

### Working with Anti-Asian Violence and Embodiment Resistance

- Yufa, an immigrant from China, shares experiencing both numbness and overwhelm when attempting embodiment practice in the context of anti-Asian violence.

- Resourcing through visualizing a loved one, specifically her mother giving a hug, is identified as a wise and effective pathway for creating a felt sense of safety.

- Deepening the physical reality of the resource, allowing warmth and the sense of being held to fill the body, is recommended before attempting to approach fear-inducing material.

- The approach of anchoring fully in the resource and then gently testing how much attention can be brought to fear while remaining stable is introduced as a preview of trauma-informed work.

- Because the mother is also Asian American and therefore a source of both comfort and fear, enlarging the resource to include a wider circle of caring beings and allies is suggested.

- True safety is reframed not as a guarantee against harm but as a spiritual refuge in which the felt sense of connection and love becomes larger than fear.

### Working with Chronic Pain and Embodiment

- Students with chronic pain face a reflexive tendency to shy away from the body because physical sensations serve as triggers and reminders of suffering.

- The equation of pain multiplied by resistance equaling suffering is introduced to highlight that it is fear of unpleasant sensations, rather than the sensations themselves, that creates the greatest difficulty.

- Naming fear directly, saying "scared" rather than "I am scared," is presented as a way to create distance from the emotion and reduce its power over experience.

- Bringing kindness and awareness to the fear surrounding pain, rather than immediately diving into the unpleasant sensations, enlarges the field of consciousness and creates more space.

- Strategies such as attending to other senses like listening or looking outward can establish a larger field from which constellations of unpleasant sensations can be touched into and out of with greater freedom.

- Deeper strategies for working directly with chronic pain are noted as a subject to be addressed in dedicated future sessions within the training program.

### The Challenge of Holding Receptivity and Action Simultaneously

- Ingo describes experiencing a tension between a receptive, open-awareness mode and an action-oriented doing mode, finding it difficult to hold both as situations speed up or grow more complex.

- When moving into action, a conceptual film forms over direct experience, replacing immediate perception with a sense of a doing self trying to get somewhere.

- Interaction with others draws so much attention that contact with open awareness is easily lost, making the integration of receptivity and engagement a fragile and demanding practice.

- The tendency to fall back into a contracted self is identified as the habit of selfing, an architectural feature of the brain's fight-flight-freeze system that reasserts itself quickly.

- Meditation is described as the practice that allows practitioners to see this contraction, occupy a larger space, and gradually extend the duration of integrated presence.

- Practicing sustained mutual presence with another person, noticing when it collapses without judgment, and returning is offered as a concrete relational training approach.

- The dissolution of the separate self into a shared "we" space is acknowledged as both deeply desirable and consistently challenged by the brain's defensive apparatus, requiring ongoing compassion and patience.

### Closing Poem and Invitation to Embodied Presence

- Rebecca Baggett's poem "Testimony," written for her daughters, is shared as a closing offering in honor of living embodied presence.

- The poem affirms the beauty of the world despite violence, environmental degradation, and personal terror, pointing to spring grass, river rocks, and autumn leaves as recurring miracles.

- All beings, from the brown recluse to the humpback whale, are named as equally necessary, situating human presence within the broader web of life.

- Pablo Neruda's vision of a great and common tenderness is invoked as the animating spirit of the session, with the observation that anyone who truly notices the world must want to save it.

- A final guided pause invites participants to breathe, feel their bodies and hearts, and sense the presence that connects all beings through that great and common tenderness.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Embodiment as the Ground of Practice**
- Tara Brach described embodiment as the very ground of all mindfulness teaching
- Being awake in the body gives rise to love, compassion, and full aliveness
- Embodiment is described as a direct portal to wisdom and to perceiving the nature of reality
- Mindful awareness of bodily sensations and senses is the central theme of the session
**The Default Mode and Leaving the Body**
- The brain has a default setting of scanning the future or past and solving problems when attention is unoccupied
- The more stressed or anxious a person is, the faster the mind moves away from present-moment sensation
- John O'Donohue is cited: the body knows it belongs to life and spirit; it is the mind that makes lives homeless
- People regularly dissociate from bodily sensations, feelings, and awareness of how they move through space
**Why Embodiment Feels Unsafe**
- For many students, feeling the body does not feel like home; it can feel traumatizing or unsafe
- It is only when people feel safe enough that real mindfulness of the body can be cultivated
- Teachers must always keep this sensitivity in awareness and offer options in guidance
- Guidance should include options such as keeping eyes open, choosing a grounding posture, and trusting one's own intuition to turn attention elsewhere if needed
**The Body as Wilderness**
- Entering the body is framed as entering the wilderness: raw, changing, intense, sometimes pleasurable, sometimes painful, and out of control
- The wilderness is where fear lives, but also where love, aliveness, and mystery reside
- The wilderness is described as a portal to freedom and to discovering who one is beyond the constructed self
- When truly staying in the wilderness, the controlling, doing self is out of a job, which can feel anxious or unfamiliar
**Formal Practices**
- Body scans are cited as a key formal practice
- Repeatedly moving attention from thoughts back into the body with kind and interested attention
- Students are introduced to these practices as part of the training program
**Informal Practices**
- Choosing specific daily activities to practice body awareness: walking to the mailbox, washing dishes, folding laundry, eating, drinking tea
- Using signs of trance as cues to return to the body, even briefly, by taking a few breaths and asking "what's it like right now?"
- Inviting students to have an anchor for presence: the breath or sensations in the hands
**Four Signals of Trance for Informal Practice**
- Obsessive thinking: cycling thoughts of fear, worry, and rehearsing; when these arise, return to the body and ask "what am I unwilling to feel?"
- Judgmental thinking: habitual self-judgment and judgment of others driven by negativity bias; use as a cue to interrupt and return to body awareness
- Self-distracting or numbing behaviors: compulsive checking of email or texts; the suggestion is to pause one out of four times and return to the body
- Speeding: moving through life quickly due to an undercurrent of "not enough time"; slowing down reveals twice as much experience
**Resourcing for Trauma-Sensitive Practice**
- When embodiment feels overwhelming, resourcing is recommended before entering the body
- Bringing to mind a loved one, visualizing them, and feeling the physical sensation of their embrace creates a felt sense of safety
- The resource should be deepened and made very physical so that love and connection fill the body
- Once well-anchored in the resource, one can experiment with briefly touching into the area of fear and withdrawing if it becomes too much
- The goal is for caring and connection to feel larger than the fear
- Enlarging the resource to include a whole circle of caring beings increases resilience
**Working with Chronic Pain**
- Fear surrounds and amplifies chronic pain; the equation offered is pain times resistance equals suffering
- Naming the fear, saying "scared" rather than "I'm scared," separates the emotion from identity and reduces its power
- Bringing kindness and awareness to the fear, rather than diving directly into unpleasant sensations, enlarges the field of consciousness
- Fear stories about duration and deprivation are identified as a major source of suffering beyond the sensations themselves
- Using other senses such as listening or looking outward can establish a larger field from which to touch in and out of unpleasant sensations with more freedom
**Receptive Awareness and Action**
- Ingo Dokker described the challenge of holding open, receptive awareness while also being in action or interaction
- In receptive mode, there is a risk of paralysis; in doing mode, attention on perceiving is lost
- Tara Brach identified this as the challenge of integration: holding receptivity while engaged
- The architecture of the brain and the fight-flight-freeze response cause rapid recontracting into a sense of a separate, doing self
- Practicing with another person, such as meditating together and noticing when the shared field dissolves, is offered as a path
- The "we" space, where the sense of a separate self dissolves, is described as fragile and requiring ongoing practice and compassion
**Checking In as a Teaching Tool**
- Teachers are encouraged to regularly invite students back to the body during sessions using brief inquiries
- Example inquiries: "Was I aware of my body as I was listening?" "Could I feel the energy in my hands, feet, belly?"
- Students can be invited to review their day and count how many moments body and mind were in the same place

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Trauma and Embodiment**
- For many students, feeling the body does not feel safe and can be traumatizing
- Common reports include: always being lost in thoughts, inability to feel sensations in much of the body, being able to touch in but not stay, becoming judgmental and reactive when staying, and feeling overwhelmed when trying
- Overwhelm is identified as a signal of trauma requiring careful, slow re-entry
**Anti-Asian Violence and Resistance to Practice**
- Yufa described experiencing both numbness and overwhelm when attempting embodiment practice in the context of anti-Asian violence
- The dual extremes led her to stop practicing altogether
- Fear for her mother, who is also Asian American, arose even within a resourcing visualization
**Chronic Pain**
- Gianna Valafuoco raised the challenge of students and practitioners with chronic physical pain
- The reflexive tendency to shy away from the body when it is a source of pain was described as almost instinctive
- Fear is identified as closely tied to and possibly a source of chronic pain
- The suffering is compounded by fear stories about duration, deprivation, and worst-case outcomes
**Obsessive and Cycling Thoughts**
- Described as a universal challenge: repeating thoughts of fear, worry, and rehearsing that pull attention away from the body
- Identified as one of the four key signals of trance
**Judgmental Thinking**
- Habitual self-judgment and judgment of others driven by a negativity bias of "something's wrong"
- Identified as a second key signal of trance and a barrier to embodied presence
**Numbing and Self-Distraction**
- Compulsive checking of email and texts identified as a common form of numbing
- Described as a way of avoiding feeling what is present in the body
**Speeding and Time Pressure**
- An undercurrent of "I don't have enough time" drives habitual speeding through daily activities
- Speeding disconnects people from bodily experience
- Re-arriving in the body after speeding is described as uncomfortable, with anxiety and restlessness
**The Challenge of Holding Receptivity During Action**
- Ingo Dokker described being torn between a receptive, open-awareness mode and a doing mode
- In receptive mode, there is a risk of paralysis; in doing mode, open awareness collapses
- As complexity or speed increases, the capacity to hold both diminishes
- The open, receptive state is described as fragile and easily broken down
**Discomfort Upon Re-Arriving in the Body**
- Tara Brach acknowledged that re-arriving in the body after being caught in a trance is often uncomfortable
- There is anxiety and a pull to keep moving forward rather than settling into present-moment sensation
- Cutting off from the body's fear also means cutting off from the body's love and wisdom
