### Mindfulness of Breath - Establishing an Anchor
📅 October 15, 2026

### Outline
### The Role of Breath Awareness as a Universal Meditation Anchor

- Steadying attention through mindfulness of breathing is a foundational element present in every major spiritual tradition and practice.

- Described as a gateway to becoming more fully present, open, and alive, breath awareness is considered one of the most universally accessible meditation techniques.

- A common early discovery for new practitioners is that the mind resists direction, wandering into thinking, planning, remembering, and judging, a phenomenon traditionally referred to as "monkey mind."

- Modern culture has further conditioned shortened attention spans, making the need for a reliable anchor such as the breath even more relevant.

- Breath is particularly well-suited as an anchor because it is always present, constantly changing in rhythm and quality, and can be returned to within a single moment regardless of how distracted the mind has become.

- Beyond steadying attention, the breath also serves as a mirror, making visible the surrounding mental and emotional landscape including moods, bodily states, feelings, thoughts, and stories.

### Locating and Sensing the Breath in the Body

- Practitioners are encouraged to find the location in the body where the breath is most easily sensed, with common options including the nostrils, the upper lip, the throat, the chest, or the belly.

- A subtle temperature difference can be observed between the cooler in-breath and the warmer out-breath, offering an additional point of sensory focus.

- For those who find it difficult to feel the breath directly, placing the palm of the hand on the belly to feel its rise and fall is recommended as a grounding and comforting alternative.

- Mindfulness of breathing is distinguished from breath control practices such as pranayama, as its purpose is to allow the body to breathe itself naturally while bringing mind, body, and heart into unified presence.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's approach involves noticing whether the breath is long or short, bringing in a quality of calming, softening the belly, and introducing a half smile to encourage ease and relaxation.

### Techniques for Stabilising and Deepening Breath Attention

- Adding a silent whisper of language in the mind, such as "calm" on the in-breath and "ease" on the out-breath, can help some practitioners maintain connection with the breath.

- Naming the breath as "in-breath" and "out-breath" or "rising" and "falling" is another supportive technique, with the instruction that the naming should occupy only about five percent of attention while the direct felt experience occupies the remaining ninety-five percent.

- Counting the breath from one to ten, backwards from ten, or in other variations, is offered as a further method to keep the mind gently engaged without becoming mechanical.

- Noticing the space or pause between the in-breath and out-breath is introduced as a more subtle instruction for practitioners who have become more settled.

- Attending to the beginning, middle, and end of each breath is presented as a way to deepen concentration and cultivate finer sensitivity over time.

- As practice deepens and the breath becomes more subtle, attention is encouraged to match that subtlety rather than pushing or forcing the breath to remain perceptible.

- Sustained breath awareness can eventually lead to a state of absorption in which the practitioner becomes the breath, accompanied by experiences of joy, rapture, and a dissolving of the sense of the body.

### The Importance of Returning Attention Without Self-Judgment

- Wandering of attention is described as a natural and expected part of breath practice rather than a sign of failure or inadequacy.

- The moment of noticing that attention has wandered is identified as a moment of awakening, representing the practitioner becoming aware again after a period of unconscious drifting.

- Each return of attention to the breath, however many times it occurs, is itself the training, steadying the mind and bringing mind and body back together.

- The image of training a puppy is used to illustrate the appropriate attitude of gentle, patient redirection rather than harsh self-criticism.

- Practitioners are encouraged to begin with very small increments such as three breaths at a time, keeping the practice inviting and free from striving or the pressure to achieve a particular state.

### When Breath Awareness Is Not Appropriate and Alternative Anchors

- Approximately twenty percent of practitioners report difficulty working with the breath, and this is acknowledged as a valid experience rather than a personal failing.

- Trauma held in the body can make breath awareness inaccessible, as unprocessed experiences may surface immediately when attention is directed to the breath, requiring a different approach before breath practice becomes possible.

- A body scan, involving a slow movement of attention through the body, is offered as one alternative anchor for those for whom breath awareness is not suitable.

- Resting attention in the general field of bodily sensation, without focusing on the breath specifically, is another viable alternative.

- Listening to sound is presented as an effective anchor because it cannot be controlled in the same way as the breath, naturally drawing attention into the present moment.

- Touch points, involving a sequential movement of attention through specific areas of the body such as the hands, buttocks, and feet, are guided as a structured alternative that provides stability without requiring breath focus.

- Common difficulties such as feeling that the breath is being controlled are addressed by first inviting relaxation, and if that is insufficient, by becoming mindfully aware of what controlled breathing feels like rather than resisting it.

### Practicing in Community and the Role of a Supportive Field

- Practicing alongside others creates a shared field of stillness and presence that supports individual practitioners in stabilising their own attention.

- The collective quality of a group sitting together with the same intention is described as something that actively assists practitioners who might otherwise lose focus or feel restless on their own.

- Teachers are identified as contributors to this field, with their own calm and meditative presence during guided sessions influencing the quality of the space held for students.

### Practical Guidance for Leading Breath Meditation Sessions

- Before beginning a guided session, attention should be given to the physical environment including temperature, lighting, and seating, to ensure participants feel held, safe, and comfortable.

- Opening instructions should invite participants to sense their surroundings first and then settle inward, grounding themselves in their seated body before turning attention to the breath.

- Setting a clear intention at the start of a session, framing the practice as a specific period of stabilising attention, helps orient participants and creates a purposeful container.

- Participants should be reminded that whatever state they arrive in, whether scattered, anxious, or upset, is a valid starting point from which to find the breath.

- Offering the option to close the eyes or keep them half open, and beginning with a few deeper cleansing breaths, supports the transition into a more settled and present state.

- The pacing and tone of the teacher's voice, along with deliberate pauses, serve as instruments for inviting calm, steadiness, and presence throughout the guided practice.

- Periodic gentle reminders to return to the breath during a session help participants who have drifted, functioning as a consistent cue to come back to the present moment.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Monkey Mind and Modern Attention**
- The mind resists instruction during meditation — it thinks, plans, remembers, judges, and wanders
- This is traditionally called "monkey mind," moving from branch to branch, thought to thought
- Modern culture has trained people toward a 15-second attention span through commercial media
- The breath is always present and only a moment away, making it an ideal anchor
**The Breath as Universal Anchor**
- The breath is ever-present, always changing, with varying rhythms, length, and temperature
- It connects practitioners to the wider world — inter-breathing with trees, oceans, and the atmosphere
- The breath serves as a mirror, revealing moods, body states, feelings, thoughts, and stories
- It is a gateway to interdependence, deep calm, clear seeing, and concentration
**Where to Sense the Breath**
- Coolness in the nostrils or tingling in the back of the throat
- The spot on the upper lip where cool air touches on the in-breath
- The in-breath is cooler; the out-breath is warmer
- Rise and fall of the chest or belly
- For those who struggle to feel the breath: placing the palm of the hand on the belly
**Supporting Tools for Breath Awareness**
- Whispering words in the mind, such as "calm" on the in-breath and "ease" on the out-breath, drawn from Thich Nhat Hanh
- Naming the breath: "in-breath," "out-breath," or "rising," "falling" — kept at 5% of attention
- Counting breaths from 1 to 10, backwards from 10, or from other numbers to maintain gentle engagement
- Thich Nhat Hanh's instruction to bring a half smile and soften the belly
- Noticing whether the breath is long or short
- Noticing the space between breaths and the beginning, middle, and end of each breath
**The Return as the Core Practice**
- The moment of noticing the mind has wandered is described as a "magic moment" — the moment of waking up
- Returning to the breath, even hundreds or thousands of times, is the training itself
- The image of training a puppy is used: gently bring it back, without punishment
- Judging oneself for wandering is identified as a common misuse of the practice
**Deepening Concentration**
- As practice deepens, attention should become quieter and more subtle to match the subtlety of the breath
- Pushing the breath arises when attention is too coarse — softening attention is the remedy
- Deep concentration brings joy, rapture, and natural delight
- The body may begin to dissolve; one-pointedness of mind and heart develops
- Eventually the practitioner moves from being attentive to the breath to becoming the breath
**Trauma and Sensitivity in Teaching**
- Approximately 20% of practitioners may find breath awareness difficult
- Causes include neurological makeup, personal attention style, and unprocessed trauma
- Teachers are called to be sensitive and offer alternatives when breath practice is not right for a student
**Alternative Anchors**
- Body scan: a slow scan through the body
- General body awareness: feeling the field of sensation without focusing on breath
- Listening to sound: cannot be controlled, brings presence, develops steadiness
- Touch points: cycling attention through specific body areas — right hand, left hand, right buttock, left buttock, right foot, left foot — and repeating
**Coherence Breathing**
- Long, slow, matched in-breath and out-breath activates the parasympathetic nervous system
- Calms the sympathetic nervous system — a rebalancing effect known to yogic traditions for thousands of years
- After coherence breathing, the instruction is to release control and return to mindful, non-directed breath awareness
**Stagecraft and Leading Guided Meditation**
- Check the physical environment: temperature, lighting, seating
- Help students become comfortable and grounded in their bodies
- Invite awareness of surroundings before turning inward
- Set an intention for the session
- Remind students to start wherever they are — scattered, worried, or upset
- Offer the option to close eyes or keep them half open
- Begin with a few deeper cleansing breaths to establish presence
- Ask students to find where they sense the breath most easily
- The teacher participates in the meditation while leading it
- Periodically remind students to return: "this breath"
**Non-Striving and Relaxed Receptivity**
- Practice is described as inviting rather than forcing
- Keeping sessions short at first — even three breaths or three minutes
- Bite-sized chunks without striving
- The things we love, we have to learn to leave alone — allowing the breath to breathe itself
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Breath as Gateway to Interdependence**
- Breathing is described as inter-breathing with the natural world — pines, redwood trees, ocean air
- The breath connects each person to the living earth and to all beings
- This framing positions breath awareness not merely as a personal practice but as a recognition of interconnection
**Monkey Mind as a Universal Human Condition**
- The wandering mind has been recognized across centuries and cultures
- Ancient teachings named it "monkey mind" long before modern psychology
- Modern media and commercial culture have intensified this tendency by conditioning short attention spans
**Waking Up Through Returning**
- The moment of noticing distraction is reframed as awakening rather than failure
- Each return to the breath is an act of presence and training
- This reframing has significant implications for how practitioners relate to themselves and their minds
**Community as a Supportive Field**
- Practicing together creates a shared field of stillness that supports individual practitioners
- Teachers contribute to this field through their own presence and calm while leading
- The need for community and encouragement is presented as a natural human quality, not a weakness
**Trauma-Informed Teaching**
- Unprocessed trauma can be held in the body and triggered by breath awareness
- Sensitive teaching requires recognizing when a student cannot proceed with breath practice and offering alternatives
- The artistry of teaching includes reading the student and responding with care
**The Art of Non-Doing**
- Mindfulness of breathing is not breath control — it is allowing the body to breathe itself
- Over-eager attention can disturb the very thing being observed
- Subtlety, softness, and receptivity are cultivated as the practice deepens
**Societal Distraction and the Need for Anchoring**
- Distraction is named as a symptom of contemporary culture
- The breath offers a counter to this — always available, always present, requiring no technology or special conditions
- Teaching breath awareness is framed as a response to a widespread cultural condition

### Stories
**The Woman Who Could Not Breathe — Jack Kornfield**
A young woman on retreat was unable to sustain breath awareness. Within 15–20 seconds of beginning, she would pop out of her body and become anxious. Jack sat with her and invited her to feel the alarm in her body rather than focus on the breath. She located the sensation around her throat. A memory surfaced: at age seven, she had witnessed her father, a veterinarian in rural Indiana, euthanize kittens with ether when the litter was too large to give away. When she later had her tonsils removed in the 1950s, the surgeon used ether, and the smell triggered abject terror. Every time she attempted breath awareness, this unprocessed trauma arose. It took considerable time of understanding and releasing before she could return to the breath.
**The Mother Waiting for Her Baby to Breathe — Jack Kornfield**
A friend of Jack's had a home birth with a midwife. The baby was born with the cord wrapped around him and was very blue. After unwrapping the cord and attempting CPR, they were far from a hospital and had to wait to see if the baby would breathe on his own. The mother, who had practiced mindfulness of breathing on retreats, said that in those moments she truly understood what it meant to pay attention to the breath. The baby survived and later ran an internet company.
**The Sayadaw from Burma — Jack Kornfield**
A meditation master from Burma with whom Jack practiced for weeks and months required Jack to report something new he had observed about the breath each day during their daily meetings. This instruction was intended to cultivate genuine curiosity and prevent the practice from becoming rote.
**Mullah Nasruddin and the Mirror — Jack Kornfield**
The Sufi holy fool Mullah Nasruddin went into a bank to cash a check. When asked to identify himself, he reached into his pocket, pulled out a small mirror, looked at it, and said, "Yep, that's me, all right." This story is used to illustrate how the breath serves as a mirror for self-awareness.
**The Zen Monk and the Stream — Jack Kornfield**
A young monk complained to his Zen master that paying attention to the breath was boring and asked if there was something better. The master grabbed the monk, dragged him to a stream running through the monastery, and held him underwater until he struggled. When he let him up, the master asked, "Boring?"
**Carmen and the Jump Rope — Jack Kornfield**
On a sunny Sunday afternoon in Seattle, a young priest stopped to talk to a parishioner and her five-year-old daughter, Carmen, who had a new jump rope. The priest demonstrated jumping rope, and Carmen gradually learned to jump on her own. She wandered off with her new skill but returned dragging her rope, saying to her mother, "I can do it, but I need lots of clapping." This story is used to illustrate the value of community and encouragement in practice.
**The Gift Card and the Deep Breath — Jack Kornfield**
A cashier at a department store was dealing with a long line of customers when a woman demanded a customized $15 gift card, though only $10, $25, and $50 denominations were available. The woman continued to argue. The clerk took a deep breath. The customer noticed and accused the clerk of being rude, saying the deep breath was disrespectful and threatening to speak to the manager. Used to illustrate that even the breath can be misread in social contexts.
**Julia Child and the Department Store — Jack Kornfield**
Jack references Julia Child's observation that much unnecessary kitchen equipment is bought by people who came in only to buy men's underwear. Used to illustrate how attention gets pulled away from its original intention.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Controlling the Breath**
- A common complaint is feeling that the breath becomes controlled or forced during meditation
- Instruction: first relax; if still controlled, become mindful of what controlled breath feels like
- If the breath feels tight, invite relaxation or shift to sound or touch points temporarily before returning
**Difficulty Feeling the Breath**
- Some practitioners cannot easily sense the breath
- Remedy: place the palm of the hand on the belly to feel the rise and fall directly
**Trauma**
- Unprocessed trauma held in the body can be triggered by breath awareness
- Symptoms include popping out of the body, restlessness, anxiety, and inability to sustain attention on the breath
- Requires sensitive recognition by the teacher and a shift to alternative anchors
- Working with trauma is noted as a topic to be addressed further in the training week
**Common Hindrances Named**
- Distraction
- Pain
- Boredom
- Sleepiness
- Restlessness
- Unfinished emotional business — things that have caused upset
- Plans, anxieties, and imagined delights
**Boredom**
- Named as a resistance that arises as the breath becomes more subtle
- The Zen story of the monk and the stream is used to reframe boredom as a lack of genuine attention rather than a problem with the practice
**Sleepiness and Restlessness**
- Both named as hindrances to be addressed further during the training week
- No specific remedies detailed in this session beyond acknowledging them and allowing them to rise and fall
**Self-Judgment**
- Practitioners commonly use the wandering of attention to judge themselves as bad meditators
- This is identified as a misunderstanding of the practice
- The moment of noticing is reframed as a moment of waking up, not failure
