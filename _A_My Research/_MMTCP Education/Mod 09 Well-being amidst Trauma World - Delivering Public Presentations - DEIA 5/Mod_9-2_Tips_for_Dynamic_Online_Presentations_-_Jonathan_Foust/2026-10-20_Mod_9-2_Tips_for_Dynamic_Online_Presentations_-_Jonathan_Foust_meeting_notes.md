### Outline
### Scope and Purpose of the Presentation

- Covered in this session are the technology fundamentals and practical tips for presenting online, based on Jonathan Foust's experience transitioning from live to online presentation.

- Excluded from this session are the broader principles of how to structure a talk, as those were addressed in a separate earlier presentation.

- Key areas to be addressed include how to look your best on camera, how to sound your best, and how to work effectively with both small and large groups online.

### Camera and Video Quality Recommendations

- Upgrading from a built-in device camera to a dedicated external webcam is strongly recommended, as the improvement in image quality is significant and the cost is relatively low.

- A webcam management app, such as Webcam Settings for Mac or the Logitech proprietary app, allows for fine-tuning of zoom, pan, tilt, warmth, and saturation to optimize the on-screen image.

- Framing choices range from wide to close-up, with a more intimate, slightly closer framing generally being better received by audiences.

- Applying the rule of thirds is recommended, where the presenter's eyes are positioned in the upper third of the frame rather than centered, as this creates a more visually pleasing composition.

- Camera placement should be roughly at eye level or slightly above to produce the most natural and flattering angle.

### Lighting Setup for Online Presentations

- Three-point lighting is the preferred approach, consisting of one light on each side of the presenter and one light behind, which adds depth and dimension to the image.

- Ring lights are a common alternative that can be effective, though they present a drawback for glasses wearers, as the circular reflection can appear visibly in the lenses or on the eyes.

- Showing some background in the frame is beneficial, as it adds visual depth, though the background should remain uncluttered and non-distracting.

### Visual Style and Appearance Choices

- Presenters have the option to choose warm, cool, saturated, or desaturated color profiles depending on the tone they wish to convey.

- A black-and-white or sepia visual style is one deliberate aesthetic choice that can create a dramatic and distinctive look, as demonstrated in Jonathan Foust's own YouTube content.

- The use of makeup is a personal choice, though it can help reduce hot spots or shine under lighting conditions.

### Audio Quality and Its Importance

- Audio quality is considered more critical than video quality, as audiences will tolerate imperfect visuals but are far less forgiving of poor sound.

- A high-quality microphone such as the Blue Yeti, ideally mounted on a boom arm, is recommended for capturing clear and professional-sounding audio.

- Lavalier microphones are also a viable option, and the choice of microphone can meaningfully affect the listener's experience.

- Presenters guiding people into meditative or non-ordinary states of consciousness have a particular responsibility to ensure their audio is as clear and supportive as possible.

- Voice training or deliberate practice in modulating tone, pacing, and delivery is encouraged as a complement to good microphone technology.

### Eye Contact and Note Management on Camera

- Thinking of the camera as a single individual rather than a large audience helps create a more personal and connected delivery.

- Placing notes or a script directly beneath the camera, as high on the screen as possible, keeps the presenter's gaze close to the lens and minimizes the appearance of reading.

- Keeping attention approximately two inches below the camera lens is generally sufficient to give viewers the sense of direct presence and engagement.

- Looking directly into the camera lens at key moments of emphasis creates a stronger sense of connection and punctuates important points effectively.

- Narrowing the margins of any reading window reduces the visible left-to-right eye movement that signals to viewers that a script is being read, which creates distance between presenter and audience.

- Using cryptic or abbreviated notes rather than a full script encourages a more natural and conversational delivery style.

### Working with Small Groups on Zoom

- Zoom is the primary platform for small group online sessions, and becoming thoroughly comfortable with its features and nuances is essential for effective facilitation.

- Screen sharing can be used to display PowerPoint or Keynote presentations, quotes, or photographs, though it should be used selectively given that many participants may be listening to audio only.

- Using a photograph during guided meditation serves as a useful visual demarcation that signals the transition into meditative practice and redirects the attention of open-eyed participants away from the presenter.

- Chat functionality during Zoom sessions can either enhance interaction and community or create distraction, depending on the facilitator's preference and the group's dynamics.

- One effective use of chat is to invite participants to submit two words describing their current state following a meditation, and then read selected responses back to the group as a way of reflecting the collective tone.

- Breakout groups are a powerful tool for integration, community building, and deep listening, following the pedagogical sequence of leading an experience, facilitating reflection, sending participants to small groups, and then returning to a large group discussion.

- Groups of three or four are preferable to pairs, as pairs can create safety concerns if participants feel uncomfortable with their partner, and odd-numbered groups leave one person without a partner when there is no assistant available.

- Groups of five offer more cross-pollination of ideas but consume more time, requiring careful consideration within the overall lesson plan.

- Establishing norms of deep listening within small groups means explicitly instructing participants to refrain from commentary, advice giving, fixing, and clarifying questions.

- Allowing small groups to manage their own timing internally, rather than relying on external reminders from the facilitator, tends to produce a more focused and respectful sharing environment.

- Pre-written prompts for small groups, including the core inquiry question, a two-minute warning, and a one-minute closing notice, should be prepared in the lesson plan in advance so they can be quickly copied and pasted into the broadcast function.

### Managing Questions and Large Group Interaction

- Two primary approaches to handling questions exist, the first being moderated questions where an assistant filters and selects submissions, and the second being spontaneous hand-raising from participants.

- Moderated questions offer greater control and allow the facilitator to steer the conversation in a direction that supports the session's goals.

- Spontaneous hand-raising introduces a quality of aliveness and improvisation, though it carries the risk of off-topic contributions or statements disguised as questions.

- Applying the improv principle of "yes and" to all incoming questions, regardless of their nature, allows the facilitator to respond constructively and find the most useful path forward in any moment.

- Listening for the question behind the question, as modeled by experienced facilitators, is a valuable skill that deepens the quality of Q&A exchanges.

- Questions are ultimately where the most relevant and alive content emerges, as they reflect what participants are actually experiencing rather than what the presenter assumes they need.

### Delivering Broadcast Presentations Without a Live Audience

- Broadcast-style presentations, where there is no interaction or Q&A, require the presenter to sustain energy and engagement without any real-time feedback from the audience.

- Pausing after a punchline or key point is unnecessary in a broadcast format, as there is no live audience reaction to wait for, and the presenter can continue at a steady pace.

- Smiling consistently and visibly, including a subtle smile at the corners of the eyes, significantly improves the warmth and approachability of the on-screen presence.

- Tone of voice, pacing, and deliberate pauses are powerful tools for communicating the quality of the experience being offered, and speaking too quickly undermines this effect.

- Mistakes, fumbles, and unexpected moments should be embraced rather than avoided, as they contribute to spontaneity and authenticity in a live or recorded broadcast.

- Incorporating errors or unexpected moments into the flow of the presentation, rather than stopping to correct them, can produce some of the most memorable and engaging content.

### Recording Formats and Technical Archiving

- Zoom recordings are convenient and sufficient for sharing on YouTube or Facebook, but the video quality is limited and may not meet archival standards.

- Recording directly in QuickTime on a Mac produces high-quality files that can be edited and repurposed in any program, though the file sizes are very large, with an hour-and-fifteen-minute session generating approximately six gigabytes of data.

- Managing large recording files requires an external hard drive and a reliable online backup solution to ensure content is preserved safely.

- Facebook Live and YouTube Live are effective for broad distribution but share the same quality limitations as Zoom recordings, making them less suitable for archival purposes.

- Pre-recording sessions in advance of a scheduled broadcast allows the presenter to record at their personal peak performance time rather than being constrained by the broadcast schedule.

### Next Steps

- Presenters are encouraged to invest in upgrading their camera, microphone, and lighting setup as foundational steps toward higher-quality online delivery.

- Developing comfort with Zoom's full feature set, including breakout rooms, chat, screen sharing, and prompts, is identified as an ongoing practice priority.

- Exploring recording formats and establishing a reliable archiving workflow for high-quality content is recommended for those producing material intended for long-term use.

- Continued practice with voice modulation, eye contact, smiling, and note management is encouraged as part of the ongoing development of an effective online presentation style.

- Additional resources will be shared by Jonathan Foust, and support is available for those seeking further guidance on any of the topics covered.
