### Mod 9-1 Tips for Preparing and Delivering Your Public Presentations
📅 October 20, 2026
👥 **Main Teacher:** Jonathan Faust | **Facilitator/Host:** Steve Lessard | **Organization:** Sounds True / Soundstreet Studios
Tags: Mindfulness Teaching, Public Speaking, Dharma Talks, Presentation Skills, Teacher Training, Meditation, Experiential Learning, Diverse Audiences, Finding Your Voice, Pragmatic Dharma
### Outline
### Finding Your Teaching Voice and Style

- Experimenting with different formats for preparing talks, such as writing word for word, using outlines, or a hybrid approach, is encouraged as a way to discover what works best for each individual teacher.

- Modeling a respected teacher closely is recommended as a foundational strategy, based on the neuro-linguistic programming principle that replicating someone's approach can help build competence before a personal style emerges.

- Jonathan described spending two solid years listening to Gil Fronsdal's teachings to establish a strong Dharmic foundation, then backfilling with personal stories and readings to develop his own voice.

- Tara Brach similarly modeled herself after Jack Kornfield for many years before her own distinct style emerged, illustrating that this process is common among experienced teachers.

- Giving the kind of talks one personally wants to hear is presented as a reliable compass for identifying one's authentic teaching style.

- Embracing one's quirks and unique qualities, rather than trying to appeal to everyone, is highlighted as the most effective way to attract an audience that genuinely resonates with a teacher's voice.

- Feedback from listeners, particularly moments when a story or teaching visibly lights people up, serves as a key indicator that a teacher has connected with their authentic voice.

- Asking the question "why is this important" before each talk is identified as a powerful tool for accessing passion and emotional authenticity, even when delivering familiar material.

### Balancing Classical and Applied Dharma in Presentations

- A tension exists between preserving the purity of classical Dharma teachings and making them relevant and accessible to modern audiences, and teachers are encouraged to identify where they personally stand on this spectrum.

- Jonathan's approach, described as pragmatic Dharma, focuses on applying ancient teachings to real and challenging life situations rather than emphasizing textual study or original translations.

- Framing talk titles around practical questions, such as how to transform one's relationship to anger or how to work with depression, is offered as an effective method for grounding Dharma in everyday relevance.

- Classical frameworks such as the Four Noble Truths and the characteristics of reality tend to weave naturally into applied Dharma talks without needing to be the explicit focus.

- Accepting one's particular orientation and directing students with different interests toward other teachers is presented as a healthy and honest approach to teaching.

### Structuring and Preparing Talks

- Beginning the preparation process by asking a series of intentional questions helps clarify the purpose, tone, and content of a talk before any structural decisions are made.

- Key preparatory questions include why the topic is important, what the teacher is most excited about the listener gaining, how the listener should feel at the end, what the listener should be able to remember, and what the call to action will be.

- Considering how the teacher themselves wants to feel at the end of a talk is described as a critical and often overlooked question that can reshape the entire approach to delivery.

- Humor can be generated by asking what is weird, hard, strange, or scary about a topic, which is also identified as a classic comedy writing technique.

- Starting to note ideas for the next talk the morning after delivering one is recommended as a best practice for keeping the subconscious engaged with the material throughout the week.

- The PARTS formula, standing for Phrase or point, stAry, Reflection, Teaching points, and Sell, is presented as a clean and reliable structure for organizing any talk.

- One complete PARTS unit takes approximately ten to fifteen minutes, meaning a thirty-minute talk typically contains two units and a forty-five to sixty-minute talk contains approximately four.

- Stories and direct experiences are consistently identified as the most memorable and impactful elements of any talk, and the principle of show don't tell is emphasized as central to effective Dharma teaching.

- A call to action at the end of a talk serves as an invitation for listeners to engage with the practice, and is considered an essential component of any well-structured presentation.

### Technology and Tools for Talk Preparation

- Going paperless using an iPad Pro is presented as a preferred approach that allows all resources, outlines, and notes to be accessible in one place during delivery.

- MindNode is recommended as a simple and clean mind mapping application for generating intuitive outlines and organizing initial ideas.

- OmniOutliner is described as a collapsible outlining program that allows sections such as poems or quotes to be hidden and revealed as needed during a talk.

- A workflow is described in which a talk begins as a mind map in MindNode, is exported as an OPML file, and is then opened in OmniOutliner for further development.

- SimpleNote is recommended as a repository for collecting quotes, readings, stories, and humor, with keyword tagging used to retrieve relevant material when preparing a specific talk.

### Delivery Techniques and Refining Presentation Skills

- Becoming too absorbed in reading from notes creates a sense of disconnection from the audience, and getting off the page as much as possible is strongly encouraged.

- The two-second rule, which involves holding eye contact with individual audience members for two seconds at a time, is recommended as a technique for creating genuine connection and keeping listeners engaged.

- Applying this rule creates a slight edge of discomfort for both speaker and listener, but results in heightened attentiveness and a more relational dynamic in the room.

- Opening a talk with an immediate story or statement rather than procedural remarks such as thanking the audience or checking the sound is described as a technique drawn from TED Talk guidance that sharpens the impact of the opening.

- Listening back to recorded talks, while uncomfortable, is identified as one of the most effective methods for identifying verbal habits and areas for improvement.

- Inviting trusted colleagues or partners to provide feedback on talks is described as invaluable for ongoing development as a teacher.

- Starting with a tightly structured talk and loosening it over time through repeated delivery is compared to how comedians refine material before a major performance.

### Presenting to Diverse and Specialized Groups

- Safety is identified as the most important precondition for any experiential learning to occur, followed by transformation through experience and integration of that experience into daily life.

- Different groups require different conditions to feel safe, and reading the room to understand what a specific audience needs is described as an essential skill.

- Corporate audiences, executives, and legal professionals tend to feel safe when the presenter establishes credibility as a content expert, which can be achieved by grounding the talk in the physiology of stress.

- Presenting the stress response, the relaxation response, the consequences of unmanaged stress, and evidence-based solutions creates a logical framework that earns the trust of analytically oriented audiences.

- Layering in brief experiential exercises and gradually extending their length is an effective strategy for guiding resistant or skeptical groups into deeper practice.

- Youth groups and marginalized communities, such as LGBTQ youth, tend to feel safe when the presenter demonstrates respect, avoids positioning themselves as an authority, and acknowledges where participants are coming from.

- Acknowledging the diversity present in a room, including naming the various subgroups represented, is described as a simple and meaningful gesture that signals welcome and inclusion.

- Awareness of whether examples or analogies used in a talk are relatable or potentially alienating to non-dominant groups is highlighted as an important ongoing responsibility for teachers.

- Recognizing the limits of what a dominant-culture teacher can offer to non-dominant audiences, while still striving to be as welcoming as possible, is presented as an honest and respectful stance.

### Sharing Personal Experience in Talks

- Bringing personal stories into a talk can be highly effective, but a clear distinction exists between material that is confessional and material that serves a teaching point.

- Positioning oneself as the fool rather than the hero in personal anecdotes is identified as a storytelling approach that tends to feel more relatable and engaging to audiences.

- Revealing one's own foibles and areas of ongoing growth with transparency is described as a strategy that builds connection and models the humility central to Dharma practice.

### Expanding Reach Through Recording and Publishing

- Recording meditations and talks and uploading them to platforms such as Insight Timer is recommended as a way to extend a teacher's reach and give students ongoing access to their voice.

- Starting a podcast is presented as a relatively accessible next step for teachers who are giving talks regularly, with LibSyn identified as a recommended hosting platform that can distribute content directly to iTunes and other venues.

- Uploading talks to YouTube is described as a valuable long-term investment, as the platform increasingly supports audio content and can generate unexpected global connections with listeners.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to begin experimenting with different talk preparation formats to identify the approach that best supports their individual style and delivery.

- Actively seeking out a role model among established Dharma teachers and closely studying their approach is recommended as a near-term developmental practice.

- Recording upcoming talks and reviewing them critically, as well as inviting feedback from trusted peers, is identified as a priority for ongoing improvement.

- Exploring platforms such as Insight Timer, LibSyn, and YouTube for publishing talks and meditations is presented as a concrete step toward building a broader teaching presence.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Finding Your Voice**
- Jonathan recommends finding a role model whose style resonates and doing exactly what they do until your own voice begins to emerge
- Jonathan spent two solid years listening to Gil Fronsdal, taking notes, ensuring the Dharmic spine was present, then backfilling with personal stories
- Tara Brach cloned Jack Kornfield's style for many years before her own personal style emerged

- Michael Port's marketing principle referenced: be as quirky and unique as possible to attract people who resonate with your voice
**Preparing a Talk — Questions Jonathan Asks Himself**
- Why is this topic important? (doorway to passion and emotion)
- What am I most excited about the listener getting out of this?
- How do I want the listener to feel at the end?
- What do I want the listener to be able to remember at the end?
- What is the call to action?
- How do I want to feel at the end?
- Humor-generating prompts: What's weird, hard, strange, or scary about this topic?
**Writing and Structuring Talks**
- Options include writing word for word, using an outline, or a hybrid approach
- Jonathan uses a hybrid: outline with some sections written in more detail
- Tara uses bold shorthand for key concepts; Jack Kornfield pulls from a pile of resources
- Jonathan starts writing ideas for the next talk the morning after delivering one, to engage the subconscious
**Technology and Apps**
- Jonathan uses an iPad Pro for writing and delivering all talks
- MindNode: mind mapping app for intuitive outlining (iOS/Mac; Android alternatives available)
- OmniOutliner: collapsible outlining program; can import from MindNode via OPML file
- SimpleNote: repository for quotes, readings, stories, and humor, searchable by keyword
**Rhetorical Structures for Talks**
- Problem-solution structure: goal, issues, severity, prevention, solution
- Skills talk: benefit of skill, examples, how to practice, take-home application
- Strategy structure: objective, strategies, advantages/disadvantages, recommended strategy
- Jonathan's preferred structure: how do I want people to feel at the end, and what are the main points?
**The PARTS Formula**
- P — Phrase or Point (e.g., "Suffering is a natural occurrence")
- A — Anecdote or Story that brings the point alive (show, don't tell)
- R — Reflection (e.g., "Has anything like that ever happened to you?" or a short guided meditation)
- T — Teaching Points (key clarifications and elaborations)
- S — Sell (reinforcing why the point is important)
- One PART unit = approximately 10–15 minutes
- A 30-minute talk = approximately 2 PARTS; a 45-minute to 1-hour talk = approximately 4 PARTS
**Delivery and Audience Engagement**
- The two-second rule: hold eye contact with each person for two seconds when off the page
- Getting off the page and engaging directly with the audience creates connection
- TED Talk tip: "Open the gate and go for it" — avoid false starts like "Can you hear me?" and begin directly with a story or point
- Starting tight with structure and loosening over time, similar to a comedian refining material on the road
**Teaching to Diverse Populations**
- Three core principles: Safety, Transformation (through experiential learning), Integration
- Nothing happens until participants feel safe
- For executives and corporate groups: establish content expertise, use physiology of stress as an entry point, layer in experiences gradually
- For LGBTQ youth: demonstrate respect, non-authority presence, and openness; allow vulnerability to emerge naturally
- Acknowledge the diversity in the room; mention subgroups present when appropriate
- Be aware that some examples or analogies may be alienating or triggering for non-dominant groups
- Acknowledge limitations as a dominant-culture teacher; recognize the power of non-dominant teachers for non-dominant audiences
**Classic vs. Pragmatic Dharma**
- Tension between preserving original teachings and making Dharma relevant and applicable
- Jonathan's style: pragmatic Dharma — applying ancient teachings to real, contemporary life challenges
- Talk titles framed as "how to" (e.g., "How to transform your relationship to anger")
- Four Noble Truths and characteristics of reality weave in naturally even in applied Dharma talks
**Recording and Distribution**
- Record meditations and talks; upload to Insight Timer as a simple repository
- Start a podcast using LibSyn (Liberated Syndication) which ports directly to iTunes and other platforms
- Upload talks to YouTube for broad reach; Jonathan receives messages from around the world as a result
**Self-Editing and Information Management**
- Reading the room: watch for glazing eyes as a signal you are sharing too much
- Replace content-heavy delivery with storytelling to make points more effectively
- Listening back to your own recordings reveals verbal habits (e.g., overuse of "really," "very," "okay")
- Invite trusted listeners to give feedback; Jonathan cites Tara Brach as a valuable feedback source
**The Shadow in Teaching**
- Attachment to outcome and the identity of being "the teacher" as one aspect of shadow
- Being part of a community of teachers helps contextualize the ego's role in teaching
- Strategy of embracing and being transparent about personal foibles — taking the role of the fool
- Seeing parts of oneself that are "not fully cooked" arising during teaching as an opportunity for transparency
**Advice to a Novice Teacher**
- Teach anything to anyone, anytime, anywhere — say yes to opportunities to gain mastery
- Jump in and learn through direct practice rather than waiting until everything is perfectly aligned
- Mastery comes through practice, evaluation, and returning to the sincerity of one's calling
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Rarity of Dharma Practice and Service**
- To take human form is rare; to be exposed to the Dharma is rarer still; to contemplate and practice it is exceptionally rare; to serve the Dharma is the rarest of the rare
- A call to action in teaching is an invitation into this rare doorway of practice
**Experiential Teaching as a Path to Transformation**
- Dharma teachers are experiential teachers guiding people into a different state of consciousness
- The goal is not information transfer but a shift in how people feel and relate to their experience
- Safety must be established before any transformation can occur
**Pragmatic Dharma as a Bridge**
- Ancient teachings that were true thousands of years ago remain true today and will remain true in the future
- The work of the pragmatic Dharma teacher is to apply these timeless principles to the "gnarly stuff" of modern life
- Both classical and applied Dharma serve valid purposes; teachers should find what resonates for them
**Authenticity and the Teacher's Role**
- Authenticity — including tears, vulnerability, and personal stumbling — signals genuine connection to the material
- The teacher who sets themselves up as the fool rather than the hero creates more personal and engaging connection
- Transparency about one's own unresolved areas can itself be a teaching
**Inclusion and Awareness of Dominant Culture**
- Non-dominant participants benefit most from seeing non-dominant teachers
- Well-intentioned dominant-culture teachers should continue to sensitize themselves to what may alienate or trigger others
- Acknowledging all subgroups present in a room signals welcome and belonging
**The Comedian and the Dharma Teacher**
- Only two populations celebrate when they go through a hard time: comedians and Dharma teachers
- Difficult personal experiences become material for teaching; suffering is not wasted
