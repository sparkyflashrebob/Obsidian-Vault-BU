### Outline
### The Role of the Teacher and the Shift from Student to Practitioner

- Completing the two-year Mindfulness Meditation Teacher Certification Program represents a significant commitment of heart, mind, and resources for all participants.

- Jack Kornfield reframes the gathering by addressing participants as teachers rather than students, marking a meaningful transition in their development.

- A story is shared about the Dalai Lama's perspective on teacher training, in which he stated that no certificate or formal recognition can make someone a teacher, and that only the arrival of students confirms one's role as a teacher.

- An encounter with an MMTCP participant who had successfully completed his practicum illustrates how meaningful impact can occur one person at a time, without requiring large-scale change.

### Overcoming Self-Consciousness and Finding Confidence as a New Teacher

- When first beginning to teach, nervousness often arises from concerns about being compared to more experienced teachers or about whether students will respond positively.

- A fundamental shift occurs when a teacher moves from asking "how am I doing" to recognizing that their role is to serve as a conduit for the Dharma rather than to present themselves.

- Borrowing teachings, stories, and approaches from respected teachers such as Thich Nhat Hanh, Sharon Salzberg, Ram Dass, and Pema Chodron is encouraged as a healthy and legitimate part of developing one's own voice.

- Just as master painters and musicians learn by studying and copying great works before developing their own style, new meditation teachers are given explicit permission to draw from established sources and make those teachings their own over time.

### Preparing a Teaching and Identifying Its Core Message

- Jack Kornfield describes his personal preparation process as involving quiet reflection, often during walks, during which he allows the spirit of what he most wants to communicate to emerge naturally.

- Rather than relying on lists or formal structures, the most important element of preparation is identifying the heart of the message, the single most essential thing the teacher wants students to feel or understand.

- The Buddha's teaching from the Anguttara Nikaya is cited, comparing the Dharma to a cool, clear pond that washes away the fatigue, feverishness, and suffering of those who enter it, illustrating the healing function of teaching.

- The core message a teacher wishes to convey may vary from session to session and can include themes such as graciousness, freedom, compassion, trust, love, or spaciousness depending on what feels most alive and true in the moment.

- Teachers are invited to reflect inwardly not only on what formal content they will present but also on what they most want students to feel and carry with them after the session.

### Adapting to the Audience and Trusting Intuition in the Moment

- A question is raised about how to balance a pre-planned message with what a teacher intuitively senses the audience needs in a given moment.

- Trusting one's own instincts and remaining flexible in response to what arises in the room is identified as a key quality of effective teaching.

- An example is shared of Ram Dass transforming the disruptive sound of fire engine sirens during a Kundalini Yoga class in Manhattan into a teaching tool, redirecting the energy as an opportunity for practice rather than a distraction.

- Flexibility is further illustrated through the example of a student sharing news of a loved one's death just before a session, prompting the teacher to incorporate that tenderness into the teaching rather than proceeding with the original plan.

### Teaching Mindfulness During Crisis and Conflict

- A participant describes her work teaching mindfulness in Farsi to students primarily based in Iran, many of whom are living through ongoing political violence, mass killings of young people, and the suppression of a freedom movement.

- The challenge of showing up as a teacher while personally experiencing the emotional weight of witnessing a crisis in real time is acknowledged as deeply difficult, including the physical sensation of shaking and feeling as though one is sitting in a war zone.

- Her teaching motto, that people are an extension of each other and that kindness is essential, is shared alongside her commitment to non-harming while also refusing to silence herself in the face of brutal government oppression.

- A parallel is drawn to Ajahn Chah's teaching during the Vietnam War era, in which he described a monastery near the Cambodian and Laotian borders as a terrain of peace, a place that reminded people of their deeper connection and humanity even as bombs fell nearby.

- The importance of creating spaces that embody peace and love is emphasized not as a retreat from truth-telling but as a demonstration that it is possible to remain centered and grounded while speaking honestly about injustice.

- A guided visualization is offered in which the participant is invited to call upon allies such as Rosa Parks, the Dalai Lama, and her own teachers, receiving from Rosa Parks the symbolic gift of a raised fist representing the courage to stand for what is right.

- The visualization concludes with the participant feeling warmth and love replacing anger, and with the entire group offering their collective support and blessings for her teaching work.

### The Teacher's Role as Empowerment Rather Than Knowledge Transfer

- The deepest function of a mindfulness teacher is described not as conveying information or techniques but as empowering students to recognize capacities they already possess within themselves.

- Looking into the hearts of students and affirming that they already know how to trust, let go, love, and access compassion is identified as the most central and beautiful aspect of the teaching role.

- Freedom, compassion, and well-being are described as the birthright of every person, and the teacher's work is to remind students of what is already true within them.

- The courage carried in one's ancestral lineage is offered as a resource that teachers can help students reconnect with, as illustrated by the image of Rosa Parks passing her raised fist to the Iranian teacher as a symbol of inherited strength.

### Integrating Nature and Eco-Dharma into Mindfulness Teaching

- A participant describes her calling to merge mindfulness teachings with nature therapy, creating a practice she calls nature-inspired mindfulness that encompasses both receiving healing from nature and giving back to it.

- The emerging field of eco-dharma is referenced, with teachers such as David Loy and John Robbins cited as influences, alongside existing programs such as Mark Coleman's nature-based training at Spirit Rock.

- Buddha Dasa's advice is recalled, in which he recommended bringing stressed and confused people out into forests, mountains, and streams and teaching them loving kindness practice, saying that the rest takes care of itself.

- Nature itself is described as a teacher, and the role of the mindfulness teacher in natural settings is framed as one of creating enough quiet for students to listen to what the natural world is already communicating.

- A participant shares a formative memory from age nineteen in the Atlantic Forest of Brazil, where the sounds of waterfalls, the smell of moist soil, and the presence of the natural world produced a profound healing experience that planted the seed for her entire subsequent path.

- That personal memory is used to illustrate how a teacher who has genuinely been transformed by an experience carries it in their body and voice, allowing others to feel it and be reminded of their own connection to the natural world.

### Naming and Positioning Oneself as a Mindfulness Teacher

- A pre-submitted question raises the issue of what to call oneself as a mindfulness teacher when one does not hold a formal degree or background in psychology.

- The term mindfulness teacher is offered as a clear, accessible, and widely understood designation that works across secular contexts including education, business, and community settings.

- The broader cultural value of mindfulness is affirmed, with the medicine of presence and loving awareness described as something urgently needed in a world experiencing widespread anxiety, divisiveness, and loss of grounding.

### Teaching Mindfulness in a World of Ongoing Collective Trauma

- The question of how mindfulness teachers can sustain themselves and their message while being buffeted by ongoing collective traumas, including mass shootings and political violence, is raised directly.

- A statistic is cited indicating that a significant proportion of Americans are living with post-traumatic stress, underscoring the scale of the challenge teachers face.

- Reducing consumption of news media is recommended as a practical first step, with the observation that news is designed to generate fear and anxiety in order to capture attention and revenue.

- Engaging in activism and community response from a grounded place of love rather than reactive anger is encouraged, with Gandhi's concept of the force of love cited as a model for courageous non-violent action.

- Spending time in nature is offered as a restorative practice that provides a different narrative from the one offered by media, reminding people of continuity, resilience, and the enduring presence of the natural world.

- The fundamental goodness of the human heart is affirmed as a truth that teachers embody and reflect back to their students, even in the face of evidence of human cruelty and suffering.

- Alice Walker's phrase "we are the ones we've been waiting for" is invoked to remind teachers that someone must choose to live and model the values they wish to see in the world.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to continue reflecting on what they most want to communicate when they teach and what internal practices help them access and sustain that message.

- The importance of moving forward with teaching from a place of empowerment, trusting that the deepest wisdom within each teacher has the capacity to awaken the same in others, is affirmed as the guiding orientation for graduates of the program.

- Ongoing engagement with the eco-dharma movement and nature-based mindfulness approaches is supported as a meaningful direction for teachers called to that work.

- Graduates are encouraged to remain grounded in their own practice and to limit exposure to distressing media in order to sustain the inner stability needed to serve their students effectively.

### Detailed Subject Matter
- **The Heart of Teaching**
  - Jack invited participants to reflect not just on what they teach formally, but on what they most want people to feel
  - The core message shifts depending on the day and what has touched the teacher: graciousness, freedom, compassion, trust, love, or spaciousness
  - The shift from "how am I doing" to "here is a great gift I want to pass on" brings relaxation and authenticity to teaching
- **Evolution of Jack's Teaching**
  - Jack began teaching 48 years ago from a background of ascetic forest monastery training and Zen practice
  - Early teaching emphasized courageous warrior striving, sitting through pain, and pursuing enlightenment
  - This approach tended to increase ambition, striving, self-judgment, and grasping
  - Over time the approach evolved toward mindful loving awareness — a different kind of courage: the courage to not turn away, to be present, to forgive, to see with compassion
  - The shift moved from "getting from here to there" to "coming from there to here" — into the reality of the present moment
- **Teaching as Conduit**
  - Jack described realizing his job was not to present or sell himself but to be a conduit for the Dharma
  - Teachers pass on tools, understandings, and things that have touched their own hearts
  - Borrowing and adapting from other teachers (Thich Nhat Hanh, Sharon Salzberg, Ram Dass, Pema Chodron) is encouraged and healthy
- **Flexibility and Intuition in Teaching**
  - Steve Kaplan raised the question of balancing a prepared message with what is intuited from the audience

  - Example given of Ram Dass adapting a Kundalini Yoga class in Manhattan to incorporate fire station sirens as free Kundalini energy
- **Empowerment as the Core of Teaching**

  - Teachers look into the hearts of students and affirm: you can trust, you can be spacious, you have deep compassion, you have the courage of your ancestors, you can love

- **Hatred Does Not End by Hatred**
  - Jack cited the teaching that hatred never ends by hatred but by love alone is healed

  - This principle was applied both to personal practice and to social and political engagement
- **The Human Heart Is Basically Good**

  - Freedom, compassion, and well-being are the birthright of every person

- **Empowerment Over Information Transfer**
  - The deepest work of a teacher is not conveying knowledge or practices but looking into the hearts of students and affirming their own capacity for trust, spaciousness, compassion, courage, and love

### Stories
- **Ram Dass and the Fire Station Sirens** — Jack Kornfield, referring to Ram Dass
  Ram Dass was teaching a Kundalini Yoga class in downtown Manhattan near a fire and police station. Every 20 minutes sirens would go off and students complained of distraction. Ram Dass reframed the sirens as free Kundalini energy — the energy of people rushing to help others — and instructed students to run it up their spines and let it open their minds to the vastness of the universe. He adapted his teaching entirely in the moment.
- **Ajahn Chah and the War Zone Monastery** — Jack Kornfield, referring to Ajahn Chah
  When Jack was living in a forest monastery near the borders of Cambodia and Laos during the war in Southeast Asia, activist friends came to confront Ajahn Chah about why they were meditating instead of acting. Ajahn Chah said wars come and go but people need a place that reminds them of their connection, love, and civility. He described the monastery as a terrain of peace — a place where you could drop your wallet and someone would return it, where people would help one another — as a gift to the world that hatred does not end by hatred.
- **The MMTCP Graduate at the Benefit Event** — Jack Kornfield
  Two nights before the session, Jack attended a benefit for Inside LA with Trudy Goodman and Father Greg Boyle. A man tugged at his elbow and identified himself as an MMTCP participant. He described completing his practicum, running a group where people came and stayed, launching an online offering, and being asked by his community to create something regular. Jack described the man's delight as a quiet but meaningful example of teaching one person at a time.
- **Bibi Rosa and Rosa Parks Visualization** — Jack Kornfield, with Bibi Rosa
  During the session, Jack led Bibi Rosa through a guided visualization in which she was invited to sense allies around her. She named the Dalai Lama, Lama Glenn, her teachers, and Rosa Parks. Jack guided her to receive a gift from Rosa Parks — which Bibi Rosa experienced as a raised fist, a symbol of standing for what is right. Rosa Parks then held her hand and communicated that things are impermanent and that change is possible, as evidenced by Rosa Parks' own life and legacy.
- **Sylvie Roeckhub in the Atlantic Forest at Age 19** — Sylvie Roeckhub
  Sylvie described growing up in Brazil and at age 19 being in the heart of the Atlantic forest, arriving stressed and filled with angst about her future. She described being profoundly healed by the sounds of waterfalls and rivers, the sky, and the smell of moist soil. She identified that day as the planting of a seed — the conviction that if people loved nature as she did, they would be healed and civilization would not need to destroy it. This experience led to her film Love Thy Nature and her work in nature therapy and mindfulness.
- **Suzuki Roshi and the Question of Enlightenment** — Jack Kornfield, referring to Suzuki Roshi
  Someone asked Suzuki Roshi whether he was enlightened. He turned to his wife to answer. She batted her head dismissively, as if to say "whatever." Jack used this story to illustrate that enlightenment is not about a special state but about the quality of presence and beginner's mind.
- **Jack's Mother and Historical Perspective** — Jack Kornfield, referring to his mother
  Jack's mother lived through World War I, World War II, and the Great Depression. She reminded Jack that difficult times are not new for humanity and that people have lived through other periods of great hardship.
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- **Bibi Rosa — Teaching During Active Political Violence**
  Bibi Rosa described shaking during the session and feeling as though she was sitting in the middle of a war zone. She described watching videos of bombings and killings of children in Iran throughout the day and night. She expressed difficulty showing up for sessions and even turning on her camera. She described the challenge of showing vulnerability to students while also trying to remain grounded and embody non-harming while speaking truth about a brutal government.
- **Christy Peoples — Collective Trauma from Mass Shootings**
  Christy raised the emotional weight of waking up to news of a mass shooting at a gay club in Colorado on the day of the session. She described teachers feeling buffeted by ongoing traumas and headlines and asked how to continue sharing a message of presence under these conditions.
- **General — Anxiety, Divisiveness, and Societal Unrest**
  Jack acknowledged that many people are experiencing the world as spinning out of control, caught in anxiety, anger, divisiveness, and racism. He noted that constant news consumption contributes to this state and is not healthy.
- **Jack Kornfield — Early Teaching Nervousness**
  Jack described getting nervous before teaching early in his career — worrying whether people would like what he said or compare him unfavorably to other teachers. This was resolved by shifting focus from self-concern to the Dharma as a gift to be passed on.
- **Early Ascetic Practice — Unintended Psychological Effects**
  Jack noted that his early teaching style, rooted in ascetic striving and sitting through pain, tended to increase ambition, striving, self-judgment, and grasping for goals rather than supporting genuine liberation. This was identified as not helpful for most people.
