### Outline
### Purpose and Importance of Honoring the Asian Roots of Mindfulness

- Buddhist teachings and practices sourced on Asian soil over 1,500 years ago have profoundly shaped the worldviews, relationships, and work of Western teachers in this tradition.

- Two crucial domains of awakening emerge when practitioners remember and honor the roots of these teachings, making this recognition especially important for those in teacher training programs such as MMTCP.

- Practices that have been removed from their cultural and geographic context can become energetically stripped of primary nourishment and deep wisdom, much like chemicals extracted from plants without the other components needed for full benefit.

- Remembering the Asian roots of mindfulness helps practitioners reconnect with a richness and depth that informs the emergence of teachings across many different cultures and contexts.

- Western white dominant cultures have a pattern of taking what they want from the earth, other species, and other peoples, and cultural appropriation violates, erases, and disempowers those from whom practices are taken.

- Many Asian practitioners and students have expressed hurt and anger at the erasure of their cultural contributions to mindfulness, and acknowledging this pain is part of the ongoing process of waking up for Western teachers.

- Failing to honor the source of these teachings separates practitioners from each other and from their own hearts, while a moment of genuine gratitude and acknowledgment opens humility and grace.

### The Spread of Buddhism Across Asia and Into the West

- The historical Buddha was born in the Northern India and Nepal area and spent 35 years teaching in that region before Buddhism spread to Sri Lanka, Thailand, Burma, Japan, Korea, China, Tibet, and eventually the West and Africa.

- Each time Buddhism moved into a new culture, it changed form, giving rise to traditions such as Chan in China, Zen in Japan, and Vajrayana in Tibet, yet all retained a deep reverence and connection to their roots.

- Buddhist temples and communities, particularly in Japanese and Chinese American communities, have existed in the United States for 100 to 150 years, yet the insight meditation community has not sufficiently acknowledged or connected with them.

- Failing to acknowledge these existing Asian American Buddhist communities perpetuates a long history of persecution, discrimination, and racism that Asian Americans have faced and continue to face.

- Honoring the roots of these teachings allows teachers to speak meaningfully in both secular and traditionally Buddhist settings, supporting people's awakening as part of a global community.

### Cultural Appropriation and the Extractive Approach to Mindfulness

- Mindfulness originates specifically from the Buddhist tradition and represents one articulation of the path taught by the Buddha, a fact that is important to recognize even as the teachings spread widely.

- Some secular mindfulness programs attribute quotes to contemporary Western teachers such as Daniel Goleman, Jon Kabat-Zinn, Jack Kornfield, and Tara Brach, while leaving actual quotes from the Buddha unattributed, presenting them as vague and mysterious sayings.

- Omitting attribution to the Buddha is intellectually dishonest and deprives students of the full information they would need to deepen their practice and understanding.

- An extractive approach to mindfulness, in which the practice is packaged and monetized without acknowledgment of its origins, reflects a capitalist mindset of taking something and claiming ownership over it.

- Mindfulness as taught in monasteries and practice centers is embedded in a culture of generosity, ethical life, and awareness of one's impact on the group, community, and family, elements that are lost when the practice is stripped from its context.

- Humility is deeply embedded in Asian Buddhist cultures and practices such as bowing, and this quality is essential for anyone stepping into a teaching role to accurately transmit the teachings.

- Practitioners who have completed a course in mindfulness may feel a sense of ownership over the material, but this perspective is broken in the same way that colonialism claims ownership over what it extracts from others.

### The Eightfold Path and the Limitations of Teaching Mindfulness in Isolation

- Mindfulness is only one component of the Buddha's Eightfold Path, and teaching it in isolation is analogous to giving a patient one eighth of a prescribed dosage and then wondering why the treatment is not fully effective.

- The broader path includes attention to livelihood, consumption of materials, alcohol, drugs, and food, and a wide range of relational and ethical dimensions that extend far beyond individual meditation technique.

- Divorcing mindfulness from a larger picture that includes something bigger than personal technique causes practitioners to miss the experience of plugging into a larger source, referred to in Buddhist terms as taking refuge in the Dhamma.

- Holding practice within a broader perspective helps dissolve the delusional sense of individual struggle that can otherwise overlay meditation and spiritual practice.

### Asian Buddhist Cultural Values Embedded in Mindfulness Practice

- Asian and Asian American cultures tend to emphasize awareness of one's impact on the group, family, and community, a value that is deeply embedded in the roots of mindfulness and Buddhism.

- Organized Buddhism in many of its forms is extremely patriarchal, and Western adaptations have made important changes such as including women as teachers, which represents a healthy evolution rather than a betrayal of the tradition.

- Asian cultures should not be essentialized or romanticized as uniformly wise or spiritual, as Asia encompasses enormous diversity of peoples, cities, rural areas, and social classes, all of which struggle with the same human roots of greed, hatred, and delusion.

- Anti-Asian violence and racism remain ongoing realities in the United States, and those who have benefited from Buddhist-derived practices have a responsibility to stand up against racism and acts of violence directed at Asian and Asian American people.

- Practitioners are encouraged to see themselves as connected to Asian and Asian American communities regardless of personal relationships, and to act as active supporters rather than bystanders, practicing love, kindness, and courage as qualities of the path.

### The Role of Devotion and Generosity in Asian Buddhist Communities

- A spirit of profound devotion pervades Asian Buddhist temples and communities, communicating a love of the spiritual life that is not transmitted when mindfulness is extracted from its cultural context.

- Visiting villages in Laos and remote Thailand as a monk, Jack Kornfield witnessed people of very limited material means offering food with deep gratitude, reflecting a culture in which the monastic life represented the highest value of compassion and ethical living.

- The great masters of Burma, Sri Lanka, and Thailand, including Mahasi Sayadaw, Ajahn Chah, Thongphulu Sayadaw, and Ari Ratana, came to the United States, taught alongside Western teachers, blessed them, and then encouraged them to find their own American way of transmitting the teachings.

- Ajahn Chah described the teachings of suffering and freedom from suffering as a universal human birthright, saying that teachers could call it by any name as long as they gave people the medicine.

- Western insight meditation teachers made deliberate adaptations including using chairs, teaching in English, and rejecting patriarchal customs, while still carrying the essential treasure of the teachings.

- Western teachers see themselves not as owners but as stewards of a treasure that has been passed on through generations of Asian monastics and lay practitioners, entrusted to pass it on to others.

### Mindfulness in the Zen Monastic Tradition

- Mindfulness as a concept was never explicitly named or discussed in Mushim Patricia Ikeda's initial Zen training, yet it was thoroughly embodied and built into every aspect of monastic life without being talked about.

- In the Mahayana Zen tradition, mindfulness is learned not through verbal instruction but through observing and copying the actions of others, making embodiment intrinsically central to the practice.

- Monastic practices are physically, mentally, socially, and spiritually rigorous by design, encompassing activities such as cleaning, cooking, eating, bathing, and sharing tight living quarters, all of which require constant mindfulness.

- Three aspects of awareness are cultivated through these forms, including interoception of what is happening within the body, exteroception of what is happening in the surrounding environment, and proprioception of the relationship between the body and objects and beings in the environment.

- Zen practice is grounded not only in sitting meditation but equally in manual work, with working with the hands considered a highly prized practice, alongside collective living, eating, sleeping, and dying together.

- Eating in the monastic context is a major set of both individual and communal mindfulness practices, with the forms designed so that a lack of mindfulness by one person creates a chain reaction affecting the entire community, illustrating interconnection.

- Full prostration practice, in which the entire body is placed on the floor, and the performance of thousands of such bows, makes it impossible to remain unaware of physical sensations, pain, thoughts, emotions, and vedana.

### The Centrality of Relationship and Community in Asian Buddhist Mindfulness

- In Asian and Asian American Buddhist communities, awareness of family, sangha, community, and all beings is so fundamental that it does not need to be stated explicitly, as it is simply the lived reality of temple life.

- Traditional temple life in communities such as the Vietnamese Buddhist temple in Los Angeles encompasses everything from community gatherings and rituals to funerals, weddings, children's education, conflict resolution, and shared economy.

- Half of the Eightfold Path is relational, encompassing right speech, right action, and right livelihood, all of which were embedded in the life of a village temple as the center of community.

- Mushim Patricia Ikeda expressed that for Asian practitioners, the most important mindfulness of all is awareness of relationships to family, clan, and all living beings, referred to as ketten.

- The level of consciousness with which one treats another person as a living being is presented as more important than the quantity or quality of one's meditation practice.

### Secular Adaptation of Mindfulness and Acknowledgment of Buddhist Origins

- Jon Kabat-Zinn and Daniel Goleman deliberately separated the language of mindfulness from its traditional Buddhist roots in order to bring the practices into hospitals, clinics, and school systems where explicitly religious language would have caused the programs to be rejected.

- Goleman designed his social and emotional learning programs so that their connection to Buddhism could not be proven in a court of law, anticipating legal challenges in school districts with strong Christian constituencies.

- Both Kabat-Zinn and Goleman have since publicly celebrated the Asian roots of their work, as it has become more socially and culturally possible to do so.

- Teachers are encouraged to feel out what language is appropriate in each setting, recognizing that the right approach depends on the context, the audience, and what will genuinely help people connect with the teachings.

- A simple and effective approach in secular settings is to verbally acknowledge that these practices come from Buddhism while making clear that one does not need to be a Buddhist to practice them, offering them in a spirit of openness and investigation.

- Mushim Patricia Ikeda shared a written script used in secular mindfulness teaching that describes the practice as a technique identical to Vipassana meditation from the Buddhist tradition in Thailand, Burma, and Sri Lanka, noting it is at least 2,500 years old and open to people of all backgrounds.

### Teaching Mindfulness in Christian and Interfaith Contexts

- Teaching mindfulness in predominantly Christian settings requires sensitivity to the enormous diversity within Christianity itself, as different denominations hold very different views and practices.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's teachings have been cited as a bridge across religious divides, including the observation that the Christian tradition's instruction to be still and know resonates deeply with mindfulness practice.

- When invited into Christian settings, teachers are encouraged to be clear about what they are offering, presenting it as an offering in the spirit of the Dhamma's come-and-see quality, inviting investigation rather than demanding belief.

- Finding common ground between Buddhist and Christian teachings, such as the alignment between the Buddhist practice of metta and Jesus's teachings on love, can be helpful, while also honestly acknowledging where the teachings differ rather than pretending they are identical.

- A growing dialogue between Christian and Buddhist practitioners is revealing significant common ground, particularly around devotion and prayer, which are qualities that are lost when mindfulness is extracted from its Asian roots.

### Teaching Mindfulness in Public Schools

- Existing programs such as Mindful Schools have already done substantial work bringing mindfulness into public and private school settings, and the openness of school districts to these programs varies significantly by region and cultural context.

- Bay Area school districts tend to be more open to mindfulness programs, partly due to the larger Asian American population and longer cultural familiarity with Buddhism in that region.

- Mindfulness practices introduced to young children, such as mindful tasting or mindful listening with a rain stick, have been observed to generate genuine joy and a sense of the universal accessibility of the practices.

- Bringing in a sense of the sacred or of interconnection in public school settings requires careful navigation, and connection to nature may be one accessible way to introduce that dimension across diverse cultural and religious backgrounds.

- Mushim Patricia Ikeda described beginning a mindfulness session with ninth-grade girls by opening a sharing circle about their stresses, which created enough safety and connection that the students shared deeply and afterward described meditation as their favorite thing.

- This example illustrates how mindfulness in the Asian tradition is fundamentally about relationship and connection, and that meditation in schools can serve as an instrument for waking up that sense of connection without requiring explicit Buddhist framing.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Cultural Appropriation and Honoring Roots**
- Tara Brach described two crucial domains of awakening that occur when practitioners remember and honor the Asian roots of mindfulness
- The first domain: practices removed from their cultural context can be energetically stripped of primary nourishment and deep wisdom, compared to chemicals extracted from plants without the other components needed for full benefit
- The second domain: Western white dominant cultures tend to take what they want from the earth, other species, and other peoples; cultural appropriation violates, erases, and disempowers
- Tara noted that Asian friends and MMTCP participants have shared hurt and anger about this erasure
- Not honoring the source of teachings separates practitioners from each other and from their own hearts; a moment of gratitude and honoring opens the heart
**The Historical and Geographic Spread of Buddhism**
- The historical Buddha was born in the Northern India/Nepal area and taught for 35 years in that region
- Buddhism spread to Sri Lanka, Thailand, Burma, Japan, Korea, China, Tibet, and beyond, and continues spreading to the West, Africa, and Latin America
- Each time Buddhism entered a new culture it changed form: Chan in China, Zen in Japan, Vajrayana in Tibet, all maintaining deep reverence for roots
- Jack Kornfield noted that Japanese and Chinese Buddhist temples and communities have existed in the US for 100 to 150 years, largely unacknowledged by the insight meditation community
**Mindfulness as Part of a Larger Path**
- Anoushka Fernandopoulay emphasized that mindfulness comes from Buddhism and is one part of the Eightfold Path
- Teaching only mindfulness without the broader context is like giving one tablet when the prescription calls for eight
- The full path includes attention to ethical life, livelihood, consumption, and recognition of mindfulness both internally and externally
- Secular mindfulness settings sometimes attribute quotes to modern teachers while omitting attribution to the Buddha, which Anoushka described as intellectually dishonest and depriving students of the full path
- Divorcing mindfulness from its larger context risks missing the aspect of plugging into a larger source, referred to in Buddhist terms as taking refuge in the Dhamma
**Humility, Generosity, and the Teacher's Role**
- Anoushka identified humility as deeply embedded in Asian Buddhist culture, expressed through practices such as bowing, and as essential for accurately transmitting the teachings
- She cautioned against a capitalist mindset of taking teachings and claiming ownership, using them for self-aggrandizement or reputation-building
- Practitioners should recognize that these teachings have been passed on through generations of Asian monastics and lay people until only the last one or two generations have included non-Asians in transmission
**Zen Monastic Mindfulness**
- Mushim Patricia Ikeda described mindfulness in the Mahayana Zen tradition as thoroughly embodied and built into everyday life without being explicitly named
- In Korean and Japanese Zen monasteries, mindfulness is learned through observing and copying the actions of others, not through verbal instruction
- Mindfulness is embedded in all daily activities: cleaning, sweeping, cooking, eating, bathing, sharing tight living quarters, and manual work
- Three aspects of awareness are required: interoception, exteroception, and proprioception
- Zen practice is based in sitting meditation, manual work, and collective living, eating, sleeping, working, and dying together
- Eating is a major set of individual and communal mindfulness practices; lapses in form affect the entire community, illustrating interconnection
- Prostration practice involves full-body bows; doing 3,000 in approximately five hours makes unawareness of physical sensations, pain, thoughts, emotions, and Vedana impossible
**Secular Mindfulness and Acknowledging Roots**
- Jon Kabat-Zinn brought mindfulness into hospitals and clinics using secular language because explicitly Buddhist language would have resulted in rejection
- Daniel Goleman's work on emotional intelligence and social-emotional learning spread to tens of thousands of school systems; he deliberately divorced the language from traditional roots to avoid legal challenges in religiously sensitive school districts
- Both Kabat-Zinn and Goleman have since publicly celebrated their Asian Buddhist roots
- Mushim provided a model script for secular settings: acknowledging that the technique is identical to Vipassana/insight meditation from Thailand, Burma, and Sri Lanka, at least 2,500 years old, and open to all regardless of background
- Anoushka recommended simply stating that teachings come from Buddhism but that one does not have to be a Buddhist to practice them
**Teaching in Christian and Interfaith Settings**
- Mushim referenced Thich Nhat Hanh's bridging of Buddhist and Christian traditions, citing the Christian phrase "be still and know" as a point of connection
- Anoushka noted that many people from Christian backgrounds attending Buddhist retreat centers have had negative experiences with institutional religion and require some translation

- Tara noted a growing dialogue between Christians and Buddhists that highlights common ground, including devotion and prayer
**Teaching in Public Schools**

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Medicine Metaphor for the Eightfold Path**
- Anoushka used the image of a prescription for eight tablets to describe the Eightfold Path; teaching only mindfulness is like giving one tablet when eight are prescribed, and wondering why the full effect is not achieved
- The path includes right livelihood, right speech, right action, right thought, and attention to consumption, all of which are relational and ethical dimensions beyond meditation technique
**Devotion and Love as Missing Elements in Extracted Mindfulness**
- Jack Kornfield described the immense spirit of devotion encountered in Asian temples and communities as something that is not transmitted when practices are extracted
- Visiting temples thronged with people expressing love and devotion communicates something that cannot be conveyed through technique alone
- He described monks and nuns in robes as representing for villagers the highest value: the possibility of living a life of compassion, ethics, and wise speech
**The Village Temple as a Complete Community Institution**
- Jack described a Thai village temple as simultaneously a meditation center, community gathering place, site for funerals, births, and weddings, school for children, community center for conflict resolution, shared economy hub, and embodiment of the Eightfold Path
- Half of the Eightfold Path is relational; all of these functions were embedded in the teachings of ethics and a wise life
**Stewardship Rather Than Ownership**
- Jack articulated that practitioners are not owners of the teachings but stewards of a treasure to be passed on to others
- Asian teachers who came to the US blessed Western teachers and then said "now you have to find the American way," trusting them to carry the teachings forward
**Interconnection and Relationship as the Heart of Asian Buddhist Mindfulness**
- Mushim stated that in Asian and Asian American Buddhist communities, awareness of family, Sangha, community, and all beings is always present and never needs to be stated because it is assumed
- She said the most important mindfulness for Asians is awareness of relationships to families, clans, and all living beings, referred to as Ketten
- She stated directly: "I don't care how much you meditate. I care about your level of consciousness of me as a person, as an Asian American. I care only about how you treat me as a living being."
**Nature as an Embedded Element of Buddhist Practice**
- Anoushka noted that 2,500-year-old Buddhism was very nature-based; the Buddha gave instructions to sit at the root of a tree
- In Sri Lankan Buddhist monasteries, three elements are always present: a statue of the Buddha, a stupa, and a Bodhi tree, the tree under which the Buddha attained enlightenment
- This connection to nature is described as essential for healing, particularly as modern industrial life has divorced people from the natural world
**The Role of Science as a Western Religion**
- Jack described science as one of the most important religions in the West in terms of what people actually believe in
- This reality required teachers like Kabat-Zinn and Goleman to translate Buddhist teachings into scientific and secular language to gain access to hospitals, schools, and institutions
- There are now over 10,000 studies and papers supporting mindfulness, which has enabled broader acceptance
**Closing Dedication of Merit**
- Mushim closed the session with a passage from Shantideva's "Guide to the Way of the Bodhisattva": May the frightened cease to be afraid, and those bound be freed. May the powerless find power and may people think of benefiting one another. May all beings everywhere be safe, be happy, be truly peaceful, be as healthy as possible.

### Stories
**Jack Kornfield — Receiving Alms in Laos and Thailand**
- Jack described going out as a monk with his alms bowl in poor villages in Laos and remote Thailand, questioning whether he should take food from people who had very little
- Villagers looked at monks and nuns in robes and offered food with deep gratitude, because the presence of monastics reminded them that it is possible to live a life of compassion, ethics, and wise speech
- He noted that a few hundred dollars, which he could have requested from his mother, represented a year's income for one of those farmers
**Jack Kornfield — Sariputra's Nightly Practice of Gratitude**
- Jack referenced the account of Sariputra, described as the wisest disciple of the Buddha, who before sleeping would sense the direction of the person who first introduced him to the teachings of liberation and turn his head that way as a measure of respect
**Jack Kornfield — Letter and Package to the King of Thailand**
- Jack described sending a large package through his teacher to the previous King of Thailand, who was beloved
- The package contained photographs of Gaia House, Spirit Rock, and IMS, along with various books
- His note expressed gratitude for the generosity of the Thai people in welcoming practitioners like himself, Joseph Goldstein, and others, which allowed the teachings to spread to hundreds of thousands and millions of people
**Jack Kornfield — Asian Teachers Coming to the US and Sending Western Teachers Forward**
- After Western teachers had trained and begun teaching, they invited great teachers from Burma, Sri Lanka, and Thailand to come to the US, including Mahasi Sayadaw, Ajahn Chah, Thongphulu Sayadaw, and Ari Ratana from Sri Lanka
- These teachers came, taught, often taught alongside Western teachers, blessed them, encouraged them, and then said "now you have to find the American way" and returned home
- Jack described this as both problematic and a little scary, with Western teachers looking at each other and saying they were on their own
**Mushim Patricia Ikeda — Traditional Funeral on the Mountain in South Korea**
- During her eight months in a South Korean monastery in 1987–1988, Mushim witnessed the traditional funeral of a nun who had lived on that mountain for a very long time
- The nun's body was cremated in a meadow designated for that purpose
**Mushim Patricia Ikeda — Singing with the Nuns in Korea**
- While working in the fields with the nuns in Korea, they would sometimes take snacks and sing together, which was technically forbidden but described as fun
**Mushim Patricia Ikeda — Teaching Ninth-Grade Girls in Oakland Public Schools**
- Mushim was asked to teach mindfulness meditation for stress reduction to ninth-grade girls at an Oakland public school her child attended
- She began with a sharing circle asking what was most stressful in their lives, expecting the girls to feel unsafe sharing
- Every one of them shared deep and heartfelt details about stresses in their homes and lives
- After meditation and more sharing, many of the girls said meditation was their favorite thing
**Anoushka Fernandopoulay — Secular Mindfulness Settings Omitting the Buddha**
- Anoushka described being in secular mindfulness settings where quotes from Daniel Goleman, Jon Kabat-Zinn, Jack Kornfield, and Tara Brach were attributed by name, but quotes that were actually from the Buddha were presented as vague, mysterious, wise sayings without attribution
**Don Farber and Rick Fields — Refuge in Los Angeles**
- Mushim described the book "Refuge in Los Angeles," with photographs by Don Farber and text by Rick Fields, documenting life in a Vietnamese Buddhist temple in Los Angeles
- Both Farber and Fields were white men who practiced cultural humility, entering the temple not to extract or exploit but to learn, with full permission of the monks and lay community to share what they found
### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
- Mushim described Zen monastic practice as physically very rigorous by design; practitioners' bodies almost always hurt due to the demanding physical forms
- Prostration practice involves full-body bows to the floor; performing 3,000 prostrations in approximately five hours makes it impossible to be unaware of physical sensations, pain, thoughts, emotions, and Vedana, described as mostly unpleasant and sometimes kind of pleasant
- Sitting meditation in Zen is described as sitting like rocks, remaining very still and very silent for long periods of time
- Working in the fields is described as not romantic; positions include hands and knees for extended periods
- Bathing was infrequent in the monastic setting Mushim described
- Sharing tight living quarters with others requires constant mindfulness of the space and of others' movements
- Anoushka noted that organized Buddhism in many of its forms is extremely patriarchal, and that three of the four panelists, the women, might not have been included in a more orthodox Buddhist presentation
- Tara Brach acknowledged that she is still in process regarding her own waking up to the impact of cultural appropriation and the hurt it causes Asian practitioners and friends
