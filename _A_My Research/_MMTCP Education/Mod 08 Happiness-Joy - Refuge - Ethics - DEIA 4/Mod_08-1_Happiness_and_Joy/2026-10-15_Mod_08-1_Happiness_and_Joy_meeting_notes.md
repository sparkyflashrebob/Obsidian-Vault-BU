### Outline
### Buddhism and the Misunderstanding of Suffering as a Grim Path

- Often mischaracterized as focused solely on suffering, Buddhism is frequently ranked low in appeal when first encountered, particularly due to its outward appearance of solemnity and slow, joyless practice.

- A classic teaching story illustrates this misunderstanding, in which a monastery had been copying religious texts from copies rather than the original manuscript for centuries, and when the abbot finally consulted the original, he discovered the word had always been "celebrate" rather than "celibate."

- This story serves as a reminder that the essence of the spiritual path has always included joy and celebration, which is often lost through layers of misinterpretation.

### The Buddhist Understanding of Genuine Happiness as a Core Teaching

- Equanimity, as described in Buddhist teaching, creates a space of freedom that holds room for both the ten thousand joys and the ten thousand sorrows of life.

- The Buddha explicitly stated that he would not teach that genuine happiness was possible unless it truly were so, emphasizing that this potential is real and accessible.

- Happiness, as understood in this context, comes in many flavors, including the happiness of deep peace, expansive joyfulness, and a quiet, contented sense of well-being and glow.

- The Dalai Lama consistently affirms in his teachings that every person wants to be happy and that nobody wants to suffer, making happiness a universal and central aspiration of the human condition.

### Addressing the Question of Teaching Happiness Without Feeling It

- A common concern among meditation teachers is whether one can authentically teach about happiness while personally experiencing depression or a lack of access to joy.

- Inviting students to check in and measure their own sense of happiness often reveals that many people feel they have limited access to it in their daily lives.

- What matters most in teaching is not the teacher's current emotional weather system but rather a sincere trust in the potential of the path and an authentic engagement with that potential.

- Cultural and biochemical influences, including widespread depression and anxiety reflected in societal reliance on medications, shape the emotional baseline of many practitioners and teachers alike.

### Two Types of Happiness in Buddhist Psychology

- Buddhist psychology distinguishes between two fundamental kinds of happiness, providing a useful framework for organizing teachings on the subject.

- Pamoja refers to conditioned happiness, a worldly happiness linked to changing sensory conditions such as physical pleasure, meaningful compliments, relational harmony, or professional gratification.

- Sukha refers to unconditioned happiness, a happiness without a cause that is not dependent on any external condition and represents a state of inner freedom.

- Conditioned happiness, though fleeting, is considered wholesome and valuable because it helps decondition the negativity bias and creates an atmosphere conducive to the deeper, unconditioned happiness of sukha.

- Holding conditioned happiness lightly, as expressed in William Blake's phrase "kiss the joy as it flies by," reflects the open-handed quality that makes it genuinely nourishing rather than a source of grasping.

### The Two Primary Blocks to Happiness

- Two interconnected blocks to happiness are commonly identified when practitioners are invited to reflect on what stands between them and a sense of well-being.

- The first block is a chronic sense that something is missing, often expressed as the "if-only mind," in which happiness is perpetually deferred to a future condition such as better health, a different relationship, or greater financial security.

- Shining the light of awareness on the "if-only mind" is a valuable practice because it makes visible the habitual deferral of happiness and opens the possibility of questioning it.

- The second block is the "something's wrong" mode, rooted in the evolutionary negativity bias, which manifests as chronic worry, anxiety, and a habitual assumption that a problem is always present or approaching.

- A poem by Hafiz captures this dynamic by contrasting the saint who has surrendered to joy with the ordinary person who still believes they have a thousand serious moves to make, illustrating how a mental map of obstacles and future goals creates a tensing that blocks happiness.

### Mindfulness as a Foundation for Awakening to Happiness

- Mindfulness is identified as the primary ground for addressing the blocks to happiness, as bringing awareness to the sense that something is missing or wrong begins to dissolve the identity built around those narratives.

- When the light of awareness is brought to contracted states, the sense of "something bad is happening to me" begins to soften and more inner space opens up.

- Two complementary approaches to awakening happiness are mindfulness of what is arising and the intentional shifting of attention toward what gladdens and brightens the mind.

- Repeated practice of worry and anxious thinking habituates the body and mind to those states, while intentionally turning attention toward happiness gradually makes that orientation a new habit.

### Choosing Happiness as an Intentional Practice

- Happiness research consistently identifies choosing happiness as a common denominator among those who consider themselves and are experienced by others as genuinely happy.

- An anecdote illustrates this point, in which a person was asked what it would feel like to expect something good to happen today rather than something bad, and responded that it would feel "totally weird and uncomfortable," to which the reply was "good, then try it."

- Catholic mystic Henri Nouwen affirmed that joy does not simply happen but must be chosen and re-chosen every day, acknowledging the evolutionary pressure that works against this choice.

- A loving-kindness reflection using the phrase "May I feel happy" is offered as a first step in cultivating happiness, inviting practitioners to notice whether the phrase evokes grasping, unworthiness, sadness, or self-kindness.

- Directing attention intentionally toward what gladdens the mind reconnects practitioners with a larger sense of being, making them more whole and integrated rather than suppressing difficult emotions.

### Practical Ways to Gladden the Mind and Cultivate Happiness

- Physical activity such as running, yoga, tai chi, and qigong are identified as ways to shift the body-heart-mind toward greater happiness through direct physiological effects.

- Using the image and felt sense of a smile in meditation is a powerful tool for reconnecting with the innate capacity for benevolence, openness, and open-heartedness.

- Gratitude practice is highlighted as one of the most reliable ways to gladden the mind, with the simple act of finding one object to appreciate and expanding from there until a sense of tenderness and thankfulness fills the heart.

- Meister Eckhart's teaching that "thank you" is sufficient as a lifelong prayer reflects the profound power of gratitude to bring practitioners home to themselves.

- Acts of service and generosity are identified as another means of gladdening the mind, with Henri Nouwen noting that every step toward generosity is a movement from fear to love, even when the feeling of generosity is not initially present.

- Learning to savor simple joys, whether holding a child, listening to music, or hearing rain, by pausing and fully taking in what is beautiful and good, is presented as an essential practice for cultivating happiness.

- The story of Kabir the shoemaker, who repeated the divine name Ram for twenty years simply out of love for the repetition rather than desire for any outcome, illustrates the quality of pure savoring that sustains deep happiness.

### Unconditioned Happiness and the Transformation of Relationship to Life

- Sukha, unconditioned happiness, arises when there is no resistance or grasping, described by Anthony de Mello as "absolute cooperation with the inevitable."

- Wisdom recognizes that hitching happiness to any condition creates a roller coaster, and from that wisdom the grip on conditions begins to loosen naturally.

- A story about a Tibetan lama with crippling arthritis who laughs joyfully and says "of course I'm happy, it's wonderful, especially when I have no choice" exemplifies the freedom of heart that comes from full acceptance of circumstances.

- Nietzsche's observation that happiness requires very little, the lizard rustling, a breath, a glance, points to how unconditioned happiness is available in the smallest and most ordinary moments.

- Naomi Shihab Nye's poem describes happiness as something that floats, needs nothing to hold it down, and flows out of a person into everything they touch, reflecting the non-possessive and expansive nature of genuine happiness.

- Moments of happiness, when investigated, reveal a quality of presence underlying them, suggesting that it is not the external object but the presence itself that is the true source of joy.

### The Role of Joy in Teaching Meditation and Mindfulness

- Jack Kornfield references the dialogue between Archbishop Tutu and the Dalai Lama as a powerful example of how joy can be sustained through immense suffering, with the Dalai Lama asking why he should let those who have taken so much from him also take his happiness.

- Buddhist psychology lists twenty-five distinct kinds of happiness and joy, including thrilling bliss, tingling joy, lightness of being, and luminosity, offering a richly differentiated map of positive states that contrasts with Western psychology's focus on the negative and diagnostic.

- When teaching meditation, it is important to tune students, typically from the second, third, or fourth class onward, to actively look for moments of well-being, contentment, ease, and joy rather than treating the practice as a struggle.

- Equally important is instructing students not only to notice moments of happiness but to dwell in them, allowing the well-being to fill the body, touch the cells, and make embodied changes, counteracting the negativity bias that can turn meditation into effort.

- A systematic training in joy meditation exists, including practices of wishing joy to others by picturing them as a young child on their happiest day, recognizing that the joyful spirit of that child remains inviolable within every person regardless of what has happened to them.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are encouraged to trust and communicate the genuine potential for happiness on the spiritual path, regardless of their own current emotional state.

- Practitioners are invited to begin incorporating intentional practices of gratitude, savoring, service, and loving-kindness as regular means of gladdening the mind and creating conditions for unconditioned happiness.

- Meditation teachers are encouraged to introduce joy-focused instructions early in their teaching, guiding students to notice and dwell in moments of well-being as a core part of mindfulness training.

- Each teacher is encouraged to identify and engage in personal practices that nourish their own spirit and joy, recognizing this as an essential part of what they bring to others.

- Further exploration of systematic joy meditation training is recommended, with reference to the book "The Art of Forgiveness, Loving Kindness, and Peace" as a resource for structured practice.

### Detailed Subject Matter

**Two Types of Happiness in Buddhist Psychology**

- Sukha: unconditioned happiness; happiness without a cause; not dependent on anything; a place of freedom that can be with everything
- Pamoja is taught first because enjoying wholesome pleasures helps decondition the negativity bias and creates an atmosphere conducive to sukha
- Buddhist psychology lists 25 kinds of happiness including thrilling bliss, tingling joy, lightness of happiness, and luminosity
**Blocks to Happiness**
- The "something's missing" side: a chronic sense that life is supposed to be something else; the "if-only mind" — if only I felt better, if only this relationship were different, if only I had financial security
- The "something's wrong" side: the negativity bias, an evolutionary tendency to fixate on problems; expressed as worry, anxiety, and chronic figuring out
- Living with a mental map of always being on the way somewhere, tensing against potential obstacles around the corner
- The sense of a thousand serious moves, as opposed to the saint who surrenders and trips over joy
**Mindfulness as Ground for Happiness**
- When awareness is brought to the sense that something is missing or wrong, identity starts dissolving and more space opens up
- Mindfulness allows recognition of the weather system — grief, anger, depression, sadness, excitement — without being defined by it
- The potential of the path matters more than the current weather system one is in
**Intentionally Shifting Attention**
- Where attention goes, energy flows; choices can be made to direct attention toward what gladdens the mind
- Practicing worry thoughts habituates the body to anxiety chemistry; practicing turning toward happiness builds that as a habit instead
- Each way of directing attention toward happiness reconnects with a larger sense of being and wholeness
- Not about getting rid of sorrow but becoming larger and more integrated
**Choosing Happiness**
- Happiness research shows a common denominator among those who consider themselves happy is that they choose happiness
- Henri Nouwen: joy does not simply happen; it must be chosen and kept choosing every day

- Loving-kindness phrase "May I be happy / May I feel happy" as a first step in cultivating the intention toward happiness
**Gratitude Practice**
- Gratitude shifts identity and creates a sense of opening

- Mentally whispering "thank you" while bringing to mind someone easy to love as a simple taste of intentional attention shift
- Meister Eckhart: if the only prayer ever said is "thank you," it will be enough
**Serving and Acts of Kindness**
- Small acts of kindness reconnect us to happiness even when we don't feel generous
- Henri Nouwen: every step in the direction of generosity is a movement from fear to love
**Savoring**
- Learning to pause and take in what is beautiful and good — holding a child, listening to music, the sound of rain

### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Misunderstanding of Buddhism as Grim**
- The perception of Buddhism as focused only on suffering is one of the great misunderstandings of the path
- The slow walking at retreats, the copying of texts — the outer form can obscure the inner invitation to celebrate life
**Cultural Conditioning and Mood**
- Western culture has a chemical cocktail influencing collective mood — the United States of Prozac, then the United States of Xanax — reflecting prevalent cultural moods of depression and anxiety
- Western psychology tends to focus on the medical, the negative, the problematic, and the diagnostic, whereas Buddhist psychology opens the whole realm of human possibility including 25 kinds of happiness
**The Negativity Bias**
- An evolutionary tendency that is no longer so useful in its chronic form
- Fixating on what seems to be a problem is a habit of assuming there is a problem
- Gratitude and intentional attention shift help decondition this bias
**Freedom of Heart as the Deepest Expression**
- Sukha, unconditioned happiness, is described as the freedom of heart to be with the living-dying world
- The lama with crippling arthritis who laughs and says "of course I'm happy, it's wonderful, especially when I have no choice" is offered as an expression of this freedom
- The Dalai Lama's question — why should I let them take my happiness — after the destruction of Tibetan culture illustrates happiness as an act of inner sovereignty
- Archbishop Tutu's survival of apartheid and the Dalai Lama's survival of cultural destruction are presented as exemplars of joy amid tremendous suffering
**Presence as the Source of Happiness**
- Investigating moments of happiness reveals that what allows happiness is a quality of presence, not the external object or person
- The night sky takes no credit for the moon but continues to hold it and share it — happiness flows out into everything touched when one is at home and present
**Joy as a Human Possibility Across Conditions**
- People in great poverty or refugee camps can be joyful — it is an extraordinary human possibility
- Joy is not reserved for comfortable conditions; it is available as an inner orientation

### Stories
**The Monk and the Word "Celebrate"**
Tara Brach shares a classic teaching story. A new monk in a monastery is assigned to help copy old canons and laws by hand. He realizes they are copying from copies, not the original, and raises this with the abbot. The abbot agrees it is a good point and goes down to the dark caves to examine the original manuscript, which has been locked in a vault for hundreds of years. Hours pass and the young novice goes to find him. He discovers the abbot banging his head against the wall and crying uncontrollably. When asked what is wrong, the abbot says: the word was "celebrate."
**The Facebook Post — 14 Years of Gratitude**
Tara Brach shares an email she received that morning from a friend who is part of the training. The friend posted on Facebook: 14 years prior, they sat handcuffed in the back of a police cruiser in blazing Texas heat, praying to be taken on the spot because they believed their life was over. They did not get sober by choice and the path of recovery has not been easy or perfect. But the view is very different today and they are grateful for all of it. "It's all beautiful. Thank you."
**Kabir the Shoemaker and Ram**
Tara Brach shares the story of Kabir, a shoemaker who repeated the sacred mantra "Ram" as he worked every day for twenty years. One day Ram appears and asks why Kabir has been calling him. Ram asks what Kabir wants. Kabir says he doesn't want anything. Ram asks why he has been repeating his name. Kabir says: "I just love repeating your name." From then on, wherever Kabir went, he was followed by Ram.
**The Lama with Crippling Arthritis — The Snow Leopard**
Tara Brach references Peter Matthiessen's book The Snow Leopard. Matthiessen visits a lama with crippling arthritis living in an isolated region of Tibet, knowing he will never be able to leave again. When asked through a translator how he feels about this, the lama laughs out loud with big white teeth shining, indicates his twisted legs without a trace of self-pity or bitterness as if they belonged to all of them, casts his arms wide to the sky and the snow mountains and the high sun and the dancing sheep, and cries: "Of course I'm happy, it's wonderful, especially when I have no choice."
**The Dalai Lama and Archbishop Tutu — The Book of Joy**
Jack Kornfield references a book published the prior year documenting a week-long dialogue between the Dalai Lama and Archbishop Desmond Tutu. The central question was how they could live through so much suffering — the destruction of Tibetan culture, the burning of texts, the loss of monasteries under the Dalai Lama's lifetime, and the apartheid, conflict, killing, and suffering of people close to Tutu. Both are described as exemplars of joy. The Dalai Lama says: they have taken so much from me — the ability to worship, parts of our culture, texts, so much — why should I let them take my happiness?
**The Doctor and the Chopin**
Jack Kornfield shares a personal story. When his daughter was born, he was at the hospital and spoke with the doctor about what he did for a living. The doctor confided he wished he had been a musician because he loved to play concert piano. After the successful emergency cesarean, another doctor told the physician it had been an honor to assist him. Jack turned to the physician and asked whether he could honestly say he wished he had been a musician after helping bring a new life into the world and saving another. The doctor nodded and said it went pretty well, then said he knew exactly why — because that morning he had gotten up early and played Chopin at the piano for an hour.
**The Friend and the Question About Something Good**
Tara Brach shares a story about a friend in Washington who is a soldier. Her friend asked her: "What would it feel like for you to think that something good was going to happen today?" rather than something bad around the corner. The friend responded: "Totally weird and uncomfortable." Her friend said: "Good, then try it."
