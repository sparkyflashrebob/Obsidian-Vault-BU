### Outline
### Happiness Through Integrity as a Foundation of Mindfulness Teaching

- Framed as a follow-up to prior sessions on equanimity, joy, and well-being, this session introduces the happiness that arises from integrity as a critical and often overlooked dimension of mindfulness teaching.

- Often conflated with cultural debates, religious commandments, or moralistic frameworks in Western culture, integrity is better understood in the mindfulness tradition as a universal law of human happiness.

- Rather than being rooted in concepts of sin or moral judgment, the principle of integrity mirrors the physician's first directive of doing no harm, serving as a practical foundation for a free heart and a free mind.

- Understood through the Pali compound "sati sampajenya," mindfulness encompasses both the loving awareness of what is happening and a mindful, intentional response to circumstances.

- Even brief moments of mindfulness create a gap before action, and within that gap lies the doorway to freedom, happiness, and the capacity to discern whether one's actions are harmful or beneficial to oneself and others.

### The Absence of Ethics in Western Psychology and Its Consequences

- Notably absent from much of Western psychotherapy, the dimension of ethics and integrity represents a significant gap in how the mind and well-being are addressed in clinical practice.

- When clients present with behaviors such as secret affairs or financial dishonesty, conventional therapy tends to explore motivation and emotional emptiness without directly addressing whether those behaviors produce happiness or harm.

- Posing direct questions such as whether a person sleeps well, feels anxious, or risks destroying their relationships brings the ethical dimension into focus without moralizing.

- Across virtually every culture, actions such as stealing, lying, and harming others are recognized as destroyers of happiness, pointing to a near-universal understanding of integrity as foundational to well-being.

- Attempting to pursue liberation of the heart without the basis of integrity is likened to rowing across a stream while the boat remains tied to the dock, illustrating that ethical grounding is a prerequisite for deeper practice.

### The Power of Integrity as a Moral Force

- Illustrated through the story of Chinese dissident Mr. Liu Jing, who endured eleven years of imprisonment and physical hardship rather than sign a false confession, integrity is shown to be a force more powerful than the desire for outer freedom.

- What prevented him from signing was the presence of his family and friends in his mind, representing an inner moral compass rooted in truth and relational care.

- This kind of integrity produces a form of happiness that no other source can provide, and its effects extend beyond the individual to benefit the surrounding community.

- Imagining a world in which even one precept, such as refraining from killing or committing to truthfulness, were widely observed illustrates how profoundly integrity shapes collective human experience.

- Described as something embedded in the DNA of human beings across indigenous and diverse cultures worldwide, the impulse toward honest speech and non-harm is presented as a near-universal moral foundation rather than a culturally relative construct.

### Conscience, Moral Intuition, and the Natural Basis of Integrity

- A psychological experiment involving buckets of water at different temperatures is used to illustrate how the nervous system can be confused by relative perceptions, contrasting this with the clarity felt when in the presence of an honest and upright person.

- Being with someone of integrity is described as a gift, and a Buddhist saying compares the perfume of integrity to sandalwood and jasmine, noting that it rises even to the heavens.

- Conscience is identified in Pali and Sanskrit as a quality born into human beings, functioning as an inner compass that most people carry from the very beginning of life.

- While sociopaths and those affected by trauma or injury may not operate from this compass, the majority of people possess an innate sense of right and wrong that, when acknowledged within mindfulness practice, brings ease, clarity, courage, and happiness.

- Bringing this natural moral awareness into the field of mindfulness is itself an expression of loving kindness and care for both oneself and others.

### Navigating Ethical Dilemmas as a Mindfulness Teacher

- As teachers of mindfulness, participants will encounter students facing complex moral and ethical dilemmas that cannot be resolved through a cookie-cutter approach.

- Illustrated through the story of Zen Master Katagiri Roshi, who responded to his students' dilemma about cockroaches in the kitchen by refusing to give an answer and leaving the decision in their hands, the teaching points to the necessity of wrestling personally with ethical questions.

- This approach reflects that integrity, virtue, and care for one another are not simply rules to be applied but qualities that must be lived and discerned from within.

- Described as the crowning glory of human beings, integrity reduces regret and diminishes the social need for enforcement mechanisms such as guards, prisons, and weapons.

- A study correlating toll payment compliance among therapists at an Illinois mental hospital with the rate of patient improvement is cited as evidence that a therapist's personal integrity directly influences therapeutic outcomes.

### The Five Mindfulness Trainings as Ethical Foundations for Practice

- Adapted from Thich Nhat Hanh's formulation, the first training involves cultivating compassion and protecting the lives of people, animals, and plants, rooted in a reverence for life and a commitment to minimizing harm in all actions.

- Chief Seattle's observation that what happens to the beasts happens to humanity is invoked to underscore the interconnectedness of all life and the importance of this training at the present moment in history.

- The second training addresses the suffering caused by exploitation, social injustice, stealing, and oppression, framing non-stealing not merely as refraining from taking but as a broader practice of stewardship, non-greed, and awareness of power differentials.

- William Faulkner's call to always refuse to bear injustice, outrage, dishonor, and shame regardless of personal gain illustrates the moral force embedded in this second training.

- The third training concerns sexual conduct, calling for responsibility over one's own sexual energies and protection of the safety and integrity of individuals, couples, families, and society, with particular emphasis on the power differential that arises in the teacher-student relationship.

- Dean Allen Jones' observation that spiritual experience can lead to inflation, and that even deeply peaceful individuals may still react disproportionately to minor frustrations, is offered as a reminder of the humility required in this area.

- The fourth training focuses on mindful speech and deep listening, calling practitioners to be faithful to the truth, to avoid harmful gossip, and to recognize that the people and things one must lie about come to exert ownership over one's life and family.

- A story of a Japanese American woman who stopped graffiti in her workplace simply by posting a humble, sincere sign asking people not to desecrate her place of work illustrates the quiet power of honest and genuine communication.

- The fifth training addresses mindful consumption, calling for care in eating, drinking, and consuming so as not to cloud the mind or enable harm to oneself or others, with reference to the widespread suffering caused by alcohol and drug addiction across millions of families.

### Teaching Ethics and Integrity Within a Mindfulness Context

- Participants are invited to reflect on which of the five trainings they would find easiest or most difficult to teach, which are hardest to practice personally, and what language they would use when presenting these concepts.

- Tara Brach notes that much of the suffering visible in contemporary society is partly attributable to decades of silence around living with integrity, and that the culture has become increasingly relativistic even at an intellectual level.

- A hesitancy to use the language of ethics, virtue, or morality in teaching is acknowledged, arising from awareness that many students already carry a heavy burden of self-judgment and a sense of falling short.

- Teachers are encouraged to remain attentive to who is listening and how they might be receiving teachings on integrity, given that most people already carry difficult feelings about their own dishonesty, overconsumption, or harmful speech.

- In traditional Buddhist monastic settings, lay practitioners recite the precepts regularly not as binding vows but as intentions and reminders, a framing that aligns with Mother Teresa's distinction between being called to be successful and being called to be faithful.

### Next Steps

- The group is expected to consider how to integrate integrity and ethics into mindfulness teaching in a way that is compassionate, non-moralistic, and sensitive to the emotional state of students.

### Stories
**The Chinese Dissident — Mr. Liu Jing**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. Mr. Liu Jing served 11 years in Weinan No. 2 prison in Shaanxi province. He was forced to sit on a tiny wooden stool without moving for 10 hours a day and was beaten if he moved or spoke to other prisoners. To end his suffering, all he needed to do was sign a statement saying he had made mistakes in his thinking, without naming others. Against all odds, he refused. Each time he considered signing, he saw the faces of his family and friends before him and could not sign. His refusal is offered as an example of moral force and integrity as a more powerful motivator than outer freedom.
**The Psychology Conference Presentation**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. He recounts presenting at a large psychology conference decades ago on the topic of teaching ethics and integrity to clients. He described working with clients having secret affairs or embezzling money, and engaging them not moralistically but by asking whether their actions made them happy, whether they slept well, and whether their behavior might destroy their marriage or reputation. He described this as explaining "how the game is rigged."
**The Psych 101 Hot and Cold Water Experiment**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. A student is invited to the front of a class where there are three buckets of water — one very hot, one with ice, and one at room temperature. The student plunges both hands into the hot and ice water, holds them for about 30 seconds, then places both into the room temperature bucket. One hand perceives the water as cold, the other as hot, confusing the nervous system. Used to illustrate how perceptions can seem relative but the felt sense of being with an honest, upright person is universally recognizable and real.
**Zen Master Katagiri Roshi and the Roaches**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. Students in a Zen community came to Katagiri Roshi with a dilemma: small roaches had infested the kitchen and rice pantry. Despite efforts to remove them using baking soda and diatomaceous earth, the roaches persisted. The students had taken a vow not to kill, yet roaches were appearing in the food being served, causing students to stop coming. When they posed the question to the Zen master, he sat quietly and then said, "I'm not going to tell you. I leave it in your hands." Jack Kornfield uses this to illustrate that ethical dilemmas sometimes require personal wrestling rather than prescribed answers.
**The Illinois Toll Road Study**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. A graduate student interning at an Illinois mental hospital near a toll road rigged a camera to observe which therapists working at the hospital paid their tolls at an unstaffed booth and which did not. The student compiled a list of names and later discovered that toll payment correlated directly with the success of those therapists' work with patients — therapists who paid their tolls had patients who got better faster.
**The Japanese American Woman and the Graffiti**
Shared by Jack Kornfield. A Japanese American woman whose job included cleaning public bathrooms in a large building repeatedly had to clean off graffiti and other defacing material sprayed by young people in the area. After it happened several times, she put up a sign that read: "Please do not desecrate my place of work." The simple, genuine, and humble appeal caused the graffiti to stop. Used to illustrate the power of honest, direct, and non-aggressive speech.
