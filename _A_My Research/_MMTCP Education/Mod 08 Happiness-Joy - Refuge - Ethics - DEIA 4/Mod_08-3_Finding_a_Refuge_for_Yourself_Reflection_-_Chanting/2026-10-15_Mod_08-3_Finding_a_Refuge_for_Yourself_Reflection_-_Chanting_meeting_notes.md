### Outline
### The Five Precepts of Non-Harming as a Living Practice

- Presented as medicines for healing personal and worldly conflict, these teachings have been passed from one living being to another across thousands of years and are now entrusted to the participants.

- Underlying the precepts is the practitioner's own integrity and intention, which serve as the foundation for taking them on as genuine personal trainings.

- Awareness of the suffering caused by the destruction of life calls for a vow to cultivate compassion and to learn ways of protecting the lives of people, animals, and plants.

- Rooted in awareness of suffering caused by exploitation, social injustice, stealing, and oppression, the second precept involves a vow to cultivate loving-kindness and work for the well-being of all living beings.

- Determination not to steal or possess anything that rightfully belongs to others forms the ethical core of the second training.

- Awareness of the suffering caused by sexual misconduct calls for a vow to cultivate responsibility for one's own sexual energies and to protect the safety and integrity of individuals, couples, families, and society.

- Mindful, loving speech and deep listening are vowed as the response to the suffering caused by unmindful speech and the inability to hear others' pain.

- Awareness of the suffering caused by unmindful consumption and addiction calls for a vow to cultivate good physical and mental health through mindful eating, drinking, and consuming, for oneself, one's family, and society.

- A traditional Pali chant was offered affirming that these trainings and practices bring joy, integrity, happiness, and liberation to the heart.

### Taking Refuge in the Three Classical Gateways

- The three refuges are described as archetypal gateways that both serve the awakening of heart-mind and are an expression of it, complementing the training precepts.

- Refuge in Buddha nature is introduced as taking refuge in the awake awareness, the luminosity and consciousness, that is already present and lives through each person.

- Participants were invited to sit in silence and sense for themselves the personal meaning of taking refuge in awareness and wakefulness.

- Refuge in the Dharma is described as taking refuge in the path, the nature of nature, and in truth, understood as full presence and complete recognition of what is here.

- A moment of silence was offered for participants to sense the personal meaning of taking refuge in truth and full presence.

- Refuge in Sangha is described as taking refuge in loving relatedness, both with each other and with all beings.

- Participants were again invited into silence to sense the longing for and personal meaning of taking refuge in community and relatedness.

### Chanting of the Three Refuges as a Closing Practice

- The session was closed with the traditional Pali chanting of the three refuges, led by Jack Kornfield and Tara Brach together.

- Those unfamiliar with the chant were invited to receive it as the energy and spirit of homecoming, of taking refuge in truth, love, and awareness.

- The chant included the threefold recitation of taking refuge in the Buddha, the Dhamma, and the Sangha, repeated three times in the traditional form.

### Next Steps

- Participants are encouraged to carry the five precepts of non-harming forward as active personal trainings grounded in their own integrity and intention.

- Ongoing reflection on the personal meaning of the three refuges in awareness, truth, and loving relatedness is intended as a continuing contemplative practice.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**The Five Precepts of Non-Harming**
- Jack Kornfield led a reflective reading of five training precepts, inviting participants to receive them as genuine personal practices
- Precept 1 — Protection of Life: Awareness of suffering caused by the destruction of life; vow to cultivate compassion and learn ways to protect the lives of people, animals, and plants
- Precept 2 — Non-Stealing and Social Justice: Awareness of suffering caused by exploitation, social injustice, stealing, and oppression; vow to cultivate loving-kindness and work for the well-being of people, animals, and plants; determination not to steal or possess what belongs to others
- Precept 3 — Sexual Responsibility: Awareness of suffering caused by sexual misconduct; vow to cultivate responsibility for one's own sexual energies and actions; commitment to protecting the safety and integrity of individuals, couples, families, and society
- Precept 4 — Mindful Speech and Listening: Awareness of suffering caused by unmindful speech and inability to listen; vow to cultivate mindful, loving speech and deep listening
- Precept 5 — Mindful Consumption: Awareness of suffering caused by unmindful consumption and addictions; vow to cultivate good health — physical and mental — for oneself, family, and society through mindful eating, drinking, and consuming
**Traditional Pali Chant on the Precepts**
- Jack Kornfield offered a traditional chant: "Sī lena sukha-tiññānti..." which he translated as affirming that these trainings and practices bring joy, integrity, happiness, and liberation to the heart
**The Three Refuges**
- Tara Brach introduced the Three Refuges as archetypal gateways that allow full inhabiting of one's beingness
- The training precepts were described as both serving the awakening of heart-mind and being an expression of it
- Refuge 1 — Buddha Nature: Taking refuge in awareness, the awake awareness that is present; described as wakefulness, luminosity, and consciousness that lives through us; participants were invited to sit in silence and sense what this means personally
- Refuge 2 — Dharma: Described as the path, the nature of nature, and truth; taking refuge in the Dharma means taking refuge in full presence and recognizing fully what is here; participants were invited to sense the personal meaning of taking refuge in truth
- Refuge 3 — Sangha: Described as loving relatedness; taking refuge in Sangha means taking refuge in relatedness with each other and all beings; participants were invited to sense the longing for and meaning of this refuge in their own lives
**Closing Chant of the Three Refuges**
- Jack Kornfield and Tara Brach led a chanting of the traditional Pali refuges: "Buddham Saranam Gacchami, Dhammam Saranam Gacchami, Sangham Saranam Gacchami" repeated three times
- Tara Brach described the chant as the energy or spirit of homecoming — taking refuge in truth, love, and awareness
- Those unfamiliar with the chant were invited to feel it as energy or spirit
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**Teachings as Living Transmission**
- Jack Kornfield described the teachings as having been carried and offered from one person to another, one warm hand to another, one living being to another, for thousands of years
- The teachings are now entrusted to the participants — described as being in their good hands and good hearts
**Teachings as Medicine**
- The teachings were explicitly described as medicines for healing the parts of the world and the conflicts of the world
- They were characterized as beautiful, powerful, and entrusted to practitioners
**Integrity as Foundation**
- Personal integrity and beautiful intentions were identified as what supports and underlies the practices
- Taking the precepts as genuine personal practices was emphasized as essential
**Suffering as the Starting Point for Practice**
- Each of the five precepts begins with awareness of a specific form of suffering — destruction of life, exploitation, sexual misconduct, unmindful speech, unmindful consumption — framing practice as a compassionate response to real-world harm
**Societal Scope of the Precepts**
- The precepts extend beyond personal conduct to encompass animals, plants, families, couples, and society at large, reflecting a broad ethical and ecological awareness
**Refuge as Homecoming**
- Tara Brach framed the Three Refuges not as external shelters but as gateways to fully inhabiting one's own beingness
- The act of taking refuge was described as a homecoming — returning to truth, love, and awareness
**Communal Practice and Sangha**
- The closing chant was offered as a communal act, with those who knew it joining in and those who did not invited to receive its spirit
- Sangha was described as loving relatedness with each other and all beings
