# Mod 13-1 Desire - Oct, 20

### Mod 13-1 Desire
📅 October 20, 2026
👥 Jack Kornfield (Main Teacher), Tara Brach (Co-Teacher)
Tags: Desire, Attachment, Non-attachment, Mindfulness, Loving Awareness, Freedom, Spiritual Practice, Addiction, Consumer Culture, Teaching Mindfulness, Longing, Compassion, Self-Compassion, Healthy vs. Unhealthy Desire
### Outline
### The Nature of Desire as an Inherent Human Quality

- Rooted in evolutionary biology, desire is described as a fundamental wiring in human beings, traceable back to the earliest organisms that moved toward pleasure and away from pain.

- Framed through the metaphor of hidden chocolate chip cookies, the impossibility of truly surrendering desire is highlighted, as the self remains the only one aware of where it is concealed.

- Central to the discussion is the question of how teachers of mindfulness and contemplative practice can effectively and accurately teach about desire and attachment, given how easily these concepts are misunderstood.

### Freedom of Heart as the Core Purpose of Contemplative Teaching

- Described as the singular essence underlying all mindfulness, loving-kindness, and compassion training, freedom of heart is likened to the ocean having only one taste, the taste of salt.

- This freedom is not a withdrawal from the world but rather a fuller, more open presence within it, allowing one to respond, know, and find wholeness in each moment.

- Illustrated through the example of Aung San Suu Kyi, who endured 17 years of house arrest, the loss of her husband, and separation from her children, yet maintained an inner freedom by refusing to harbor hatred toward her captors.

### Desire as a Source of Suffering and Entanglement

- Desire becomes a source of suffering when it is misdirected, as illustrated through the account of a woman named Sandra whose lifelong struggle with binge eating left her feeling like a hungry ghost filled with self-hatred.

- Sandra's belief that food held an unparalleled capacity to bring satisfaction and relieve suffering repeatedly failed her, resulting in physical and emotional pain, shame, and self-criticism.

- Recovery came through growing mindfulness of the body, the ability to stay present with discomfort, and the cultivation of self-compassion, which allowed her to observe compulsive patterns without being consumed by them.

- Desire can also infiltrate spiritual practice itself, as expressed through the poetry of Kabir, who observed that each renunciation, whether of fine clothes, sexual longing, or anger, simply gave way to another form of grasping, including pride in one's own spiritual progress.

### The Concept of Seeing Without the Distortion of Longing

- Drawing from Taoist thought, the ideal of seeing the world with eyes unclouded by longing is presented as a counterpoint to the distorting effect of desire on perception.

- Illustrated through the image of a pickpocket who, upon meeting a saint, sees only what might be in the saint's pockets, desire is shown to narrow awareness and cause one to miss the fuller reality of a situation.

- Attachment to views and opinions is identified as another dimension of this problem, with the Buddha's observation noted that those strongly attached to their views go around the world annoying people.

### Consumer Society and the Collective Dimension of Desire

- Modern consumer society is described as an addicted society that deliberately keeps individuals distracted through constant stimulation, preventing them from being present to larger issues such as environmental degradation, racism, and ongoing warfare.

- Advertising is identified as a key mechanism of this distraction, using psychological sophistication to associate products with deep human longings such as wealth, status, and sexual fulfillment.

- At a collective level, greed for money and power is traced as the root of broader social ills including oppression, war, injustice, and racism, situating individual desire within a wider cultural and political context.

### Distinguishing Healthy from Unhealthy Attachment and Desire

- A critical distinction is drawn between healthy and unhealthy attachment, noting that Western psychology recognizes healthy attachment, such as the bond between parent and child, as essential to well-being, and its absence as the source of attachment disorders.

- Gandhi's statement that for those who are poor, God comes in the form of bread is used to illustrate that basic material needs represent a legitimate and necessary form of attachment.

- William Blake's insight is cited to clarify that those who enter the gates of heaven are not those who have suppressed their passions, but those who have cultivated an understanding of them.

- Beneath surface desires lies a deeper longing, which may be for acceptance, love, connection, security, or adventure, and distinguishing between longing that is meaningful and healthy versus that which entangles is presented as a key teaching task.

- Nisargadatta's provocation that students do not desire enough, and should instead desire to embrace the whole world rather than a small piece of it, reframes the problem as one of narrowness rather than desire itself.

### Teaching Students to Work Mindfully with Desire and Attachment

- Students are to be invited into a direct embodied inquiry, beginning from a place of well-being and wholeness, and then observing desire as it arises, including in the context of eating meditations that explore where hunger actually resides, whether in the tongue, the eyes, the belly, or the mind.

- The phenomenon of anticipation is highlighted through the example of imagining ice cream, with research suggesting that the anticipation of pleasure is often more intense than the experience itself, illustrating how desire operates in the nervous system.

- Practitioners are encouraged to drop below the narrative that desire tells and to pause and sense, with loving awareness, whether a given desire is healthy and skillful or destructive and leading toward suffering.

- Ajahn Chah's metaphor of sitting under a mango tree and choosing between rotten and good mangoes is used to convey that one can sit in the presence of desire and discern its quality rather than being automatically driven by it.

- Healthy desires, such as those oriented toward care, nourishing, love, freedom, and justice, can still be held lightly, and the risk of becoming attached to spiritual practice itself, as Kabir illustrates, is presented as an important caution.

### The Practice of Studying the Absence of Desire and Attachment

- Equally important to studying the presence of desire is inviting students to reflect on times when desire and attachment are absent, and to notice what remains in those moments.

- What tends to remain in the absence of craving is described as aliveness, care, attention, love, and connection, pointing to a quality of presence that is neither grasping nor indifferent.

- Students are guided to notice the embodied difference between living with grasping attachment and living with a sense of presence and clarity, asking how each state feels in the body and what it does to the mind.

- An eight-year-old poet's advice to eat only the bait and not the hook, nibbling slowly rather than gobbling all at once, is offered as an intuitive expression of this mindful relationship with desire.

- When unhealthy desire is met with loving awareness and compassion rather than suppression or judgment, it can transform into an expression of awakening, becoming commitment, dedication, care, and love.

### The Root of Desire and the Practice of the U-Turn

- The Latin root of the word desire, desiderare, meaning away from your star, is offered as a linguistically embedded insight that longing arises from a perception of separation from one's source, the light of awareness one needs for sustenance.

- This framing supports Nisargadatta's invitation to desire more broadly, as the problem is not desire itself but the narrowing fixation on a specific object as the solution to one's sense of incompleteness.

- A practice called making a U-turn is introduced, in which attention is redirected from the external object of fixation back to the internal experience of wanting itself, allowing the practitioner to examine what is actually happening inside.

- Judgment about desire is identified as a significant obstacle to this inquiry, as most people carry a background belief that they should not be needy or wanting, and this must be brought into the lens of mindfulness as part of the exploration.

- Students are guided to embody desire fully, noticing posture, facial expression, and physical tension, so that the wanting mind becomes a recognizable and familiar body-mind state that can be identified when it arises.

- Tracing desire deeper to the longing within the longing is described as a path toward belonging, in which fully inhabiting the longing reveals that what one seeks is already present, and the star is already here.

### Contentment and the Knowledge of Enough

- The story of author Joe Heller at a party hosted by a billionaire is recounted, in which Heller responds to a question about the billionaire's wealth by saying he possesses something the billionaire can never have, the knowledge that he has enough.

- This anecdote is presented as a vivid illustration of the freedom that arises not from acquiring more but from recognizing sufficiency, pointing to the quality of presence and wholeness that contemplative practice aims to cultivate.

### Next Steps

- Teachers are to develop a clear and nuanced framework for presenting desire and attachment to students, one that distinguishes healthy from unhealthy forms of each and avoids reinforcing the misconception that all desire must be eliminated.

- Embodied inquiry practices, including eating meditations, body-awareness exercises, and the U-turn technique, are to be incorporated into teaching as primary tools for helping students directly experience and study desire rather than conceptualize it.

- Students are to be guided not only in observing the presence of desire and attachment but also in reflecting on their absence, as this dual inquiry is identified as essential to pointing toward a deeper quality of freedom, care, and wholeness.

- The upcoming session with Tara on addiction is noted as a continuation of this thematic exploration, extending the discussion of desire into the specific domain of addictive behavior.

### Detailed Subject Matter
**Desire as Wired-In Human Nature**
- Desire is traced back to ancestral biology — even amoebae moved toward pleasure and away from pain
- Desire is described as intrinsic to human incarnation, not something to be eliminated
- Attempting to suppress desire is compared to hiding chocolate chip cookies from yourself when only you know where they are hidden
**Freedom of Heart as the Core Teaching**
- The essence of mindfulness, loving-kindness, and compassion teachings is described as freedom of heart
- Liberation teachings are said to have one taste: inner freedom
- Freedom is not about removing oneself from the world but being more fully present and open to life
- Aung San Suu Kyi is cited as an example of inner freedom maintained through 17 years of house arrest, including loss of family and inability to be with her husband at death
**Healthy vs. Unhealthy Desire**
- Healthy desire includes needs for food, shelter, job security, belonging, love, connection, adventure, and justice
- Unhealthy desire involves grasping, entanglement, and suffering — illustrated through binge eating and addiction
- William Blake is cited: those who enter heaven are not those who have no passions, but those who have cultivated understanding of them
- Nisargadatta is cited as saying students do not desire enough — encouraging desire for the whole world rather than small pieces of it
**Healthy vs. Unhealthy Attachment**
- Western psychology recognizes healthy attachment, such as parent-child bonding, which produces well-being; its absence leads to attachment disorders
- Unhealthy attachment involves grasping, fear, anxiety, and suffering
- Gandhi's statement is referenced: for the poor, God comes in the form of bread, illustrating legitimate need
- A contrast is drawn between a mother with a dying infant and a nearby shipment of military weapons, raising questions about collective values
**Desire in Consumer and Addicted Society**
- Society is described as an "addicted society" that keeps people distracted from environmental, racial, and political problems
- Advertising is cited as deliberately exploiting desire — liquor ads promising inner gold, car ads linking purchase to sexual fulfillment
- Highly paid psychologists are said to design product packaging to trigger grasping in children
- Collective greed is linked to greed for money and power, leading to oppression, war, injustice, and racism
**Spiritual Practice and Desire**
- Kabir's poem illustrates how spiritual practice itself can become an object of attachment — giving up fine clothes, then noticing the burlap is elegantly worn; suppressing sexual longing, then becoming angry; releasing rage, then becoming greedy; dissolving greed, then becoming proud
- The Tao is referenced: seeing with eyes unclouded by longing
- The pickpocket-meets-saint analogy illustrates how desire narrows perception to only what one craves
**Teaching Strategies for Desire and Attachment**
- Teachers are advised to clarify that both desire and attachment have healthy and unhealthy forms
- Students should be directed to experience desire in the body, not just conceptually
- The eating meditation is suggested: examining where hunger lives — in the tongue, eyes, belly, or mind
- Students are invited to notice the anticipation of desire versus the actual experience
- Ajahn Chah's mango tree analogy: sitting under a mango tree with fallen mangoes, choosing between rotten and good ones — students can sense whether a desire is healthy or destructive
- Students are invited to reflect on times of less attachment and less desire, and to notice what remains: aliveness, care, love, contentment
- The absence of desire is presented as equally important to study as its presence
**Longing Beneath Desire**
- Beneath surface desires lies a deeper longing: for acceptance, love, connection, security, openness, or adventure
- Geneen Roth's book Feeding the Hungry Heart is cited as an exploration of acting out of desire that does not feed what is truly important
**The U-Turn Practice (Tara Brach)**
- When fixated on an external object of desire, the practice is to turn attention back to the wanting itself
- Suffering arises from fixating on an external object to satisfy an inner need
- The more one fixates, the further one moves from one's inner source
- Ryokan is quoted: stop chasing after so many things to find meaning
**Etymology of Desire (Tara Brach)**
- The Latin root of desire is desiderare, meaning "away from your star"
- The connotation is that longing arises from a perception of being separated from one's source
- When the longing is fully inhabited, it becomes belonging — we become what we are longing for
- The star, the source, is described as already present
**Embodying Desire in Teaching (Tara Brach)**
- Students are asked to get in touch with something they strongly crave and to embody it fully — noticing posture, clenched fists, facial expression, leaning forward
- This anchors familiarity with the body-mind state of wanting
- Judgment about desire prevents intelligent examination of it; the background sense of "I shouldn't be needing this" must also be brought into mindfulness
- The inquiry "what is here when there is no desire and no resistance?" is offered as a powerful practice
**What Remains When Desire Is Absent**
- When grasping is absent, what remains is described as commitment, care, dedication, love, and connection
- Henry Moore is cited: the secret of life is to have a task you devote your entire life to — something you cannot possibly do — pointing to dedication rather than attachment
- Meeting people who are centered and whole, whether wealthy or poor, is offered as a lived reference point for students
### Spiritual and Societal Insights
**The Nature of Inner Freedom**
- Inner freedom is not the removal of desire but a quality of presence that is not entangled by it
- Aung San Suu Kyi's example demonstrates that freedom is an internal state that cannot be taken away by external circumstances, even severe loss and imprisonment
- The teachings of liberation are described as having one taste — inner freedom — just as the ocean has one taste, salt
**Desire as a Spiritual Misunderstanding**
- The instruction "do not be attached" or "give up desire" is frequently misunderstood by students as requiring the elimination of all wanting
- This misunderstanding can itself become a source of suffering and self-judgment
- The correct teaching is to understand desire and attachment, not to eradicate them
- Kabir's poem illustrates the trap of spiritual bypassing — replacing one form of attachment with another
**The Addicted Society and Collective Greed**
- Consumer society is structured to keep individuals distracted and grasping, preventing genuine presence
- Advertising exploits psychological vulnerabilities to manufacture desire
- Collective greed at the societal level manifests as the pursuit of money and power, leading to systemic injustice, racism, and war
- The contrast between a dying infant and a military weapons shipment is used to highlight misaligned collective values
**Longing as a Path Home**
- The Latin root of desire — away from your star — frames longing as a signal of perceived separation from one's source
- Tracing desire back to its root longing, and inhabiting that longing fully, leads to the discovery that the source is already present
- Longing, when fully met with awareness, transforms into belonging
**Contentment as a Spiritual State**
- Contentment is not indifference but a quality of presence, aliveness, and care without grasping
- Studying the absence of desire — moments of contentment — is presented as a powerful and often overlooked spiritual practice
- Joe Heller's statement "I've got enough" is offered as an example of genuine contentment
**Desire and Global Ethics**
- Healthy desire includes desire for freedom and justice at a collective level
- The question of what constitutes healthy attachment is framed as both a personal and a global ethical question
- Gandhi's framing of God as bread for the poor grounds spiritual teaching in material reality and social justice

### Stories
**Sandra and Binge Eating**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to a retreat participant named Sandra. Sandra described years of struggling with compulsive binge eating, feeling like a hungry ghost full of self-hatred. She believed food could bring satisfaction and free her from suffering, but it repeatedly failed her and brought physical, emotional suffering, and shame. Freedom came through mindfulness of her body and the discomfort she was trying to escape. With loving awareness and self-compassion, she was able to watch herself begin the old pattern and choose differently.
**Aung San Suu Kyi and House Arrest**
Shared by Jack Kornfield, referring to Aung San Suu Kyi, Nobel Prize winner and leader of Burma. Jack described spending time with her and hearing her speak about 17 years of house arrest under military guard. She described it at one point as like a long retreat. She said the military never truly had her in prison because she never allowed herself to hate them. Despite losing access to her family, being absent when her husband died, and being unable to raise her children, she maintained a quality of freedom and dignity.
**Jack Kornfield and the Ice Cream**
Shared by Jack Kornfield about his own experience. While sitting in meditation, he imagined going to get ice cream afterward. He described the anticipation building, going to the shop, getting his favorite mint chocolate chip, and noticing that after a few spoonfuls the fervor of desire had died down. He used this to illustrate how anticipation is often more intense than the actual experience, and how desire naturally subsides once met.
**Kurt Vonnegut and Joe Heller**
Shared by Tara Brach, sourced from Kurt Vonnegut. At a party given by a billionaire on Shelter Island, Vonnegut asked Joe Heller — author of Catch-22 — how it felt knowing the host may have made more money in one day than Catch-22 had earned in its entire history. Heller replied that he had something the host could never have: the knowledge that he had enough. Tara shared this at what she described as Heller's memorial context as Vonnegut wrote it.
**Mother and Infant in a Developing Nation**
Shared by Jack Kornfield as an illustrative scenario. A mother in a developing nation's slum cradles a whimpering infant with dysentery, severely dehydrated, possibly dying, with no money for medical care. Older children are pale and thin in the corner of a small shack. Several kilometers away, a new shipment of military transport trucks and weaponry is being unloaded at a seaport. Jack used this to raise the question of collective values and what constitutes healthy attachment at a societal level.
