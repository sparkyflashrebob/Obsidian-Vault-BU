# "A Cup of Tea" - Master Nan-in - Spiritual Story and Explanation

**Key Figures:** Master Nan-in (Zen Master) and a University Professor

## The Story

Nan-in, a Japanese Zen master who lived during the Meiji era, received a university professor who came to inquire about Zen.

The professor was articulate, well-read, and eager to display his extensive knowledge of philosophy, theology, and spiritual doctrine. As Nan-in listened quietly, the professor continued talking at length about his thoughts and theories on Zen.

Nan-in offered to serve tea. He set out the cups and began pouring steaming hot green tea into the visitor's cup.

The cup filled to the brim, but Nan-in did not stop pouring. The tea spilled over the rim, flooded the saucer, and began cascading across the low wooden table, pooling toward the visitor's robes.

The professor watched the overflow in mounting bewilderment and agitation until he could no longer contain himself:

> *"It is overfull! No more will go in!"*

Nan-in stopped pouring, set down the teapot, and looked calmly into the professor's eyes:

> *"Like this cup, you are full of your own opinions and speculations. How can I show you Zen unless you first empty your cup?"*

---

## Moral & Spiritual Meaning

### 1. Epistemic Humility & *Shoshin* (Beginner's Mind)
In Zen practice and spiritual inquiry, genuine reception requires an open container. If the intellect arrives already saturated with pre-formed conclusions, conceptual baggage, and intellectual pride, there is no empty space for fresh realization or direct experience. Shunryu Suzuki famously echoed this principle: *"In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's there are few."*

### 2. The Trap of Intellectual Grasping (*Diṭṭhi-upādāna*)
The professor mistook intellectual knowledge *about* Zen for the lived reality of Zen. The story dramatizes how attachment to concepts and dogma (*diṭṭhi-upādāna*) blinds an earnest seeker from direct perception.

### 3. Deliberate Disruption as Compassionate Teaching
Nan-in did not engage in philosophical debate; he allowed physical reality (the spilling liquid) to make undeniable what verbal argument could never penetrate. The shock of the overflowing liquid shattered the professor's conceptual trance in an instant.
