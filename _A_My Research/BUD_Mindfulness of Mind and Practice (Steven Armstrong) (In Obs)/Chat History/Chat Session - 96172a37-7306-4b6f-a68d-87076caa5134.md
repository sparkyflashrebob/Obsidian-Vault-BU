The talk given on August 23, 2008, focuses on the core teachings of the Buddha, particularly the **Three Trainings of the Noble Eightfold Path** and the necessity of balancing the **Seven Factors of Enlightenment** to achieve liberation.

## I. The Foundational Structure: The Four Noble Truths and Three Trainings

The discourse begins by establishing the bedrock of Buddhist tradition: the **Four Noble Truths** [1].

1. **The Truth of *Dukkha*:** This is the pain, suffering, insecurity, and unsatisfactoriness present in life [1].
2. **The Cause of *Dukkha*:** This suffering is caused by craving or clinging attachment [1].
3. **The Cessation of *Dukkha*:** It is possible to reach the end of craving and thus the end of suffering [1].
4. **The Path of Awakening (The Noble Eightfold Path):** This path, consisting of **three essential trainings**, must be developed by the practitioner to realize the end of *Dukkha* [1, 2].

### The Different Trainings Offered

The Noble Eightfold Path is condensed into three levels of training, moving from coarse physical purification to subtle purification of understanding [2, 3].

| Training (Pali) | Focus and Function | Target Defilement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. *Sīla*** (Ethical Conduct) | Purifying speech and behavior by living according to the precepts (moral conduct) [2, 4]. This allows the practitioner to live in harmony [2]. | **Transgressive Defilements:** Those "most egregious" defilements that harm others [2]. |
| **2. *Samādhi*** (Concentration) | Training the mind to subdue defilements temporarily, gaining the "happiness of an untormented mind" [3, 4]. This is also called training the mind to be tranquil [3]. | **Obsessive Defilements:** The internal "yada yada yada" of the mind (e.g., I want this, I don't want that) [2, 4]. |
| **3. *Paññā*** (Wisdom/Insight) | The subtlest and most powerful practice: developing **right understanding** by practicing **insight (*Vipassanā*)** [3, 5]. This practice sees that experience arises due to conditions, lasts a while, and ends [5]. | **Latent Defilements/Wrong Understanding:** Uprooting the wrong understanding from the mind so that these defilements "won't have a chance to obsess the mind" when conditions change [3]. |

The goal of the wisdom training is to realize for oneself the **three universal characteristics** of all experience: **impermanence** (*anicca*), **unsatisfactoriness** (*dukkha*), and **insubstantiality/impersonality** (*anattā*) [6-8]. Insight practice (Vipassanā) is the path to achieving a sure heart's release [9].

## II. The Seven Factors of Enlightenment

The seven factors of enlightenment (or awakening) are presented as the **essence of the balanced mind** and the qualities that open the door to tranquility and peace [10]. The successful practice of the path requires developing and refining the **balance** among these seven factors [10, 11].

This balance is illustrated by the analogy of a tightrope walker carrying a heavy, long pole: **Mindfulness** is the heavy pole that keeps the center of gravity low and allows for adjustment [11, 12]. The mind must adjust by raising the tranquilizing factors if there is too much energy, or raising the energizing factors if the mind is too tranquil or chilled out [11].

The seven factors are divided into one balancing factor, three energizing factors, and three tranquilizing factors [11, 13].

### The Balancing Factor: Mindfulness (*Sati*)

Mindfulness is defined as **"not forgetting the present moment"** [14, 15]. It involves recognizing the present moment's experience by activating the concentrating factors of **connecting** and **sustaining attention** [6, 15].

### The Three Energizing (Arousing) Factors

These factors make the mind active, engaging, and dynamic, counteracting sluggishness [16].

| Factor | Function in Practice | Pitfalls/Balance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Investigation of the Dharma (*Dhamma Vicaya*)** | The wisdom factor; understanding "this is the way it is" [16]. In concentration practice, this means **recognizing the sign of *Samādhi*** (concentration) [16]. | In excess, it means trying too hard and pushing through the experience to insight prematurely, instead of resting in balance to develop tranquility [17, 18]. |
| **2. Energy/Effort (*Virya*)** | Perseverance required to turn attention back to the present moment [19]. It includes the four right efforts, such as overcoming defilements that have arisen (turning attention back) [17, 20]. | **Excess:** Leads to striving, overexerting, hypervigilance, restlessness, tension, and tightness [19, 21]. **Deficient:** Leads to being dull, lethargic, or lazy ("gazing without seeing") [19, 21]. |
| **3. Joy (*Pīti*)** | Arises from connecting and sustaining attention with sufficient continuity [22]. It is the mind's delight in being able to **do its work unhindered** (i.e., to know) [22, 23]. It overcomes aversion [24]. | Joy is considered one of the "spiritual goodies" [22, 25]. If intense, it is called **pseudo Nibbāna** because it is seductive and powerful, leading practitioners to believe they are enlightened and causing attachment [14, 24, 25]. |

### The Three Tranquilizing Factors

These are calming, grounding energies in the mind [13].

| Factor | Function in Practice | Insight/Results |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Calmness/Tranquility (*Passaddhi*)** | A calming quality [13]. | Helps subdue agitation and provides comfort [26]. |
| **2. Concentration (*Samādhi*)** | The **collectedness** and **stability** of mind [27]. It stabilizes the object (like sensations at the nostrils) and also stabilizes the awareness of the object [27]. | Allows the mind to see through the appearance of things to their inner essence and to see things **as a whole** [27, 28]. The collectedness achieved by experienced meditators (like Dipa Ma) allows them to synthesize complexity, such as weaving all ten Rorschach inkblots into a single teaching of the Dharma [28, 29]. |
| **3. Equanimity (*Upekkhā*)** | The capacity for **non-reactivity**; the ability to be with things as they are [30]. The mind is balanced and equanimous, regardless of whether the experience is pleasant or unpleasant [8, 30]. | Equanimity makes the mind **unshakable** and steady [8]. It is strengthened by seeing the extremes of pleasant and unpleasant phenomena, enabling the practitioner to let go of attachment to the pleasant and fear of the unpleasant [8]. |

The sources detail contrasting views between the practices and core tenets of **Buddhist Meditation** (specifically the Dharma path) and the doctrines of **Transactional Theology** (a described Christian heresy). The contrast hinges primarily on the source of authority, the mechanism for achieving desired results, and the ultimate goal of practice.

## I. Authority and Source of Truth

**Buddhist Meditation and Empirical Reality:**
Buddhist teachings emphasize an **empirical** and internal realization of truth, rather than reliance on dogma or external deity [1, 2]. The historical Buddha was a man who studied his own mind and offered thoughts on suffering and happiness, contrasting with the Christian narrative of Jesus as the son of the creator [2].

* The practice of meditation, particularly Vipassanā (insight), is designed to gain **wisdom** through **personal experience and direct observation** about the nature of the mind and reality [3, 4]. Intellectual "knowing" is insufficient; one must observe reality moment to moment and sit with the mind's torments [4, 5].
* The emphasis in the Vipassanā tradition is that "it's up to you to study your own mind and come to your own awakening" [2]. The path must be traversed through continued *training* in moral and spiritual wisdom [6].
* The Dharma itself is defined as "the way things are" or the truth [7]. The four noble truths—the bedrock of all Buddhist traditions—are viewed as an articulation of how things are, which practitioners are encouraged to verify for themselves [1, 8].

**Transactional Theology and Divine Sovereignty (or lack thereof):**
Transactional theology (TT) is described as a **dangerous heresy** that reduces God to a "benevolent genie who has the power to grant wishes" [9].

* TT suggests that believers have the power to compel God into granting desires, assuming they can "turn on this tap of our blessing by thinking and acting a certain way toward God" [9, 10].
* The primary biblical rebuttal to this heresy is the assertion of **God’s immutable sovereignty**, meaning God alone determines all outcomes, and there is no other source of power or authority [11].
* The TT view is seen as training Christians to think in opposite ways by insinuating they hold the reins for their own future and control God's responses by what they say or do [12].

## II. Methodology and Motivation for Action

**Buddhist Practice: Disentangling Clinging and Uprooting Suffering**
The Buddhist path, summarized by the Noble Eightfold Path, involves three trainings: ethical conduct (*sīla*), concentration (*samādhi*), and wisdom/insight (*paññā*) [13-16].

* **Non-Transactional Motivation:** The entire purpose of practice is the **liberation of mind through the uprooting of defilements** (such as greed, hatred, delusion, anger, jealousy, and anxiety) [17-20]. Suffering is caused by "visiting forces known as defilements" [21].
* **Ethical Foundation:** The training in ethical conduct (*sīla*) involves purifying speech and behavior to avoid "transgressive defilements" that harm others, allowing one to live in harmony [16, 22, 23]. This moral conduct is considered a preliminary, foundational practice for developing insight [24].
* **The Problem of Clinging:** Buddhism explicitly argues against clinging to pleasant, fleeting experiences. Moments of joy and ecstasy, emphasized in the West, will inevitably fade away and can cause one to "cling to them and crave them even more" [25, 26]. Such blissful states that arise in meditation (*spiritual goodies*) are powerful but are referred to as **pseudo Nibbāna** and must be let go of, as attachment to them degrades the practice [27-29].
* **Effort as Deconditioning:** Practice is about deconditioning the mind, not reconditioning it, by understanding what is happening [30, 31]. Effort is defined as **perseverance** in turning attention back to the present moment [32].

**Transactional Practice: Quid Pro Quo to Receive Blessings**
Transactional theology dictates that the relationship with God is a "quid pro quo arrangement" (something for something) [33].

* **Conditional Blessing:** TT claims that one must meet God's expectations to "release God's favor," suggesting that God is unable to bless until certain prerequisites are met [34].
* **Leveraging Faith:** Believers are taught that their "faith" obligates God to grant desires [33]. TT false teachers twist scripture to claim God rewards financial gifts with abundant riches or always heals physical ailments [35].
* **Blame for Failure:** When the promised blessings fail to appear (because God is not a genie), TT shifts the blame onto the follower, suggesting they didn't show enough faith or try hard enough [36, 37].

## III. View of Happiness and Reality

**Buddhist View: Inner Peace and Impermanence**
The goal of Buddhist practice is the realization of the liberated mind [38].

* **Highest Happiness is Peace:** The ultimate achievement is the highest form of happiness, which is **inner peace** or *nibbāna* [25, 39-41]. Peace is described as the inner contentment and serenity that comes from the knowledge that no matter what happens, happiness is accessible [39]. This peace is not permanent (nothing is), but it is always accessible [39].
* **Acceptance of Suffering (*Dukkha*):** All phenomena are characterized by **impermanence, unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*), and insubstantiality (*anattā*)** [42-47]. The first noble truth is the truth of *dukkha* (pain/insecurity/unreliability), which must be investigated and accepted, not avoided [46, 48, 49]. Recognizing the insubstantiality of experiences means there is nothing for the mind to cling to, leading to the end of craving [47].

**Transactional View: External Rewards and Ecstasies**
Transactional theology focuses on seeking external, peak experiences and material well-being in the present world [50].

* **Focus on Peak Experiences:** The concept of happiness familiar in the West (and propagated by this heresy) emphasizes **peak experiences, ecstasies, and unforgettable moments of joy** [25, 26].
* **Desire-Driven:** TT suggests that God's chief purpose is to grant us what we desire, and the believer can determine the content of God's blessings (e.g., believing you will be healed or rich) [9, 35]. This is viewed as fixing attention on the fallen world (Satan’s dominion) rather than the eternal [50].
* **Failure of Promises:** Transactional theology eventually disappoints its followers because God does not cater to their lusts, causing victims to become disillusioned with the church, doubtful of God, and cynical about their faith [37, 51].

| Aspect | Buddhist Meditation / Dharma | Transactional Theology |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source of Truth** | **Internal, Empirical Experience:** Truth is confirmed by observing one's own mind and body moment-to-moment; the Buddha was a man, not a deity [1, 2, 4]. | **External, Conditional Deity:** God is viewed as a benevolent genie who grants wishes if approached correctly through faith [9, 33]. |
| **Goal of Action** | **Liberation from Defilement:** The purpose is to purify understanding to permanently uproot the sources of suffering (*dukkha*) and craving [15, 20, 52]. | **Acquisition of Desire:** The goal is to obtain personal desires, monetary wealth, or healing by compelling God to act [33, 35]. |
| **Nature of Happiness** | **Inner Peace and Contentment:** Highest happiness is unshakable peace, accessible regardless of external conditions, recognizing that all things are impermanent [3, 25, 39]. | **Peak Experiences and Material Gain:** Happiness emphasizes ecstasy, joy, and seeking the world's rewards [25, 50]. |
| **View of Suffering** | **Universal Characteristic (*Dukkha*):** Suffering is inherent to conditioned existence; it is impersonal and must be investigated and accepted to achieve cessation [30, 46, 49]. | **Personal Failure or External Problem:** Infirmities can be remedied by greater faith, and failure to receive blessings is blamed on the individual's lack of sufficient faith [35, 36]. |

This contrast is essentially one between a path of **inner, empirical liberation** through understanding reality as it is (Buddhism) and a system of **external negotiation** based on performance and conditional faith intended to change reality (Transactional Theology) [33, 53].

The factors detailing the arousing (energizing) and tranquilizing aspects of concentration are known collectively as the **Seven Factors of Enlightenment** (or Awakening) [1-4]. These factors are considered the essence of the balanced mind and are crucial for developing concentration (*Samādhi*) and insight (*Vipassanā*), opening the door to peace and awakening [4].

The seven factors are comprised of three energizing qualities, three tranquilizing qualities, and mindfulness, which acts as the balancing element [2, 3].

## I. The Balancing Factor: Mindfulness (*Sati*)

Mindfulness is necessary for maintaining balance, much like the heavy, long pole carried by a tightrope walker, which keeps the center of gravity low and allows for small adjustments [1, 5].

* **Role and Function:** Mindfulness is about **establishing a balance between tranquility and alertness** [6]. It is necessary to apply effort in a balanced way; too much striving makes the mind "too tight" or brittle, while too little effort means the goal won't be achieved automatically [6].
* **Definition:** Mindfulness means **not forgetting the present moment** [7]. It involves being attentive, alert, and relaxed, activating the factors of connecting and sustaining attention on the present moment [7, 8].
* **Recalibration:** Mindfulness recognizes when the present moment is out of balance (too energized or too tranquil) and recalibrates the effort needed to make adjustments [8, 9]. For instance, if there is too much energy, one leans toward tranquility; if one is too chilled out or tranquil, one raises the energizing factors [1].

## II. The Three Energizing (Arousing) Factors

The three energizing factors are **Investigation of the Dharma**, **Energy**, and **Joy** [10]. When these factors are present, the mind is active, engaging, dynamic, and not sluggish [10].

### 1. Investigation of the Dharma (*Dhamma Vicaya*)

This is considered the wisdom factor, leading to the understanding, "This is the way it is" [10].

* **Function in Concentration:** In concentration practice, investigation involves **recognizing the sign of *Samādhi*** (concentration) [11]. One investigates the conditions (like paying attention, or being relaxed or attentive) that allow Samādhi to arise [11, 12]. By refining the understanding of how *Samādhi* arises, one can enhance those conditions and arouse concentration more often and for longer periods [12].
* **Deficiency and Excess:** If deficient, one fails to recognize *Samādhi* and repeats the same mistakes [12]. If in excess, the mind is too active, leading to restlessness from reviewing books or thinking about the Dharma, rather than observing one's direct experience [13].

### 2. Energy/Effort (*Virya*)

Energy is essential because the path has many pitfalls. Right effort is often summarized by the **Four Right Efforts** [14, 15]:

1. **Avoid** defilements that have not yet arisen (e.g., avoiding potential distraction) [15]. This effort is required to avoid indulging habits [16].
2. **Overcome** defilements that have arisen [16]. In concentration practice, this means recognizing the mind is off the chosen object and gently **redirecting attention** back to it [17]. The true effort here is the willingness to let go of obsession [17].
3. **Arouse** wholesome states of mind that have not yet arisen (e.g., maintaining the intention to be mindful) [18].
4. **Develop** wholesome qualities that have already arisen [19]. Recognizing wholesome states (like calmness, enjoyment, or curiosity) strengthens them [19].

* **Balance of Effort:** Effort is not difficult (like "gritting your teeth and struggle"); it is simply **perseverance** in turning attention back to the present moment [19, 20].
* **Excess and Deficiency:** Excess effort leads to striving, hypervigilance, tightness, tension, and restlessness [21]. Deficient effort leads to being dull, lethargic, or lazy (gazing without seeing) [21].

### 3. Joy (*Pīti*)

Joy is an energizing spiritual quality that starts as interest or zest and matures into rapture and ecstasy [22].

* **Arising:** Joy arises from the mind being able to perform its work **unhindered**—by connecting and sustaining attention with sufficient continuity [23]. It is the delight the mind takes in *knowing things as they are*, not personal or sensual pleasure [23].
* **Impact:** Joy can manifest as physical chills, tingling, goosebumps, or intense energy [24]. It overcomes aversion [25].
* **Attachment:** Joy can be so powerful and seductive that it is called **pseudo Nibbāna** [22, 26]. It often leads to attachment and the belief that one has attained the goal [25, 27, 28].

## III. The Three Tranquilizing Factors

The three tranquilizing factors are **Calmness (Tranquility)**, **Concentration**, and **Equanimity** [3]. These are grounding and calming energies that provide balance [9].

### 1. Calmness/Tranquility (*Passaddhi*)

Tranquility is the experience most people initially seek in practice when their minds are racing and stressed [9].

* **Arising:** It arises from connecting and sustaining attention on the chosen object; trying to force calmness is agitating [29].
* **Balance:** When tranquility arises, the mind is at ease and peaceful, suppressing agitation [29, 30]. When it is in **excess** (too high), the mind becomes dull, soporific, or experiences the "hundred yard gaze," and risks sinking out the bottom [30]. In this case, the energizing factors must be raised to meet the high level of tranquility, resulting in a **dynamic stillness** [30, 31].
* **Benefit:** Tranquility is accompanied by wholesome mental states such as lightness, adaptability, proficiency, and pliability of mind, allowing the mind to remain undisturbed by arising experiences [31].

### 2. Concentration (*Samādhi*)

Concentration is the collectedness and stability of the mind [32].

* **Power and Stability:** As the mind collects, it stabilizes both the object and the awareness of the object, becoming more powerful and seeing things in greater detail [32].
* **Unity:** The concentrated mind brings everything into a **single unity** [33]. For example, a concentrated mind viewing random floorboards automatically perceives a pattern, or when facing a complex problem, the solution appears because the mind can hold and resolve all opposing possibilities into a single unity [33]. A profound example is provided of a highly concentrated practitioner (*Deepama*) who wove all ten Rorschach inkblots into a single teaching of the Dharma [34, 35].

### 3. Equanimity (*Upekkhā*)

Equanimity is the non-reactive state of mind that leads to an unshakable and steady mind [35, 36].

* **Function:** It involves arresting the mind before it falls into reactivity [35]. With equanimity, the mind does not react with:
  + **Aversion** to unpleasant experiences [35].
  + **Attachment** to pleasant experiences [35].
  + **Confusion or delusion** to neutral experiences [35].
* **Cultivation:** Equanimity allows one to stay open to all that life offers, feeling the pleasantness and unpleasantness without being caught in habitual reaction, thus responding out of understanding and compassion [37, 38].
* **Liberation:** Equanimity, when mature, provides the platform upon which the mind can access the unconditioned (*Nibbāna*) [39]. It is the stability of mind that allows the practitioner to see the three universal characteristics (impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and insubstantiality) in every experience [38, 40].

The goal of practice is the development and refinement of the balance among these seven factors [4].
