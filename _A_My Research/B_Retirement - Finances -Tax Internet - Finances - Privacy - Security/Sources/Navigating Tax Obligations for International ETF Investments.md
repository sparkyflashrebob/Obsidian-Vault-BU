Investing in foreign companies through ETFs triggers tax obligations in both the foreign country and the U.S. However, the process is streamlined to avoid double taxation and offers major tax efficiencies. [1, 2, 3]

1. Foreign Withholding Taxes
   Foreign companies often pay dividends. Before these dividends reach the ETF, the foreign government usually deducts a withholding tax (typically between 15% and 30%, depending on the country). The Process: The ETF automatically receives the dividend with the tax already subtracted. Your Action: You will see the gross dividend and the foreign tax withheld on your year-end Form 1099-DIV. [3, 5]
2. The Foreign Tax Credit
   To prevent you from getting taxed on the same income twice, the IRS allows you to claim the Foreign Tax Credit (via IRS Form 1116 ). This credit allows you to offset your U.S. tax liability dollar-for-dollar by the amount of foreign tax already withheld. [3]
3. Capital Gains
   When you sell your international ETF for a profit, you are generally only subject to U.S. capital gains taxes. Short-Term Gains: If you hold the ETF for 1 year or less, gains are taxed at your standard ordinary income rate. Long-Term Gains: If you hold the ETF for more than 1 year, you qualify for preferential long-term capital gains tax rates (0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on your income level). [4, 9, 10, 11]
4. Tax-Advantaged Accounts
   If you hold international ETFs in a tax-advantaged account like an IRA or 401(k), you generally will not owe immediate U.S. taxes on dividends or capital gains. Note: While you won't be taxed by the U.S., you still forfeit the foreign withholding taxes deducted at the source, and you cannot claim the Foreign Tax Credit for those withheld funds. [12, 13]
