Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) is an investment strategy where you invest a fixed amount of money at regular intervals, regardless of market fluctuations. Instead of putting a large lump sum all at once, you spread your purchases out over time to reduce the impact of market volatility. [1, 2, 3]

How It Works

Consistent Schedule: You invest a set amount (e.g., $100) on a routine basis, such as weekly, bi-weekly, or monthly.

Share Fluctuation: Because your dollar amount is fixed, you automatically buy more shares when prices are low and fewer shares when prices are high.

Average Cost: Over time, this blends your entry points, potentially resulting in a lower average cost per share than if you had bought all at once. [8]

Common Examples

Retirement Accounts: The most common form of DCA is contributing to a 401(k) or similar plan where a fixed portion of every paycheck is automatically invested.

Brokerage Apps: Setting up recurring deposits into specific stocks, index funds, or ETFs on platforms like Fidelity or Robinhood. [1, 3, 9]

Benefits & Trade-offs

Removes Emotion: It helps you avoid the stress and guesswork of trying to "time the market".

Manages Risk: It prevents the scenario of investing a large lump sum right before a market drop.

Potential Drawbacks: In a consistently rising bull market, lump-sum investing typically outperforms DCA because all your money is invested earlier and captures more gains. [2, 7, 8]

For a deeper dive into the math and whether it suits your portfolio, you can review the Charles Schwab guide on managing market timing risk. [8]
