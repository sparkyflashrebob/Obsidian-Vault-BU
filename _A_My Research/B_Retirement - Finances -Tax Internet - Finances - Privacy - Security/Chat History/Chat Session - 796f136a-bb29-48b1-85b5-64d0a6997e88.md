The test ran each strategy through **69 rolling 30-year market periods** (from 1928 through 2025) and scored them out of 100 based on portfolio survival, income consistency, protection against bad market starts, total spending enjoyed, and ending balance [3-5].

---

### **1. Traditional 60/40 Portfolio (Total Return Strategy)**

* **How It Works:** 60% stocks, 40% bonds. Withdraw $47,000 in year one, increase the payout annually for inflation, and rebalance once a year by selling assets [6].
* **Paycheck Score:** **63 / 100** [7, 8]
* **Overall Results:** Survived about 80% of historical 30-year periods overall [9]. However, during the 10 worst market starting points, it survived only **3 out of 10 times** (running out of money by year 20 in the 1966 market test) [9, 10].
* **Benefits:**
  + Simple, liquid, transparent, and avoids complex products or dividend-chasing [6, 8].
  + Performs very well during neutral or bull markets (e.g., left $1+ million in real value in a median 1951 start and over $3 million in a 1988 start) [11, 12].
* **Drawbacks:**
  + Fully exposed to **sequence of returns risk** [8, 9].
  + Forces retirees to sell depressed stocks during market drops to fund living expenses, permanently locking in losses and destroying portfolio longevity [9, 10].

---

### **2. Pure Income Portfolio ("Income First")**

* **How It Works:** 65% dividend-paying stocks, 35% bonds [7]. Paychecks are funded purely by stock dividends and bond interest without ever selling principal; the paycheck fluctuates based on what the portfolio generates [7, 13].
* **Paycheck Score:** **85 / 100** *(2nd Place)* [8, 14]
* **Overall Results:** Survived **100%** of all 30-year and 35-year historical periods, achieving the highest median ending portfolio balance (~$1.8 million) [15, 16].
* **Benefits:**
  + Completely eliminates sequence of returns risk because principal is never sold [15].
  + Outstanding wealth preservation and legacy tool for leaving money to heirs [8, 15, 17].
* **Drawbacks:**
  + **Income Unreliability:** The natural yield met the full income goal only about half the time (~50% of years resulted in mandatory pay cuts) [8, 17, 18].
  + Prioritizes preserving account value over lifestyle, forcing retirees to cut back spending when market yields are low [8, 17-19].

---

### **3. Partial Guaranteed Income (Annuity + 60/40 Portfolio)**

* **How It Works:** Uses $250,000 (25%) to purchase a joint lifetime annuity paying a fixed $15,500/year (6.2% payout rate), with the remaining $750,000 invested in a 60/40 portfolio [14, 20].
* **Paycheck Score:** **59 / 100** *(4th Place / Last Place)* [8, 21]
* **Overall Results:** Survived roughly 75% of 30-year periods overall [21]. In the 10 worst market starts, it survived only **2 out of 10 times** (running out of portfolio money by year 19 in the 1966 test) [10, 21].
* **Benefits:**
  + Creates a floor of predictable, lifetime income that pays out regardless of market swings or how long you live [20, 22].
  + Reduces the initial withdrawal burden on the remaining stock/bond portfolio in year one [20].
* **Drawbacks:**
  + **Inflation Vulnerability:** Standard annuity income is flat and does not adjust for inflation [21, 22].
  + Over time, as living costs rise, the smaller $750,000 investment portfolio must bear a heavier load, making it run out faster than the plain 60/40 strategy during inflationary periods [10, 21, 22].

---

### **4. Buckets & Guardrails Strategy**

* **How It Works:** Segregates $1 million into three distinct buckets:

  1. *Cash Bucket:* $94,000 (2 years of immediate income) [23].
  2. *Income Bucket:* $235,000 (~5 years of stable bonds/income) [23].
  3. *Growth Bucket:* $671,000 (Stocks for long-term growth) [23].

  + Protects short-term income in cash so you never sell stocks at a loss during bear markets [23, 27].
  + **Dynamic Adaptation:** Automatically cuts spending slightly when markets crash (preventing portfolio failure) and awards raises when markets boom (allowing you to enjoy your money) [24, 26-28].
  + Delivers the best balance of safety, inflation protection, and lifestyle maximization [16, 25, 27].
* **Drawbacks:**

  + Requires the emotional discipline to accept temporary spending reductions during prolonged bear markets [29].
  + Slightly more complex to set up and track than a basic static withdrawal plan [24, 28].

The methodology for setting and managing trailing stop-losses is broken down as follows:

---

### 1. The Core Philosophy: "Three Outcomes. Never a Fourth."

Every position is allowed to end in only three ways [2, 3]:

* **A Big Win:** The stock breaks out and runs. You hold it as long as it stays above its rising baseline, moving your stop up behind it [3].
* **A Small Win:** The stock moves up slightly and then rolls over, triggering your stop to lock in a minor gain [3].
* **A Small Loss:** The trade fails early, and the stop takes you out quickly with a small, manageable loss [3].

**The Rule:** There is **never a fourth outcome**—a big loss [3]. Losses beyond 20% to 30% are considered completely avoidable by automating the exit in advance [3].

---

### 2. The "Recovery Math" Behind Small Losses

The workbook emphasizes that limiting loss size is pure arithmetic, not caution [4]:

* A **10% loss** requires an **11% gain** to break even [5].
* A **30% loss** requires a **43% gain** to get back to level [5].
* A **50% loss** requires a **100% gain** (doubling your money) just to break even [4].
* A **70% loss** requires a **233% gain** to recover [4].

Automating the exit ensures you never fall to the bottom of the table, where recovering lost capital becomes mathematically unviable [4].

---

### 3. Step-by-Step Trailing Stop Protocol

#### **Step 1: Set the Order Immediately Upon Buying**

Place the stop-loss order at the exact same moment you buy [1]. This removes emotion, fear, and hope from the decision because you set the order while you are calm—not while staring at a plummeting chart at 9:00 AM [1].

#### **Step 2: Place It Relative to Support**

Position the stop-loss slightly below the moving-average line that the stock is respecting (for example, the 50-day moving average) [6].

#### **Step 3: Size the Stop Width to Asset Volatility**

Do not set arbitrary tight stops (like 2% or 3%) on volatile stocks [6]. Match the stop width to how much the stock actually moves daily [6].

* *Example:* For a high-volatility small-cap stock that normally moves 20% in a single day, set a wider 20% stop [6]. Setting a stop too tight will get you knocked out by routine noise on day two before the actual trend develops [6]. If a wide stop exceeds your personal risk tolerance, buy calmer assets instead [6].

#### **Step 4: Trail the Stop Up behind New Highs**

As the stock climbs and takes out previous highs, move the stop-loss up behind it [6, 7].

#### **Step 5: The One-Way Rule**

**The stop only ever moves one way—UP [1, 7]. Never move a stop down [7].**
Once the trailing stop is set and adjusted, step away from the screen [7]. If the market turns and volume selling hits, the automated order takes you out on your terms without requiring real-time emotional decisions [7, 8].

Here are the different types of financial investments available and how they work, categorized by their primary characteristics:

### Cash and Short-Term Investments

These investments prioritize keeping your money safe and accessible while generating some yield, usually for short-term goals or emergencies.

* **Savings Accounts:** These bank accounts offer high liquidity, meaning you can easily access your money when needed for bills or emergencies [1, 2]. They are protected by FDIC insurance up to $250,000 per person, per bank [2].
* **Certificates of Deposit (CDs):** CDs are time-deposit accounts issued by banks where you agree to leave your money untouched for a specified period (from 1 month to 20 years) [3]. In return, the bank pays a fixed interest rate, but you may face a penalty if you withdraw the money early [3]. You can also build "CD ladders" by staggering maturities to free up cash at preset intervals [4].
* **Credit Union Share Certificates (CUSCs):** These work exactly like CDs but are issued by credit unions and are insured by the NCUA [5, 6]. Instead of paying interest, they distribute earnings in the form of fixed dividends [7].
* **Money Market Mutual Funds:** These are mutual funds that invest in very short-term, low-risk debt securities [8]. They aim to maintain a stable price of $1.00 per share [9]. They come in categories like government (holding US Treasury securities), prime (corporate and government securities), and municipal (state and local debt) [8].

### Fixed-Income Investments (Bonds)

Bonds are essentially loans you make to an institution (like a government or corporation) that pays you back with interest.

* **US Treasurys:** Debt issued by the US government, making them highly secure [10]. They are exempt from state and local taxes and come in several forms, including short-term Treasury bills, medium-term notes, long-term bonds, and Treasury inflation-protected securities (TIPS) [11, 12].
* **Municipal Bonds ("Munis"):** Debt issued by states, cities, or public agencies [10, 13]. The interest they pay is generally exempt from federal income taxes, and often from state and local taxes if you live in the issuing state, making them highly attractive for high-income earners [13].
* **Corporate Bonds:** Debt issued by companies [10]. These typically offer higher yields than government bonds but come with greater credit risk, meaning the company could default on the loan [10].
* **Bond Funds:** Instead of holding individual bonds until they mature, you can buy into bond funds (mutual funds or ETFs) that hold a diversified portfolio of bonds [14]. These can be actively managed by professionals or set to track an index, but their value fluctuates daily with market prices [14].

### Equities (Stocks and Funds)

Equities represent ownership in companies and are generally used for long-term growth to outpace inflation.

* **Index Funds:** These funds track specific market indexes (like the S&P 500 or the NASDAQ) rather than trying to pick individual winning stocks [15, 16]. They allow you to invest across hundreds or thousands of companies at once, offering broad diversification and typically very low management fees [15, 17]. Some focus on specific segments, such as large companies, mid-sized companies (Mid-Cap), or companies with high Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) standards [18-20].
* **Dividend-Paying Stocks:** Some companies pay out a portion of their profits to shareholders in the form of dividends [21]. "Qualified" dividends from US corporations are particularly beneficial because they are taxed at favorable long-term capital gains rates rather than standard income rates [21, 22].

### Annuities (Insurance-Based Investments)

Annuities are contracts with insurance companies designed to protect savings or guarantee income.

* **Deferred Fixed Annuities:** You invest money with an insurance company, which guarantees a specific fixed interest rate over a set period (usually 3 to 10 years) [23]. The earnings grow tax-deferred until you withdraw them [23].
* **Qualified Longevity Annuity Contracts (QLACs):** A specific type of deferred income annuity purchased with retirement funds (like an IRA or 401k) [24]. You pay a lump sum upfront, and it provides a contractually guaranteed lifetime income starting later in life (up to age 85), acting as "longevity insurance" against outliving your savings [24].

### Professionally Managed Solutions

* **Separately Managed Accounts (SMAs):** An investment portfolio owned by you but actively managed by a professional advisor [21, 25]. They offer transparency and allow the manager to use tax-efficient techniques, like tax-loss harvesting—selling assets that have lost value to offset taxes on your gains [25].

### 1. Understanding Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

RMDs are mandatory withdrawals that the IRS requires you to take from specific retirement accounts annually. The government allows tax-deferred growth while you are working, but eventually requires you to withdraw the funds so they can be taxed [1].

* **Applicable Accounts:** RMDs apply to Traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s, SEP IRAs, and SIMPLE IRAs [1]. Notably, **Roth IRAs** are not subject to RMDs during the owner's lifetime [2], [3]. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, pre-death RMDs from **Roth 401(k)** accounts have also been eliminated starting in 2024 [4].
* **The Age Rule:** The age to begin RMDs increased to **73** in 2023. It is scheduled to rise to **75** in 2033 [5], [6].
* **Calculation:** The amount is calculated by dividing your account balance (as of December 31 of the previous year) by a life expectancy factor provided by the IRS [7].
* **Penalties:** Failing to take an RMD results in a penalty on the amount not withdrawn. Previously 50%, the SECURE 2.0 Act reduced this excise tax to **25%**, and further down to **10%** if the error is corrected in a timely manner [8], [9].
* **Tax Impact:** RMDs are taxed as ordinary income. A significant influx of taxable income from RMDs can trigger the "**Tax Torpedo**," a phenomenon where rising income causes Social Security benefits to become taxable, effectively creating marginal tax rates significantly higher than your actual tax bracket [10], [11]. High RMDs can also trigger **IRMAA** (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount), which increases Medicare Part B and D premiums [12].

### 2. The Roth Conversion Strategy

A Roth conversion involves moving funds from a pre-tax account (like a Traditional IRA) to a Roth IRA. You pay income tax on the converted amount in the year of the conversion, but future growth and withdrawals are tax-free [13].

* **RMD Management:** Since Roth IRAs do not have RMDs, converting pre-tax funds reduces the balance subject to future RMDs. This can lower your future taxable income and potentially prevent you from entering higher tax brackets later in retirement [14], [3].
* **Timing the "Gap Years":** Financial planning research suggests executing conversions during the "gap years"—the period after you retire but before you claim Social Security or start RMDs. During this time, your taxable income may be artificially low, allowing you to convert funds at a lower tax rate than you might face once RMDs begin [15].
* **The "Tax Torpedo" Defense:** By converting funds early, retirees can reduce the size of their tax-deferred accounts. This lowers future RMDs, which helps avoid the sharp spike in marginal tax rates caused by the interaction of RMD income and Social Security taxation [16], [17].
* **Risks and Phaseouts:**
  + Conversions increase your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) for that year. This can inadvertently trigger phaseouts for deductions, such as the senior tax deduction or State and Local Tax (SALT) deductions, effectively increasing the cost of the conversion [18], [19].
  + High income from a conversion in one year can spike Medicare premiums two years later due to IRMAA rules [20].

### 3. Gifting Strategies

Gifting is a method to either satisfy RMD requirements tax-efficiently or to purposefully utilize the "forced" income RMDs provide.

#### Charitable Gifting (QCDs)

Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs) allow you to transfer funds directly from your IRA to a qualified charity.

* **Tax Benefits:** A QCD counts toward your RMD requirement but is **excluded from your taxable income** [14]. This lowers your Adjusted Gross Income (AGI), which can help you avoid higher Medicare premiums and tax on Social Security benefits [21].
* **Age Requirement:** You can begin making QCDs at age **70½**, which is earlier than the RMD age of 73 [14].
* **Limits:** As of recent updates, individuals can donate up to **$105,000** (indexed for inflation, noted as $108,000 in one source) per year via QCDs [22].
* **Strategy:** This is ideal for "charitable givers" who intended to donate anyway. It is more tax-efficient than withdrawing the cash, paying tax, and then donating, because it keeps the income off your tax return entirely [22].

#### Generational Gifting

For retirees who do not need RMD income for living expenses, RMDs can serve as a "forcing mechanism" to transfer wealth to heirs while the retiree is still alive [23].

* **Annual Exclusion:** You can gift up to **$19,000** (2025 figure) per recipient annually without filing a gift tax return. Married couples can combine this to gift $38,000 per recipient [24], [25].
* **Lifetime Exemption:** If you gift more than the annual exclusion, it simply reduces your lifetime estate tax exemption, which is nearly $14 million for individuals and $28 million for couples [25].
* **Philosophy:** This strategy frames RMDs not as a tax burden, but as "permission" to help children or grandchildren now, rather than waiting to leave an inheritance [24].

### Summary of Strategic Flow

1. **If you want to lower future taxes:** Use **Roth Conversions** in your early retirement years (60s to early 70s) to reduce the account balance that generates RMDs [26].
2. **If you want to support a cause:** Use **QCDs** starting at age 70½ to satisfy RMDs without increasing your taxable income [14].
3. **If you have excess income:** Use the mandatory RMD withdrawals to fund **Generational Gifts** to family members tax-free (up to annual limits) [23].
