The Nature of Reality
The sources present varied, and at times seemingly contradictory, views on reality:
● Constructed Reality: Many sources assert that reality, as we experience it, is largely
a construct of the mind. This perspective suggests that reality is not fixed but is
continually created. What we perceive as reality is a blend of sensory information and
our brain's interpretations and predictions. It's described as a "story generated by our
brains" that usually matches the real world but is susceptible to influence. Some sources
go further, stating that our sense of self and the world is an altered state of
consciousness, built and constrained by the words we use.
● Objective Reality vs. Perceived Reality: While some acknowledge an "objective
reality" that exists independently of language or observers, they emphasize that our
access to it is always through our perception, which can be flawed. The "New Model of
Perception" even argues that physical reality, in its seamless, undivided wholeness,
is obscured by perception, which delivers an "articulated version" to us. This
model suggests that features and objects are "uniquely perceptual phenomena" and not
simply copied from the external world.
● Mind-Made Reality (Buddhist/Eastern Perspective): From a Buddhist standpoint, the
perceived phenomenal universe is considered illusory not necessarily as a fantasy, but
because our perceptions and preconditions mislead us into believing we are separate
from its elements. Some schools, like Dzogchen, literally consider perceived reality
unreal, describing all visions (perceptions through senses) as like a "big dream".
This view is rooted in the concept that reality is a "projection" resulting from karmic
seeds. It aligns with the idea that the "original nature of Mind" implies all phenomena are
"Mind born," like a dream experienced as real even though it's self-produced.
● Multiple Realities: William James, cited in the sources, suggested that there are
"subworlds of reality," each real in its own fashion while attended to, but whose
reality lapses with attention. This suggests a pluralistic view of reality, where different
sciences might depict reality in contradictory ways, yet these contradictions don't negate
the unity of reality itself.
The Role of Observation
Observation is presented as a transformative act, not a passive one:

● The Observer Effect: The act of looking at something changes it, an effect applicable
to people, animals, and even atoms. This means that observing behavior can distort
it, as individuals tend to act differently when aware of being watched. In quantum
physics, observation (which requires physical interaction) leads to the "collapse" of a
probability wave, meaning a potential state becomes an actual, observable result.
● Observation in Psychology: In the Ellipsis system for analyzing human behavior,
observation-influencing factors are considered circumstances within an interaction that
affect deception and stress analyses. The subjective nature of observation means "what
we're able to observe is only our limited reality, but doesn't define or affect the reality of
the object itself".

Cognition's Influence on Reality and Perception
Cognitive processes are deeply interwoven with how reality is perceived and constructed:
● Predictive Brain Model: The brain is viewed as a "predictive machine" that
constantly anticipates sensory signals and uses the comparison between predicted
and perceived signals to generate and update a mental model of the environment. This
"predictive coding" suggests that perception is a "controlled hallucination" where
top-down predictions are refined by bottom-up sensory error signals. Higher-order brain
regions, with longer timescales, are crucial for organizing perceptual input into
semantically meaningful schematic representations and exhibit anticipatory signals.
● Top-Down and Bottom-Up Processes: Perception is understood as an interaction
between sensory stimuli (bottom-up) and conceptual knowledge (top-down).
Expectations, motivations, attitudes, cultural values, beliefs, and physical/mental states
are all top-down factors that influence perception.
● Language and Categorization: Language is a primary tool of the "left-brain interpreter".
It can influence our perception of reality, for instance, by altering how we perceive
products through marketing. Language is categorical, focusing on individual parts, while
spatial processing (right brain) deals with the whole at once. Our ability to assign
meaning and talk about objects is ingrained in discourse and language communities,
making the "representation" of the world fluid. Words have the power to alter our
perception and moral interpretation.
● Beliefs and Expectations: Our beliefs significantly shape what happens in our brains
and bodies, influencing our perception to align with existing beliefs. For example, a
positive attitude towards math can lead to better memory function in the hippocampus
when doing math problems. Expectations can even influence the experience of pain.
● Unhelpful Thinking Patterns (CBT): Cognitive Behavioral Therapy identifies three
layers of unhelpful thinking: automatic thoughts, intermediate beliefs, and core beliefs.
These patterns contribute to psychosocial difficulties, and CBT aims to identify them and
generate more balanced thoughts by examining evidence for and against them.
● Metacognition: This refers to people's ability to predict and monitor their
understanding. It involves active choice and evaluation of strategies, consideration of

resources, and feedback. Optimizing metacognition through language is suggested as a
way to improve diagnostic reasoning.
● The "Self" as a Construct: Several sources, particularly those influenced by Eastern
philosophies and modern neuroscience, propose that the concept of the self is a
construct of the mind rather than a physical entity in the brain. The "left-brain
interpreter" is believed to constantly analyze experiences and create "stories" about
reality, including a sense of "me," often making up interpretations that are not necessarily
true. The self is described as "more like a verb than a noun," implying its existence is
dependent on the process of thinking. Without thought, the self may not exist.
Perception and its Mechanisms
Perception is described as a complex, active process:
● Active Construction: Perception is an active construction that goes beyond mere
sensory input. It's about translating and understanding what we receive through our
senses, curating information, and making sense of the world.
● Brain's Role in Images and Knowledge: Factual knowledge for reasoning and
decision-making comes to mind in the form of images, which are constructions of the
brain. These images can be perceptual (from current sensory input) or recalled (from
past experiences or future plans). The brain processes these images, and dispositional
representations (our full repository of knowledge) are activated, inducing changes in the
organism's state and altering the body image.
● Embodied Cognition: The body, as represented in the brain, is seen as the
indispensable frame of reference for mental processes. Our thoughts, actions, joys,
and sorrows use the body as a "yardstick". Feelings are highlighted as "cognitive" and
integral to reason, serving as internal guides by providing an image of our flesh
juxtaposed with other objects and situations, thereby modifying our comprehensive
notion of them.
● Frontal Cortex and Attention: The prefrontal cortices are essential for global attention
and working memory, receiving signals from all sensory regions and thus privy to signals
about virtually any activity in our mind or body. The frontal cortex is involved in "running
as-if experiments" when choosing among difficult options, integrating thoughts with "gut
feelings".
● Holistic vs. Articulated Perception: The "New Model of Perception" posits that the first
result of perception is a non-neural, holistic "phenomenal fabric" created by sense
organs. Perceptual functioning then "deconstructs" this holistic reality into
individuated phenomena (features, objects) through neural activity. This challenges
the "Current Model" which assumes perception constructs wholes from fragmented
features. The New Model views the organism's perception as a form of "organismic
self-use," where it uses its own functioning to create features and objects.
In summary, the sources convey that reality, as we experience it, is not a simple objective given.
Instead, it is a complex, dynamic, and often subjective construction deeply shaped by our
cognitive processes, including our brain's predictive mechanisms, the language we use, our

beliefs, expectations, and even the very act of observation. This "constructed reality" is a
continuous interplay between external sensory input and internal mental frameworks.
