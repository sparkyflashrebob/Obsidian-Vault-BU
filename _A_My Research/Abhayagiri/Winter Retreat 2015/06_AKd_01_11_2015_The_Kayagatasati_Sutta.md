* Abiku develops mindfulness of the body by immersing himself in rapture and pleasure born of concentration, as well as divesting himself of rapture and focusing on equanimity. He pervades his whole body with these mental states. Abiku also practices mindfulness while engaged in various activities throughout the day, such as sitting, lying down, walking, or standing. The awareness of the body is important for maintaining continuity in mindfulness. This practice should extend beyond formal meditation sessions to encompass all aspects of life. It is crucial to be aware not only of bodily sensations but also of one's state of mind during these activities. Mindfulness involves using appropriate attention and grounding oneself in the present moment through a sense of proprioception (awareness of body position). The contemplation on the 32 parts aims to deconstruct the sense of self around the body and develop neutrality towards it. Some practitioners may choose a specific part from this list as an object for concentration practice.
* The discussion revolves around the term "impurity" and its translation in Pali. The root word "asuba" means "not beautiful" or "unattractive," which has a different connotation compared to "foul" or "loathsome." Sensual desire exaggerates reality by selectively focusing on attractive aspects of an object. The teaching aims to see things as they are and achieve neutrality and clarity in perception. Words like impure carry cultural baggage, especially regarding purity and caste systems in Indian culture. Contemplating the body's cells can help disassociate from the notion of ownership over one's body. Various techniques exist to explore the sense of self in relation to the body.
