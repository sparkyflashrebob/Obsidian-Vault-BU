Build a creative action in mere minutes!
MATERIALS 3 piles of cards: Tactics, Principles, Theories • Pen & paper
PLAY 1. Name your issue - say, immigration; climate change. 2. Identify your goal - say, declare a Sanctuary City; close the local coal-fired
power plant. Remember: If you are doing this for real, use the campaign goals you already have. You, um, do have goals, don’t you? ;-)
3. Team-up. 2-6 people per team. 4. Draw. Each team draws 1 card from each pile. 5. Devise an action. Use the cards to spark an action idea that addresses your
issue & goal. (10 min) 6. Showtime! Teams present action ideas in creative ways (skit, song, news
report, tableau/human sculpture, 60-second Public Service Announcement (PSA), poster, etc.) Encourage an applause and invite a critique after each team’s performance.
7. Actually. Go. Do. It.
RESOURCES Build out your next creative action… for real! Along with the Beautiful Trouble Cards, get top-secret access to some stellar action-planning resources here: www.beautifultrouble.org/fuel .
OPTIONS ● Add in Methodologies &/or Stories. ● Draw another card from the pile if you’re stuck. ● Draw 3 cards from each pile, so your team has more tools to choose from in
planning your action. (Bonus for teams that use more than one card from each pile.)
● Use the SCORE Card &/or Risk Card to evaluate actions in Step 6. ● Play to win. Designate judge(s) to score each action idea. Award extra
points for creative presentations, SCORE and Risk evaluations, &/or for using ALL the cards in a team’s hand, etc. Then… announce winner(s)! And give out prizes!
● Strategize first. Prepare by going through the Strategy Cards , first. ● Record the ideas that come up. Especially, make note if any ideas spark a
“Hey, we could actually do that!” response, and then follow up.

TIPS FOR FACILITATORS ● Looking for an engaging activity for your workshop or classroom? This is
the core activity in the Beautiful Trouble Card Deck. It’ll quickly and playfully get your team or classroom designing their own actions and learning their way around the deck. Identify a community need, flip over a few cards, and — presto! — you’re now a rad action-architect & storyteller.
● Use a flipchart or white board to capture action ideas as they are presented. Take notes during feedback on each team’s ideas and presentation.
● Use a timer to signify to groups when the time for action planning is up. You may wish to go around to groups 3/4ths of the way through their allotted time to remind them to plan how they will creatively present their action to the larger group.
● Pick an issue in advance. There can be a big leap between naming the issue, identifying the goal, and then planning an action. For training purposes, it’s often best to propose an issue and goal to participants so that each group can focus on coming up with a unique approach to solving this
problem (instead of wasting precious time deciding which problem to solve). When every team has the same issue and goal, you can have a dynamic debrief on the merits or challenges of the tactics and principles used in each team’s action ideas. (Alternately, consider pre-writing scenarios on slips of paper and give one to each group.)
● Team names. It’s fun — and useful — to ask teams to come up with a team name and then during presentations, invite groups up to the front of the room by their team name.
*● What’s in your hand? Ask everyone else to guess what cards were used in the planning of the action a team presented. Or ask the group presenting to share their cards only after they’ve presented their action.*
● Creative tactics vs. “traditional” tactics. Point out how more “traditional” tactics — such as circulating a petition, meeting with an elected official, or writing a letter to the editor, etc. — are just as important (and often precede) the more creative and direct-action-esque tactics that the Beautiful Trouble card deck specializes in.
● Escalate Strategically . Have teams describe where the actions they’ve devised with this deck fit into their strategic escalation.
*● Not all tactics are created equal. Discuss the relative “weight” of tactics, for example, Divestment implies a long-term economic pressure campaign, while a Flash mob could be a fun, one-off, attention-getting action. Talk about how tactics can build on one another or in some cases be woven together.*
● Use Methodology: Action Star to make sure your action has takes into account 8 critical questions every action planner should ask themselves.
● Roses & Thorns. (A way to elicit constructive feedback and evaluate action ideas.) After each presentation, invite one (or more, if time) observation on what might work well (rose) about this action, and one critique (thorn).
● Let your body talk. Ask teams how it felt in their bodies to perform/present their action to the group. Did they feel stiff, relaxed, angry, excited, happy, hopeful…? How it feels in the body to act out an action can tell you a lot about how it might relate (or not) to the target audience.
● Movement ecology. Use Joanna Macy’s three concentric circles of the Great Turning to identify whether proposed actions are part of 1) resisting destruction, 2) building the future, or 3) shifting the culture.
*● The Principle test. Another simple way to evaluate actions is to pull a principle card and asses (see Evaluate your Action ).*
*● Know your cultural terrain / Take leadership from the most impacted / Solidarity, not aid . Introduce these cards as guiding principles for the whole activity.*
● Before facilitating Design an Action , you could lead participants through Best Actions/Worst Actions ( see instructions here or the BATMo! Version HERE ), to help get creative juices going and as a warm-up to exploring what
makes a good action. It’s also a great way for participants to get to know each other and the diversity of experience and work on myriad issues that’s in the room. Doing this activity first gets people thinking about the specifics of actions as opposed to long-term campaigns.
*● Ice-breaker. Another warm-up activity you can do with the deck is Divine the Future . Spread cards face-up on a table and ask participants to introduce themselves with the card they chose. (You might then invite participants to hold onto their card and use it (in addition to the three cards their team draws) to Design an Action.*
● Encourage participants to learn more. Each card includes a unique “pop-up code.” Turn your smartphone camera on and hover over the card. See what happens?! Your smartphone automatically pops up a link to the full version of that card in the Beautiful Trouble online toolbox.
● Don’t forget to capture your best ideas. For all of these activities, you may want to designate a notetaker who can write down — or quickly take pictures with their phone — any ideas your team generates during the game.
WHAT PEOPLE ARE SAYING In 2015, in Amman, Jordan, a group of activists at a Beautiful Trouble workshop used the cards to dream up an action to ask hiphop artist Lauryn Hill to cancel her concert in Tel Aviv, in solidarity with the Palestinian call for boycott, divestment, and sanctions. And soon after the workshop they launched a clever parody video and hashtag campaign that helped galvanize public support to pressure Lauryn Hill to cancel her concert, giving a win to the global BDS movement.
