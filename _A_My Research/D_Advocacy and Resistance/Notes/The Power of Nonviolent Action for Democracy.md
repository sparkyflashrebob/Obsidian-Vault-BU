Nonviolent action is a diverse and active technique of struggle that employs methods of protest, persuasion, noncooperation, and intervention to achieve political objectives without the use of violence [1-36]. It is considered a powerful means to protect and restore democracy by fundamentally shifting power dynamics away from authoritarian regimes [37-41].

**Efficacy of Nonviolent Action in Democracy Protection:**
Research indicates that nonviolent campaigns are significantly more likely to succeed against authoritarian regimes, especially when they are large and sustained [42-44]. This success stems from the understanding that a ruler's power is ultimately dependent on the consent and cooperation of the ruled, which can be voluntarily withdrawn [32, 38, 40, 45-49].

* **Mass Participation:** Large, mass movements that engage a broad range of communities and constituencies—including workers, teachers, lawyers, truck drivers, farmers, merchants, and students—are very difficult for regimes to repress [33, 43, 44, 50-52]. A key finding in civil resistance research suggests that no regime has remained in power when 3.5% of the population has engaged in active protest [43].
* **Withdrawal of Consent and Loyalty Shifts:** Nonviolent action targets the "pillars of support" that uphold an authoritarian regime, such as businesses, labor, faith organizations, education, civil servants, and security forces [33, 38, 40, 41, 45-47, 53-85]. When individuals within these pillars withdraw their support and cooperation—for example, through strikes, boycotts, or refusal to carry out illegal orders—it becomes difficult or impossible for autocrats to maintain power [37, 38, 44, 45, 50].

**Categories and Methods of Nonviolent Action:**
Nonviolent action encompasses a wide repertoire of tactics, generally falling into three main categories: protest and persuasion, building alternatives, and non-cooperation [37, 86, 87]. Gene Sharp cataloged 198 methods of nonviolent action [1-27, 29, 30, 36, 88-143].

1. **Protest and Persuasion:** These tactics are used to express disapproval, overcome fear and apathy, promote solidarity, and signal unjust situations [86]. Examples include:

   * **Formal Statements:** Public speeches (e.g., Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech) [29, 88], letters of opposition or support, declarations by organizations, signed public statements, and petitions [29, 88].
   * **Communications with a Wider Audience:** Slogans, caricatures, symbols [29, 89], banners, posters [89, 120], leaflets [89, 120, 144, 145], newspapers, journals, radio, and television [89, 120].
   * **Group Representations:** Deputations, mock awards, group lobbying, picketing, and mock elections [90, 120, 128].
   * **Symbolic Public Acts:** Displays of flags and colors, wearing symbols, prayer, delivering symbolic objects, protest disrobings, destruction of one's own property, symbolic lights, displays of portraits, paint as protest, new signs and names, symbolic sounds, symbolic reclamations, and rude gestures [4, 91, 120, 129].
   * **Pressures on Individuals:** Haunting and taunting officials, fraternization, and vigils (e.g., "Vigil for Water and Conscience" in Turkey) [5, 92, 121].
   * **Drama and Music:** Humorous skits, pranks, performances of plays and music, and singing (e.g., open-air ballet class in solidarity with male dancers) [93, 121, 146, 147].
   * **Processions:** Marches (e.g., March on Washington, protests against Brazil's president), parades, pilgrimages, religious processions, and motorcades [94, 121].
   * **Honoring the Dead:** Political mourning, mock funerals (e.g., scientists protesting funding policy), demonstrative funerals, and homage at burial places [95, 121].
   * **Public Assemblies:** Assemblies of protest or support (e.g., impromptu protests against Boris Johnson's suspension of parliament), protest meetings, camouflaged meetings of protest, and teach-ins [96, 121].
   * **Withdrawal and Renunciation:** Walk-outs, silence, renouncing honors, and turning one's back (e.g., Colin Kaepernick kneeling during the National Anthem) [97, 121].
2. **Building Alternatives:** These tactics strengthen the capacity and self-reliance of a nonviolent movement and undermine support for authoritarian institutions [37, 86, 87, 148, 149]. Examples include: mutual aid groups, alternative communications and transportation systems, food cooperatives, underground clinics, and groups providing a positive vision for democracy [37, 148, 150]. Establishing strike funds to support striking workers is another example [37, 148, 150].
3. **Non-cooperation:** This involves intentionally disrupting the status quo by refusing to comply with demands or withdrawing support from unjust systems [37, 38, 122]. It is often the most powerful set of methods as it directly removes sources of power from an opponent [37, 38]. Examples include:

   * **Social Noncooperation:** Ostracism of persons (e.g., bar associations boycotting judicial ceremony in Turkey) [11, 98, 121], excommunication, interdict, social boycotts, selective social boycotts, Lysistratic nonaction [11].
   * **Economic Noncooperation:** Boycotts by consumers (e.g., #logout boycott by restaurants in India, George Clooney's boycott of Brunei-owned hotels) [87, 90, 103, 118], workers restricting labor or going on strike (e.g., Swiss women demanding higher pay, Irish farmers refusing cattle to meat plants) [17, 102], producers' boycotts, suppliers' and handlers' boycotts [102], refusal to let or sell property, lockouts, refusal of industrial assistance, merchants' "general strike," withdrawal of bank deposits, refusal to pay fees/dues, refusal to pay debts/interest, severance of funds/credit, revenue refusal, refusal of a government's money, and traders' boycotts [15, 103, 121].
   * **Political Noncooperation:** Rejection of authority (e.g., Church of England withdrawing funds from climate-change contributing companies) [21, 112, 122], citizens' noncooperation with government (e.g., Ethiopian opposition parties threatening election boycott) [20, 110, 122], citizens' alternatives to obedience, rejection of authority, action by government personnel (e.g., Naval Academy grads opposing anti-DEI directive, Desiree Grace resisting Trump's orders) [53, 113, 123, 151], international governmental action (e.g., Putin cancelling trip to Paris due to war crimes accusations) [22, 114, 123], and government actions like domestic/international embargoes and blacklisting [16, 104, 121].
   * **Nonviolent Intervention:** Psychological (e.g., fasting, hunger strike, vigils) [115, 123], physical (e.g., sit-ins, stand-ins, ride-ins, wade-ins, mill-ins, pray-ins, nonviolent raids) [116, 124], social (e.g., establishing new social patterns, overloading facilities, stall-ins, speak-ins, guerrilla theater, alternative social institutions, alternative communication systems) [117, 124], economic (e.g., reverse strike, stay-in strike, nonviolent land seizure, defiance of blockades, counterfeiting, preclusive purchasing, seizure of assets, dumping, selective patronage, alternative markets/transportation/economic institutions) [25, 118, 124], and political intervention (e.g., overloading administrative systems, disclosing secret agents' identities, seeking imprisonment, civil disobedience of "neutral" laws, work-on without collaboration, dual sovereignty and parallel government) [119, 124].

**Connecting to Authoritarianism's Playbook:**
Authoritarian leaders follow a similar playbook globally, based on fear, divide-and-rule tactics, undermining power constraints, and punishing opposition to stay in power indefinitely [152]. Key tactics of authoritarianism include:

* **Politicizing independent institutions:** Targeting central banking, law enforcement, courts, election administration, etc., to turn them into weapons against adversaries [153].
* **Quashing dissent:** Weakening freedom of speech and the press, and targeting public voices like media, civil society, activists, and religious leaders [154].
* **Stoking violence:** Deliberately ignoring or inflaming politically useful violence to create cover for civil liberty restrictions or expanding security measures [155].

Nonviolent action directly counters these tactics by enabling mass defiance, forcing loyalty shifts within institutions, and building alternative structures that are outside authoritarian control [37, 38, 43, 86, 87, 148, 156]. For example, public assemblies and communications with wider audiences directly challenge the quashing of dissent [86, 88, 89, 96]. Noncooperation from civil servants or the judiciary can resist the politicization of independent institutions and illegal orders [37, 38, 113, 151, 157].

**Historical and Contemporary Examples:**

* **US Civil Rights Movement:** Dismantled racial authoritarianism in the Jim Crow South using boycotts, strikes, sit-ins, freedom rides, and other nonviolent resistance campaigns [42, 86]. Martin Luther King Jr.'s March on Washington is a prime example of nonviolent protest and persuasion [88].
* **Chile:** Striking coal miners, people banging pots and pans, and slow marches contributed to bringing down the Pinochet dictatorship [42].
* **South Africa:** A united democratic front of unions, faith organizations, and civic groups used strikes and targeted boycotts of white-owned businesses and divestment to lead to democratic transition [42].
* **South Korea:** A mass movement of youth, opposition legislators, and trade unions pushed back against martial law and impeached their autocratic leader [42].
* **Guatemala:** Citizens used nonviolent methods to challenge corrupt officials and organized crime [36].
* **Naval Academy Graduates:** Successfully crowdfunded to replace books removed from the library due to anti-DEI directives, demonstrating building alternatives backing up non-cooperation and protest [53].
* **Hong Kong:** A man intervened nonviolently between protesters and police to prevent violence [117].

**Importance of Discipline and Strategy:**
Successful pro-democracy movements emphasize strategic nonviolence, which involves agreements on actions and accountability [158-160]. This approach helps movements grow by inviting new people, allows for open organization, and makes it easier to reject provocations to violence from oppressors [83, 159, 161-163]. Political scientists Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan's research shows that an armed flank dramatically reduces a movement's ability to attract widespread participation [80, 161]. Strategic planning tools, like "Beautiful Trouble," provide methodologies for assessing situations, planning campaigns, and continuously innovating strategy, including identifying strengths, weaknesses, threats, and opportunities, and choosing tactics that support a chosen strategy [150, 164-168]. Learning from history is crucial for understanding past successes and failures in nonviolent struggle [79, 166, 169].

**Critique of "Democracy Promotion":**
The term "democracy promotion" as used by the US since the 1980s is described as an effort to penetrate and control emergent civil societies in targeted countries deemed unfriendly to American policymakers [59, 170-172]. This effort has involved funding "friendly" trade unions, political parties, feminist alliances, activist groups, and media to support US transnational interests, sometimes undermining indigenous radical movements that organically developed from struggles against US-backed dictatorships [170, 171, 173]. The goal is to suppress popular demands that could threaten US strategic military interests and corporate domination, essentially co-opting movements to channel them into alliances with global elites [171, 173].

To further analyze nonviolent action's role in democracy protection, we could delve into specific case studies of "backsliding democracies" and examine how nonviolent tactics were or could be applied to resist authoritarian consolidation, considering the nuances of economic versus political strategies.
