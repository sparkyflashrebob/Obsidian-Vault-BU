[[Bard - Wet vs Dry]]
You're right, "wet" and "dry" can also refer to specific categories of insight within Buddhist meditation, particularly in the context of Theravada Buddhism and Vipassana practice.

Here's a more accurate explanation of their meaning in this context:

**Wet insight:**

- Refers to insights that arise from observing bodily sensations, emotions, and mental phenomena that are **intense, strong, and often unpleasant**. These insights can be related to the impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), and non-self (anatta) of these experiences.
- Examples of wet insights: Observing the pulsating, burning, or uncomfortable nature of sensations, the fleeting and unsatisfactory nature of emotions, or the impersonal and constantly changing nature of thoughts.

**Dry insight:**

- Refers to insights that arise from observing phenomena that are **subtle, light, and often pleasant**. These insights can also be related to the three characteristics, but from a more neutral or peaceful perspective.
- Examples of dry insights: Observing the impermanence of subtle vibrations in the body, the fleeting nature of pleasant sensations, or the impersonal and constantly arising nature of thoughts without judgment.

The distinction between wet and dry insights helps meditators to understand the **range and depth of their practice**. It encourages them to observe all phenomena with equanimity, regardless of their intensity or pleasantness.

[[streamentry - the practice of meditation]]

**Q: What are "wet" and "dry" insight practice?** 
**A**: The term "wet insight practice" refers to insight investigation supported by samādhi, states of unification of mind resulting from concentration practice. The term comes from the fact that samādhi "lubricates" insight-oriented meditation, supporting it with states of calm, collectedness, and joy. This is in contrast to "dry insight practice", in which insight investigation is pursued directly without first training the mind in samādhi. Dry insight practice is sometimes regarded as a shortcut to insight, at the cost of a potentially rough and destabilizing path of progress. 

**Q: What are examples of wet and dry insight practice?** **A**: The most common form of "wet" insight practice is awareness of the breath (ānāpānasati). In this practice one uses the sensations of the breath, either at a focal point such as the tip of the nose or in the whole body, as a meditation object for developing samādhi (stable attention and a bright, calm, and joyful mind). Awareness of breath sensations can also serve as a vehicle for insight investigation, so this practice simultaneously cultivates both śamatha/samādhi and vipassanā. The most common form of "dry" insight practice is *noting*, in which one continually attends to and assigns simple verbal labels to moment-to-moment experience. This is a dry insight practice because no attempt is made to cultivate states of calm, joy, and stable attention.
