- perception of time passing depends on the signals that our brains receive from our bodies. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfak3jxnmgcp2tzwr358mz1))
- People can feel time flying by when they are busy or excited about something ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfakc9m4qsr9h2jb1synadq))
- but at other moments, time can drag when people are bored. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfakh06ybf2qc6ytsje4mqf))
- This suggests that our experience of time is often distorted and does not correspond to real time. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfaknvrng3tp7d4x02vvxts))
- heart and the brain are in constant communication ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfam6xmfrqf71ff2kqzr09d))
- Dr. Arslanova from the Department of Psychology at Royal Holloway, said, “Our findings illustrate the point that the novelist Murakami was making in his novel ‘Kafka on the Shore,’ when he wrote “time expands, then contracts, all in tune with the stirring of the heart.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfana2j6waa7mf00q1wyxef))
- “These patterns of contraction and expansion demonstrate how our perception of time is constantly influenced by our internal physiological states.” ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfanm0011bgj2jknv0fs68c))
- time distortions arise from phasic modulations within each heartbeat. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfaq34vt18v9yq0gs9nfb3m))
- The results may have important implications given the role that timing plays in several high-skilled tasks that humans perform (e.g., driving, playing an instrument or certain sports) and the implication of time perception in several mental health conditions, such as depression and anxiety. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gvfaqcdaf4bf6p4kp77y3f6q))
