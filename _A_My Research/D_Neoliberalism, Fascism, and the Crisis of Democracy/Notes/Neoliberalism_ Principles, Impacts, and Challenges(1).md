Neoliberalism is widely understood as a political and economic ideology that emphasizes free market principles, deregulation, and individualism [1, 2]. It posits that human well-being is best advanced by maximizing individual entrepreneurial freedoms within an institutional framework characterized by strong private property rights, free markets, and free trade [1, 3]. Critics argue that neoliberalism has reshaped societies profoundly, leading to both perceived and actual impacts across various spheres.

Here's a comprehensive overview of what the sources say about neoliberalism and its impacts:

**1. Core Principles and Characteristics of Neoliberalism:**

* **Market Supremacy**: Neoliberalism promotes the supremacy of market value, advocating for privatization, deregulation, and the free flow of capital [1, 4-7]. It believes markets are self-sufficient and the best guarantors of human welfare [8, 9].
* **Minimal State (with caveats)**: While it theoretically advocates for a "hands-off" government that doesn't interfere with individuals or markets [3, 6], in practice, neoliberalism's survival has often been ensured through **state intervention** [3, 10]. This intervention is designed to protect and advance the market, rather than for social welfare [10, 11].
* **Individualism and Personal Responsibility**: It promotes a culture of self-reliance, encouraging individuals to take responsibility for their own successes and failures, often with little regard for structural barriers or systemic injustices [6, 12]. This can lead to a culture of blame, where individuals are held accountable for their circumstances [12].
* **"Human Capital"**: Neoliberalism reconceives human beings as "human capital," tasked with improving and leveraging their competitive positioning and enhancing their value across all endeavors [5, 8, 13-17]. This transformation makes the idea of education as a democratic public good "unintelligible" [5].
* **Competition**: Competition replaces exchange as the market's fundamental principle. Consequently, **inequality becomes legitimate, even normative**, when market principles are extended to every sphere [18].
* **Historical Associations**: Neoliberalism is strongly associated with thinkers like Friedrich August von Hayek and Milton Friedman, and political figures such as Augusto Pinochet in Chile, Ronald Reagan in the United States, and Margaret Thatcher in the United Kingdom [19-21].

**2. Impact on Freedom:**

* **Redefinition of Freedom**: Neoliberalism promotes a particular model of freedom tied to "free markets, property rights, and entrepreneurial self-ownership" [22]. This economic understanding of freedom is presented as the only valid form, often obscuring other dimensions of liberty [23-26].
* **Paradoxical Unfreedom**: Despite being organized around the idea of freedom, neoliberalism has been linked to **"high levels of unfreedom"** [27, 28]. Critics argue that it "tears up freedom’s grounding in sovereignty for states and subjects alike" [17].
* **"Freedom as Capture"**: Neoliberal freedom is described as a "profound form of capture" where individuals "most perfectly live our own domination" through an internalized sense of control [29, 30]. The "seductive image" of this freedom compensates for actual precariousness [31].
* **Undelivered Promises**: The "manifest failure" of neoliberalism to deliver on its promises of freedom has become increasingly recognized [32]. This includes the transformation of "flexibility" (sold as freedom) into precarity and indebtedness [30, 33].

**3. Impact on Democracy and Political Life:**

* **Erosion of Democracy**: Neoliberalism "assaults the principles, practices, cultures, subjects, and institutions of democracy" [34, 35]. It produces widespread misery and suffering, weakening any "vestige of democracy that interferes with its vision of a self-regulating market" [20].
* **Economization of Everything**: It "economizes every sphere and human endeavor," replacing a model of society based on the social contract with society conceived and organized as markets [5, 14, 35, 36]. This leads to the **reinterpretation of democracy as the market itself**, effectively ending democratic politics [8, 9].
* **Shift in Sovereignty**: Political sovereignty is largely replaced by **economic sovereignty**, with corporate power taking over governance [37, 38]. Policy is increasingly fashioned by lobbyists for big businesses, leading to "raw money power wielded by the few undermining all semblances of democratic governance" [37, 38].
* **Dismantling the Social**: Neoliberalism wages a battle against notions of the social contract, solidarity, the collective imagination, and the public good [23, 24]. Public goods, like universities, libraries, and public services, become difficult to justify or secure publicly, with government increasingly seen as just another market actor [13].
* **De-democratization**: It de-democratizes political culture and discredits norms such as inclusion, pluralism, tolerance, and equality [39]. This leads to a "comfortable, smooth, reasonable, democratic unfreedom" [40].

**4. Economic and Social Impacts:**

* **Increased Inequality**: Neoliberal policies, such as tax cuts for the wealthy and deregulation, have been associated with a **widening gap between the rich and the poor** [2, 7]. The top income strata acquire more wealth, while the middle and lower strata experience stagnant wages, fewer benefits, and decreased upward mobility [41, 42].
* **Slower Growth**: Contrary to its promises, the US economy experienced **slower growth rates** after 1980, coinciding with neoliberalism's rise [43].
* **Financial Sector Deregulation**: Deregulation of the financial sector led to higher financial profits, increased extraction, and the 2008 financial crisis [44].
* **Erosion of Labor Rights**: There is a "consistently strong, negative relationship between neoliberal policies and respect for core worker rights" [45]. Efforts to increase participation in the global economy are often accompanied by reduced protection for workers, supporting the "race to the bottom" dynamic [46-48].
* **Social Disconnection**: Neoliberalism has been linked to **reduced social cohesion** and can reduce well-being by promoting a sense of social disconnection, competition, and loneliness [49, 50].
* **Mental Health**: It has been linked to **increased rates of depression and anxiety** due to the pressure to constantly perform and achieve [2].

**5. Connection to Authoritarianism and Fascism:**

* **Authoritarian Neoliberalism**: This term describes a shift where democratic governments, facing economic stagnation and declining legitimacy, expand their authoritarian toolkit to impose neoliberal accumulation strategies [51-53]. This has manifested as **increased police powers, suppression of protests and civil liberties, and enhanced border security and restrictions on citizenship** [51, 54-56]. Examples include post-Brexit Britain under the Johnson administration and Macron's France, characterized by "explicit forms of 'othering' and hostility towards the wider political system" [51, 54, 57-60].
* **Neoliberal Fascism**: Some sources argue that neoliberalism acts as an "enabling force" that "weakens, if not destroys the commanding institutions of a democracy," thereby providing fertile ground for the "ideological architecture, poisonous values, and racist social relations sanctioned and produced under fascism" [61]. This involves a shift from state rule to **corporate sovereignty**, promoting a culture of fear, insecurity, and precarity that reinvigorates executive power and leads to a "punishing state" [61].
* **Shared Ideals**: "Neoliberalism and fascism conjoin and advance in a comfortable and mutually compatible project and movement that connects the worst excesses of capitalism with fascist ideals," such as the veneration of war, hatred of reason, ultra-nationalism, racial purity, and suppression of dissent [61].
* **Historical Context**: Neoliberal thought was born in the shadow of European fascism and Soviet totalitarianism, with founders like Hayek believing that Keynesian welfare states and social democracy were on a "road to serfdom" [62]. While some argue neoliberalism is philosophically different from fascism, others contend that its policies (e.g., weakening unions, slashing safety nets) strip away guardrails that prevent authoritarianism [63-65].
* **Colonial Legacies**: The shared, deep-rooted colonial legacies of countries like the UK and France are argued to provide a "particularly propitious context for the intensification of hostile governance of policing and border management," as these legacies can be "re-activated" in nationalizing narratives to preserve legitimacy for a failing socio-economic model [60].

**6. Mechanisms of Dissemination and Control:**

* **Governmentality**: Neoliberalism functions as a "governing rationality" that "economizes" every sphere of human endeavor and generates distinctive kinds of subjects [5, 30, 34, 36, 66-68]. This is a "new programming of liberal governmentality," which shifts from explicit command and punishment to "conducting and compelling populations 'at a distance'" through norms and incentives [67-69].
* **Psychopolitics and Surveillance**: This refers to control exercised through the use of personal information, social media, and big data, which, instead of limiting communication, stimulates "voluntary self-exposure" [70]. Surveillance is explicitly used to sustain market deregulation, identify threats, profile individuals, and manipulate the will and consent of the people by commodifying their data [71-73].
* **"Soft Power"**: In Euro-Atlantic nations, neoliberalism disseminated more subtly through "transformations of discourse, law, and the subject," rather than overt violence [67, 74].
* **"Shared Sacrifice"**: During periods of austerity, neoliberalism utilizes a discourse of "shared sacrifice" to legitimize individual tolerance of "insecurity, deprivation, and extreme exposure" to maintain national economic competitiveness, even when economic ends are delinked from the general welfare [75, 76].

**7. Criticisms and Resistance:**

* **Empirical Failures**: Studies indicate that neoliberalism has **failed to deliver on its promises** of increased growth, equality, or mobility [41, 77]. For instance, despite arguments for deregulation, the financial sector became less efficient and more prone to crises, and antitrust relaxation did not lead to increased competition [44, 78].
* **Challenging Ideology**: Critics argue that neoliberalism is not merely an economic theory but a "political rationality" that reshapes society and human beings [36].
* **Forms of Resistance**: While neoliberal reforms provoke new patterns of dissidence, resistance can paradoxically perpetuate authoritarianism [79]. However, some scholars note the emergence of "zombie" forms of resistance, where workers and citizens explore alternative, fragmented avenues of grassroots struggle in the face of restrictions on organized labor and dissent [80]. Movements in various countries are mobilizing against the "twin evils of neoliberalism and fascism" [81].
