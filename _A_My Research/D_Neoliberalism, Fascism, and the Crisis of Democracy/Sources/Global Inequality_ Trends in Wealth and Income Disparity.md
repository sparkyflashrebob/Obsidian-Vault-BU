You're looking for specific statistics on inequality, which is a critical area of focus when discussing the impacts of neoliberalism. Here's a breakdown using common metrics and recent data, drawing from reputable sources like Oxfam, the World Inequality Database (WID), the World Bank, and the IMF.

Key Measures of Inequality

Before diving into numbers, it's helpful to understand the common ways inequality is measured:

Gini Coefficient (or Gini Index): This is the most widely used measure of income or wealth inequality. It ranges from 0 (perfect equality, where everyone has the same income/wealth) to 1 (perfect inequality, where one person has all the income/wealth). It's often expressed as a percentage (0 to 100).

Income Share of Top Percentiles: This measures what proportion of total income or wealth is held by the richest X% of the population (e.g., top 1%, top 10%).

Wealth vs. Income: It's crucial to distinguish between income inequality (how much people earn) and wealth inequality (how much people own in assets like property, stocks, and savings). Wealth inequality is generally much higher than income inequality.

Global and National Inequality Statistics

Here are some specific statistics, reflecting trends often associated with the rise of neoliberal policies:

1. Wealth Concentration at the Top (Oxfam Reports)

Oxfam consistently publishes reports highlighting extreme wealth concentration. Their data is often impactful due to its focus on the very richest:

Billionaire Wealth Surge:

As of January 2024, Oxfam reported that the world's five richest men had more than doubled their fortunes from $405 billion to $869 billion since 2020, at a rate of $14 million per hour.

During the same period (since 2020), nearly five billion people globally have become poorer.

Oxfam predicts that, if current trends continue, the world could have its first trillionaire within a decade, but poverty won't be eradicated for another 229 years. (Source: Oxfam "Inequality Inc." report, January 2024)

Richest vs. Poorest Half: In 2018, Oxfam reported that the 26 richest people in the world held as much wealth as half of the global population (the 3.8 billion poorest people). This figure was down from 43 people the year before, indicating an accelerating trend of concentration. (Source: UN, "Inequality – Bridging the Divide," citing Oxfam)

Unearned Wealth: Oxfam's January 2025 report, "Takers Not Makers," highlights that 60% of billionaire wealth is either from crony/corrupt sources, monopoly power, or is inherited. In 2024, for the first time, more billionaires were "minted" through inheritance than entrepreneurship, and every billionaire under 30 inherited their wealth. (Source: World Economic Forum, citing Oxfam, January 2025)

2. Income Share of the Top 1% (World Inequality Database)

The World Inequality Database (WID), led by economists like Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez, and Gabriel Zucman, provides granular data on income and wealth shares.

Global Income Share: The richest 10% today snap up 52% of all income, while the poorest half get just 8.5%. (Source: IMF, "Global Inequalities," citing World Inequality Report 2022)

Increase in Top 1% Share: From 1990 to 2015, the share of income going to the top 1% of the global population increased in 46 out of 57 countries with data. (Source: UN, "Inequality – Bridging the Divide")

Example - United States: The top one-tenth of one percent of the income distribution held 3.4 percent of US private income in 1980, and today that proportion approaches ten percent, a nearly tripling of their share. (Source: AIER, "Understanding Thomas Piketty's Capital in the 21st Century," citing Piketty and Saez)

3. Gini Coefficient Trends

Global Trends: While income inequality between countries has generally declined (largely due to economic growth in China and other emerging economies), income inequality within countries has largely risen. Today, 71% of the world's population lives in countries where inequality has grown. (Source: UN, "Inequality – Bridging the Divide")

Developed Countries: Since 1990, income inequality has increased in most developed countries and in some middle-income countries, including China and India. (Source: UN, "Inequality – Bridging the Divide")

4. Thomas Piketty's Research

In "Capital in the Twenty-First Century," Piketty argues that when the rate of return on capital (r) is greater than the rate of economic growth (g) over the long term (r > g), it leads to a concentration of wealth and increased inequality. His extensive historical data showed that this dynamic was a historical norm, only interrupted by the unique circumstances of the two World Wars and the Great Depression, which destroyed capital and led to policies promoting more equality. He argues that we are now returning to an era where r > g, leading to rising inequality.

Important Considerations:

Data Timelines and Comparability: Gini coefficients and income/wealth share data can vary slightly depending on the year, the source (e.g., World Bank, UNU-WIDER, WID, national statistical agencies), and whether it measures pre-tax/transfer income, post-tax/transfer income, or consumption.

Methodological Debates: While the overall trend of rising inequality in many countries is widely acknowledged, there are academic debates about the precise extent, the methodologies used, and the primary causes. For instance, some critics of Piketty have argued that his measures might not fully account for all forms of income or for social mobility. However, the general consensus among many economists and international organizations supports the observation of increasing inequality.

These statistics collectively illustrate the significant and often increasing levels of income and wealth inequality that have been observed in many parts of the world, particularly since the 1980s, a period often associated with the widespread adoption of neoliberal policies.
