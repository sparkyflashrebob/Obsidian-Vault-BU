This is Richard Wolff from Democracy at Work
responding to another Ask Prof. Wolff question
from our Patreon community. This question
comes from Kade Stevenson and I hope I'm
pronouncing the first name correctly.
Kade asks about the term classism,
and I presume then the adjective classist. What is
it? How is it used? What do I think about its use?
Well, obviously it has to do with class,
and if it's used in the same way sexism
and racism are used, it refers to a kind of
prejudice, a kind of hostility, a kind of
superiority complex in relation to
certain classes, races, sexes, and so on.
But before I can respond I have to go in briefly
to the fact that we don't all agree, you, I,
and the world, in general on what the
term class means. This is a very important
issue. We are often confronted with speeches,
statements, articles, and books that talk as if
we all know what the word means and we all agree
on what the word means. Neither of those is true.
So let's do this quickly. Five thousand years
or more, at least in the Western civilization,
class has been used as an important word in
the language that certainly goes back to the
ancient Greeks and before. Probably, the oldest,
simplest concept of class comes from the verb
“to classify,” to divide a group of people into
subgroups. You know, we can classify a classroom
as to the people that are tall and the people that
are short. We can divide any population into those
that are heavy and those that are light. Those
that have blue eyes, those that have brown eyes,
and so on. To classify simply means to
break a large group into subgroups. Okay.
In thinking about society, the communities in
which we live and work, human beings have found
it useful to classify, to break into groups the
different people in the community they live and
work in. And one of the ways to do that was
to say who has a lot of wealth or property
and who doesn't: the class of the rich and the
class of the poor. Then, of course, if you have a
little bit of subtlety you can identify classes in
the middle. The middle classes don't have as much
as the rich above them but have more than the poor
below them. Classes in terms of rich and poor,
the propertied and the property-less is a very old
way that human beings have used for a long time
to understand what's going on in their society.
People found it useful to notice how many people
are rich relative to how many people are poor
since everyone noticed there were tensions
and conflicts and envies between these classes.
Equally old is another concept that's quite
different. This other way of thinking about class
divides people not according to what they own (the
wealth, the income, the property) but rather
according to the power that they wield. Are
there people in the society who give others orders
on how to behave or are they the people to whom
orders are given and who do what they're told?
And once again, you can have some in the middle.
They don't have as much power as those above them
but they have more power than those below them.
Power and property are two different concepts
of class. You can, of course, criticize
people according to whether
they're in the rich class or
the poor class, or the middle class in
terms of wealth or in terms of power.
Now let me put my cards on the table. I
find interesting a new and different way of
understanding class and I get that understanding
from Karl Marx because he figured it out. He
didn't figure out classes in terms of wealth
and poor. That was done by people long before
Karl Marx existed, and the same with people who
thought of class in terms of power. Remember,
they're different. You can have a lot of wealth
and not much power. And you can have a lot of
power and not much wealth. It's really important
to understand that Joseph Biden doesn't have all
that much wealth but as president, he has a
lot of power and that there are very wealthy
people in the world that don't have much power
over other people for a whole host of reasons.
Here comes Marx. Marx says
there's another kind of class
that every society divides people into. There
are two groups in modern human history. One group
is the one that does the work, that literally
uses their brains and muscles to produce
the goods and services we all live on: food,
clothing shelter, transportation, education,
medical care, all of that. That's the majority.
They do the work. Then there's another group.
A small group that directs the large people
who do the work, tells them what to do,
tells them how to do it, owns whatever
they produce as fast as they produce it.
In slavery, the class of workers is called slaves
and the class of those who run the game are called
masters. In feudalism, those who do the work are
called serfs and the people who run the whole
system are called lords of the land. In our
economic system, capitalism, we likewise divide
people into two groups: employees and employers.
The mass of us are employees. A tiny minority
are employers and they run the show.
So if you're going to use the term class, please
be aware you have to make clear which of these
definitions you're going to use, and you have to
give a reason why you're using them. You cannot
pretend that we all see them in the same way.
Final point. I don't use classism as a critical
idea. I don't think I've done all that much
when I pinpoint a person as being in this class
or that class. I'm not really all that interested
in where the individual fits. I'm more interested
in understanding the system into which we all fit
in our different roles. I'm very interested in
the class structure and the class differences
but that's in order to understand and change
society. I don't want to hold individuals
responsible for the class into which, typically,
they were born through no choice of their own.
I hope this has been responsive. If not,
let us know and we'll try for another
answer in the future. This is Richard
Wolff for Democracy at Work. Thank you.
