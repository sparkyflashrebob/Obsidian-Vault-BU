The **Noble Eightfold Path** ( [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): आर्याष्टाङ्गमार्ग, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Sanskrit): *āryāṣṭāṅgamārga* ) [\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-1) [\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-2) or **Eight Right Paths** ( [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language): अष्टसम्यङ्मार्ग, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Sanskrit): *aṣṭasamyaṅmārga* ) [\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-3) is an early summary of the path of [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) practices leading to [liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlightenment_in_Buddhism) from [samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsara), the painful cycle of [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebirth_(Buddhism)), [\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGethin199881%E2%80%9383-4) [\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson201364%E2%80%9365-5) in the form of [nirvana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana). [\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTELopez2009136%E2%80%93137-6) [\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-auto-7)
The Eightfold Path consists of eight practices: right view, right resolve, right speech, right conduct, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right *samadhi* ('meditative absorption or union'; alternatively, equanimous meditative awareness). [\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198811%E2%80%9314-8)
In early Buddhism, these practices started with understanding that the body-mind works in a corrupted way (right view), followed by entering the Buddhist path of self-observance, self-restraint, and cultivating kindness and compassion; and culminating in *dhyana* or *samadhi* , which reinforces these practices for the development of the body-mind. [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9) In later Buddhism, insight ( *prajñā* ) became the central [soteriological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soteriology) instrument, leading to a different concept and structure of the path, [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9) [\[10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBronkhorst1993-10) in which the "goal" of the Buddhist path came to be specified as ending ignorance and [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebirth_(Buddhism)). [\[11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERaju1985147%E2%80%9351-11) [\[12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEliot201439%E2%80%9341-12) [\[13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey2016253%E2%80%9355-13) [\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson201364%E2%80%9365-5) [\[14\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliamsTribeWynne201252-14)
The Noble Eightfold Path is one of the principal summaries of the [Buddhist teachings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_(Buddhism)), taught to lead to *Arhatship* . [\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Cambridge_Univ._Press-15) In the [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada) tradition, this path is also summarized as *sila* (morality), *samadhi* (meditation) and *prajna* (insight). In [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana) Buddhism, this path is contrasted with the [Bodhisattva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva) path, which is believed to go beyond *Arhatship* to full [Buddhahood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhahood). [\[15\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Cambridge_Univ._Press-15)
In [Buddhist symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_symbolism), the Noble Eightfold Path is often represented by means of the [dharma wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma_wheel) ( *dharmachakra* ), in which its eight spokes represent the eight elements of the path.

#### Etymology and nomenclature

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=1)]
The Pali term *ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga* ( [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit): *āryāṣṭāṅgamārga* ) is typically translated in English as 'Noble Eightfold Path'. This translation is a convention started by the early translators of Buddhist texts into English, just like *ariya sacca* is translated as ' [Four Noble Truths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths)'. [\[16\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliams200241-16) [\[17\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellLopez200366-17) However, the phrase does not mean the path is noble, rather that the path is *of the noble people* ( [Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali): *ariya* , meaning 'enlightened, noble, precious people'). [\[18\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliams200252-18) The term *magga* (Sanskrit: *mārga* ) means 'path', while *aṭṭhaṅgika* (Sanskrit: *aṣṭāṅga* ) means 'eightfold'. Thus, an alternate rendering of *ariya aṭṭhaṅgika magga* is 'eightfold path of the noble ones', [\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson201364%E2%80%9365-5) [\[19\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswell2004296-19) [\[20\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-20) or 'Eightfold Ariya Path'. [\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-DavidsStede1921p695-21) [\[22\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-22) [\[23\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-23)
All eight elements of the Path begin with the word *samyañc* (in Sanskrit) or *sammā* (in Pāli) which means 'right, proper, as it ought to be, best'. [\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-DavidsStede1921p695-21) The Buddhist texts contrast *samma* with its opposite, *miccha* . [\[21\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-DavidsStede1921p695-21)
The Noble Eightfold Path, in the Buddhist traditions, is the direct means to nirvana and brings a release from the cycle of life and death in the realms of samsara. [\[24\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTELopez1995159-24) [\[25\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHirakawa199041-25)

##### Origins: the Middle Way

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=3)]
According to Indologist Tilmann Vetter, the description of the Buddhist path may initially have been as simple as the term *the Middle Way* . [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9) In time, this short description was elaborated, resulting in the description of the Eightfold Path. [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9) Tilmann Vetter and historian Rod Bucknell both note that longer descriptions of "the path" can be found in the early texts, which can be condensed into the Eightfold Path. [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9) [\[26\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBucknell1984-26) [\[note1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-29)

##### Tenfold path

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=4)]
In the *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* [\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BMaha-30) [\[30\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-31) which appears in the Chinese and Pali canons, the Buddha explains that cultivation of the noble eightfold path of a learner leads to the development of two further paths of the [Arahants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arhat), which are right knowledge, or insight ( *sammā-ñāṇa* ), and right liberation, or release ( *sammā-vimutti* ). [\[31\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChoong2000141-32) These two factors fall under the category of wisdom ( *paññā* ). [\[32\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFuller200555%E2%80%9356-33)

##### Short description of the eight divisions

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=5)]
The eight Buddhist practices in the Noble Eightfold Path are:
1. Right View: various summaries of "right view" can be found in the sutras. A stock phrase is the opening of the dhamma-eye, in which knowledge arises: "all that has the nature of arising has the nature of ending"; [\[33\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson2001132-34) [\[note2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-38) showing the futility of striving after worldly fullfilment. More extensive treatments state that our actions have consequences, death is not the end, and our actions and beliefs have consequences after death. The Buddha followed and taught a successful path out of this world and the other world (heaven and underworld/hell), [\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198812,_77%E2%80%9379-39) [\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVelez_de_Cea201354-40) [\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWei-hs%C3%BCn_FuWawrytko1994194-41) [\[web2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-vgweb.org-42) and his example is to be followed. Later on, right view came to explicitly include *karma* and rebirth, and the importance of the Four Noble Truths, when "insight" became central to Buddhist [soteriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soteriology), especially in Theravada Buddhism. [\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198877-43) [\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383%E2%80%9384-44)
1. Right Resolve ( *samyaka-saṃkalpa* / *sammā-saṅkappa* ) can also be known as "right thought", "right aspiration", or "right motivation". [\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Brahm-45) In this factor, one resolves to leave home, renounce the worldly life and follow the Buddhist path. [\[42\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198812%E2%80%9313-46) The practitioner resolves to strive toward non-violence ( *ahimsa* ) and avoid violent and hateful conduct. [\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383%E2%80%9384-44)
1. Right Speech: no lying, no abusive speech, no divisive speech, no idle chatter. [\[43\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-rightSpeechAccessToInsight-47) [\[44\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198812-13-48)
1. Right Conduct or Action: no killing or injuring, no taking what is not given, no sexual misconduct, no material desires.
1. Right Livelihood: no trading in weapons, living beings, meat, liquor, or poisons.
1. Right Effort: preventing the arising of [unwholesome states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Right_Efforts), and generating wholesome states, the *bojjhaṅgā* ( [Seven Factors of Awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Factors_of_Awakening)). This includes *indriya-samvara* , "guarding the sense-doors", restraint of the sense faculties, [\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Analayo2013-49) [\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383-50) akin to *pratyahara* .
1. Right Mindfulness ( *sati* ; *Satipatthana* ; *Sampajañña* ): a quality that guards or watches over the mind; [\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGethin200332-51) the stronger it becomes, the weaker unwholesome states of mind become, weakening their power "to take over and dominate thought, word and deed." [\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGethin200343-52) [\[note3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-54) In the [vipassana movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vipassana_movement), *sati* is interpreted as "bare attention": never be absent minded, being conscious of what one is doing; this encourages the awareness of the impermanence of body, feeling and mind, as well as to experience the five aggregates ( *skandhas* ), the [five hindrances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_hindrances), the four True Realities and [seven factors of awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_factors_of_awakening). [\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383-50)
1. Right *samadhi* ( *passaddhi* ; *ekaggata* ; *sampasadana* ): practicing four stages of *dhyāna* ("meditation"), which includes *samadhi* proper in the second stage, and reinforces the development of the *bojjhaṅgā* , culminating into *upekkhā* (equanimity) and mindfulness. [\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolak2011-55) In the Theravada tradition and the vipassana movement, this is interpreted as *ekaggata* , concentration or one-pointedness of the mind, and supplemented with *vipassana* meditation, which aims at insight.

##### Right view

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=6)]
See also: [View (Buddhism)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_(Buddhism))
The purpose of "right view" ( *samyak-dṛṣṭi* / *sammā-diṭṭhi* ) or "right understanding" [\[51\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGunaratana200111-56) is to clear one's path from confusion, misunderstanding, and deluded thinking. It is a means to gain right understanding of reality. [\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChryssidesWilkins2006249-57)

###### Sequences in the suttas

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=7)]
The Pali canon and the Agamas contain various "definitions" or descriptions of "right view." The *Mahasatipatthana Sutta* ( [Digha Nikaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digha_Nikaya) 22), compiled from elements from other suttas possibly as late as 20 BCE, [\[53\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTESujato2012304-58) defines right view summarily as the Four Noble Truths:
And what is right view? Knowing about suffering, the origin of suffering, the cessation of suffering, and the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering. This is called right view. [\[54\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-59)
In this, right view explicitly includes *karma* and [rebirth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebirth_(Buddhism)), and the importance of the [Four Noble Truths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths). This view of "right view" gained importance when "insight" became central to Buddhist soteriology, [\[39\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198877-43) and still plays an essential role in Theravada Buddhism. [\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383%E2%80%9384-44)
show *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta*
And what is right view? Right view is twofold, I say. There is right view that is accompanied by [defilements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asava), has the attributes of good deeds, and ripens in attachment. And there is right view that is noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path.
And what is right view that is accompanied by defilements, has the attributes of good deeds, and ripens in attachment? 'There is meaning in giving, sacrifice, [\[note4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-61) and offerings. There are fruits and results of good and bad deeds. There is an afterlife. There are such things as [serving] mother and father, and beings [devas] that are reborn spontaneously. And there are ascetics and Brahmins who are well attained and practiced, and who describe the afterlife after realizing it with their own insight.' This is right view that is accompanied by defilements, has the attributes of good deeds, and ripens in attachment.
And what is right view that is noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path? It's the wisdom—the faculty of wisdom, the power of wisdom, the awakening factor of investigation of principles [ *dhamma vicaya* ], and right view as a factor of the path—in one of noble mind and undefiled mind, who possesses the noble path and develops the noble path. This is called right view that is noble, undefiled, transcendent, a factor of the path.
They make an effort to give up wrong view and embrace right view: that's their right effort. Mindfully they give up wrong view and take up right view: that's their right mindfulness. So these three things keep running and circling around right view, namely: right view, right effort, and right mindfulness. [\[56\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-62) [\[note5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-graduated_talk-64)
Other suttas give a more extensive overview, stating that our actions have consequences, that death is not the end, that our actions and beliefs also have consequences after death, and that the Buddha followed and taught a successful path out of this world and [the other world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_cosmology) (heaven and underworld or hell). [\[36\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198812,_77%E2%80%9379-39) [\[37\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVelez_de_Cea201354-40) [\[38\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWei-hs%C3%BCn_FuWawrytko1994194-41) [\[web2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-vgweb.org-42) The *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* ("The Great Forty," [Majjhima Nikaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majjhima_Nikaya) 117) gives an extensive overview, describing the first seven practices as requisites of right *samadhi* c.q. *dhyana* . It makes a distinction between mundane right view ( *karma* , *rebirth* ) and noble right view as a path-factor, relating noble right view to *dhamma vicaya* ("investigation of principles), one of the *bojjhanga* , the "seven factors of awakening" which give an alternate account of right effort and *dhyana* . [\[58\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-65)
Alternatively, right view (together with right resolve) is expressed in the stock phrase of *dhammalsaddhalpabbajja* : "A layman hears a Buddha teach the Dhamma, comes to have faith in him, and decides to take ordination as a monk." [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9) [\[note6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-66)
show *Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta*
The venerable Sāriputta said to the venerable Mahākotthita: "Just ask, friend, knowing I shall answer." The venerable Mahākotthita said to the venerable Sāriputta: "Having accomplished what factors is a learned noble disciple in this teaching and discipline reckoned to be endowed with [right] view, to have accomplished straight view, to have accomplished unshakeable confidence in the Buddha, to have come to and arrived at the right teaching, to have attained this right Dharma and awoken to this right Dharma?"
The venerable Sāriputta said: "Venerable Mahākotthita, [this takes place if] a learned noble disciple understands unwholesome states as they really are, understands the roots of unwholesomeness as they really are, understands wholesome states as they really are and understands the roots of wholesomeness as they really are.
"How does [a learned noble disciple] understand unwholesome states as they really are? Unwholesome bodily actions, verbal actions and mental actions − these are reckoned unwholesome states. In this way unwholesome states are understood as they really are.
"How does [a learned noble disciple] understand the roots of unwholesomeness as they really are? There are three roots of unwholesomeness: greed is a root of unwholesomeness, hatred is a root of unwholesomeness, and delusion is a root of unwholesomeness − these are reckoned the roots of unwholesomeness. In this way the roots of unwholesomeness are understood as they really are.
"How does [a learned noble disciple] understand wholesome states as they really are? Wholesome bodily actions, verbal actions and mental actions − these are reckoned wholesome states. In this way wholesome states are understood as they really are.
"How does [a learned noble disciple] understand the roots of wholesomeness as they really are? That is, there are three roots of wholesomeness: non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion − these are reckoned the roots of wholesomeness. In this way the roots of wholesomeness are understood as they really are.
"Venerable Mahākotthita, [if] in this way a learned noble disciple understands unwholesome states as they really are, understands the roots of unwholesomeness as they really are, understands wholesome states as they really are and understands the roots of wholesomeness as they really are; then, for this reason, [a learned noble disciple] in this teaching and discipline is endowed with right view, has accomplished straight view, has accomplished unshakeable confidence in the Buddha, has come to and arrived at the right teaching, has attained this right Dharma and awoken to this right Dharma." [\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnalayo201113-14-67)
Likewise, the [Sammādiṭṭhi Sutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samm%C4%81di%E1%B9%AD%E1%B9%ADhi_Sutta) (Majjhima Nikaya 9), and its parallel in the *Samyukta-āgama* , refer to faith in the Buddha and understanding ( *dhamma vicaya* ) the path-factors of wholesome bodily actions, verbal actions and mental actions. [\[59\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnalayo201113-14-67)

###### Theravada

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=8)]
Right View can be further subdivided, states translator Bhikkhu Bodhi, into mundane right view and superior or supramundane right view: [\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BBodhi_NEP-68) [\[61\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFuller200556-69)
1. Mundane right view, knowledge of the fruits of good behavior ( *karma* ). Having this type of view will bring merit and will support the favourable rebirth of the sentient being in the realm of [samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsara_(Buddhism)).
1. Supramundane (world-transcending) right view, the understanding of the Four Noble Truths, leading to awakening and liberation from rebirths and associated [dukkha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dukkha) in the realms of samsara. [\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BBodhi_NEP-68) [\[62\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-70) [\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383%E2%80%9384-44) According to Bhikkhu Bodhi, this kind of right view comes at the end of the path, not at the beginning. [\[60\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BBodhi_NEP-68)
According to Theravada Buddhism, mundane right view is a teaching that is suitable for lay followers, while supramundane right view, which requires a deeper understanding, is suitable for monastics. [\[note5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-graduated_talk-64) Mundane and supramundane right view involve accepting the following doctrines of Buddhism: [\[63\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERichard_Gombrich200927%E2%80%9328,_103%E2%80%9309-71) [\[64\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeown200059,_96%E2%80%9397-72)
1. [Karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma_in_Buddhism): Every action of body, speech, and mind has [karmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma) results, and influences the kind of future rebirths and realms a being enters into.
1. [Three marks of existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_marks_of_existence): everything, whether physical or mental, is impermanent ( *anicca* ), a source of suffering ( *dukkha* ), and lacks a self ( *anatta* ).
1. The [Four Noble Truths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Noble_Truths) are a means to gaining insights and ending *dukkha* .

###### A-ditthi

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=9)]
Gombrich notes that there is a tension in the suttas between "right view" and 'no view', release by not clinging to any view at all. [\[65\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-73) According to Chryssides and Wilkins, "right view is ultimately non-view: though the Enlightened One sees things as they really are, 'he has a "critical awareness" of the impossibility of giving full and final expression to his conviction in fixed conceptual terms'. One therefore cannot cling to any particular formulation in a rigid and dogmatic manner." [\[52\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEChryssidesWilkins2006249-57)

##### Right resolve

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=10)]
Right Resolve ( *samyak-saṃkalpa* / *sammā-saṅkappa* ) can also be known as "right thought", "right aspiration", or "right motivation". [\[41\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Brahm-45) In section III.248, the Majjhima Nikaya states,
And what is right resolve? Being resolved on renunciation, on freedom from ill will, on harmlessness: This is called right resolve. [\[66\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Bsaccavi-74)
Like right view, this factor has two levels. At the mundane level, the resolve includes being harmless ( [ahimsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahimsa)) and refraining from ill will ( *avyapadha* ) to any being, as this accrues karma and leads to rebirth. [\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383%E2%80%9384-44) [\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-keownprebish333-75) At the supramundane level, the factor includes a resolve to consider everything and everyone as impermanent, a source of suffering and without a Self. [\[67\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-keownprebish333-75)

Right speech ( *samyag-vāc* / *sammā-vācā* ) in most Buddhist texts is presented as four abstentions, such as in the Pali Canon thus: [\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BMaha-30) [\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BSac-76)
And what is right speech? Abstaining from lying, from divisive speech, from abusive speech, and from idle chatter: This is called right speech.
Instead of the usual "abstention and refraining from wrong" terminology, a few texts such as the [Samaññaphala Sutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sama%C3%B1%C3%B1aphala_Sutta) and Kevata Sutta in *Digha Nikaya* explain this virtue in an active sense, after stating it in the form of an abstention. [\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Samannaphala-77) For example, Samaññaphala Sutta states that a part of a monk's virtue is that "he abstains from false speech. He speaks the truth, holds to the truth, is firm, reliable, no deceiver of the world." [\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Samannaphala-77) Similarly, the virtue of abstaining from divisive speech is explained as delighting in creating concord. [\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Samannaphala-77) The virtue of abstaining from abusive speech is explained in this Sutta to include affectionate and polite speech that is pleasing to people. The virtue of abstaining from idle chatter is explained as speaking what is connected with the Dhamma goal of his liberation. [\[69\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Samannaphala-77) [\[40\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383%E2%80%9384-44)
In the *Abhaya-raja-kumara Sutta* , the Buddha explains the virtue of right speech in different scenarios, based on its truth value, utility value and emotive content. [\[70\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKalupahana1992105-78) [\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-abhayasutta-79) The *Tathagata* , states Abhaya Sutta, never speaks anything that is unfactual or factual, untrue or true, disagreeable or agreeable, if that is unbeneficial and unconnected to his goals. [\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-abhayasutta-79) [\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKalupahana199250%E2%80%9352-80) Further, adds Abhaya Sutta, the *Tathagata* speaks the factual, the true, if in case it is disagreeable and unendearing, only if it is beneficial to his goals, but with a sense of proper time. [\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-abhayasutta-79) [\[73\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKalupahana199250-52-81) Additionally, adds Abhaya Sutta, the *Tathagata* , only speaks with a sense of proper time even when what he speaks is the factual, the true, the agreeable, the endearing and what is beneficial to his goals. [\[71\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-abhayasutta-79) [\[72\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKalupahana199250%E2%80%9352-80) [\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Ganeri2007p47-82)
The Buddha thus explains right speech in the Pali Canon, according to Ganeri, as never speaking something that is not beneficial; and, only speaking what is true and beneficial, "when the circumstances are right, whether they are welcome or not". [\[74\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Ganeri2007p47-82)

##### Right action

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=12)]
Right action ( *samyak-karmānta* / *sammā-kammanta* ) is like right speech, expressed as abstentions but in terms of bodily action. In the Pali Canon, this path factor is stated as:
And what is right action? Abstaining from killing, abstaining from stealing, abstaining from sexual misconduct. This is called right action. [\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-bucknellkangp12-83)
The prohibition on killing precept in Buddhist scriptures applies to all [living beings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentient_beings_(Buddhism)), states Christopher Gowans, not just [human beings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_beings_in_Buddhism). [\[76\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Gowans2004p177-84) Bhikkhu Bodhi agrees, clarifying that the more accurate rendering of the Pali canon is a prohibition on "taking life of any sentient being", which includes human beings, animals, birds, insects but excludes plants because they are not considered sentient beings. Further, adds Bodhi, this precept refers to *intentional* killing, as well as any form of intentional harming or torturing any sentient being. This moral virtue in early Buddhist texts, both in context of harm or killing of animals and human beings, is similar to *ahimsa* precepts found in the texts particularly of Jainism as well as of Hinduism, [\[77\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-85) [\[78\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-86) and has been a subject of significant debate in various Buddhist traditions.
The prohibition on stealing in the Pali Canon is an abstention from intentionally taking what is not voluntarily offered by the person to whom that property belongs. This includes taking by stealth, by force, by fraud or by deceit. Both the intention and the act matters, as this precept is grounded on the impact on one's karma.
The prohibition on sexual misconduct in the Noble Eightfold Path refers to "not performing sexual acts". [\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198812-87) This virtue is more generically explained in the *Cunda Kammaraputta Sutta* , which teaches that one must abstain from all sensual misconduct, including getting sexually involved with someone unmarried (anyone protected by parents or by guardians or by siblings), and someone married (protected by husband), and someone betrothed to another person, and female convicts or by *dhamma* . [\[80\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-88)
For monastics, the abstention from sensual misconduct means strict celibacy while for lay Buddhists this prohibits adultery as well as other forms of sensual misconduct. [\[81\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Emmanuel2015p440-89) [\[82\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-90) [\[83\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-91) Later Buddhist texts state that the prohibition on sexual conduct for lay Buddhists includes any sexual involvement with someone married, a girl or woman protected by her parents or relatives, and someone prohibited by *dhamma* conventions (such as relatives, nuns and others).

##### Right livelihood

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=13)]
Right livelihood ( *samyag-ājīva* / *sammā-ājīva* ) precept is mentioned in many early Buddhist texts, such as the *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* in *Majjhima Nikaya* as follows: [\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BMaha-30)
And what is right livelihood? Right livelihood, I tell you, is of two sorts: There is right livelihood with effluents, siding with merit, resulting in acquisitions; there is right livelihood that is noble, without effluents, transcendent, a factor of the path.
And what is the right livelihood with effluents, siding with merit, resulting in acquisitions? There is the case where a disciple of the noble ones abandons wrong livelihood and maintains his life with right livelihood. This is the right livelihood with effluents, siding with merit, resulting in acquisitions.
And what is the right livelihood that is noble, without effluents, transcendent, a factor of the path? The abstaining, desisting, abstinence, avoidance of wrong livelihood in one developing the noble path whose mind is noble, whose mind is without effluents, who is fully possessed of the noble path. (...)
The early canonical texts state right livelihood as avoiding and abstaining from wrong livelihood. This virtue is further explained in Buddhist texts, states Vetter, as "living from begging, but not accepting everything and not possessing more than is strictly necessary". [\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198812-87) For lay Buddhists, this precept requires that the livelihood avoid causing suffering to sentient beings by cheating them, or harming or killing them in any way. [\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383-50)
The Anguttara Nikaya III.208 asserts that the right livelihood does not trade in weapons, living beings, meat, alcoholic drink or poison. [\[46\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383-50) [\[84\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERahula200753-92) The same text, in section V.177, asserts that this applies to lay Buddhists. [\[85\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-93) This has meant, states Harvey, that raising and trading cattle livestock for slaughter is a breach of "right livelihood" precept in the Buddhist tradition, and Buddhist countries lack the mass slaughter houses found in Western countries. [\[86\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey2013273%E2%80%9374-94)

##### Right effort

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=14)]
See also: [Four Right Efforts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Right_Efforts), [Viriya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viriya), [dhamma vicaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhamma_vicaya), and [Examination of conscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Examination_of_conscience)
Right effort ( *samyag-vyāyāma* / *sammā-vāyāma* ) is preventing the arising of [unwholesome states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Right_Efforts), and the generation of [wholesome states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Right_Efforts). This includes *indriya-samvara* , "guarding the sense-doors", restraint of the sense faculties. [\[45\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Analayo2013-49) Right effort is presented in the Pali Canon, such as the *Sacca-vibhanga Sutta* , as follows: [\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BSac-76) [\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-bucknellkangp12-83)
And what is right effort?
Here the monk arouses his will, puts forth effort, generates energy, exerts his mind, and strives to prevent the arising of evil and unwholesome mental states that have not yet arisen.
He arouses his will... and strives to eliminate evil and unwholesome mental states that have already arisen. He arouses his will... and strives to generate wholesome mental states that have not yet arisen.
He arouses his will, puts forth effort, generates energy, exerts his mind, and strives to maintain wholesome mental states that have already arisen, to keep them free of delusion, to develop, increase, cultivate, and perfect them.
This is called right effort.
The unwholesome states ( *akusala* ) are described in the Buddhist texts are related to thoughts, emotions, intentions. These include the *pancanivarana* ( [five hindrances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_hindrances)), that is, sensual thoughts, doubts about the path, restlessness, drowsiness, and ill will of any kind. [\[79\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198812-87) Of these, the Buddhist traditions consider sensual thoughts and ill will needing more right effort. Sensual desire that must be eliminated by effort includes anything related to sights, sounds, smells, tastes and touch. This is to be done by restraint of the sense faculties ( *indriya-samvara* ). Ill will that must be eliminated by effort includes any form of aversion including hatred, anger, resentment towards anything or anyone.

##### Right mindfulness

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=15)]
Main article: [Mindfulness (Buddhism)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindfulness_(Buddhism))
See also: [Sampajañña](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampaja%C3%B1%C3%B1a), [Dhamma vicaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhamma_vicaya), [Satipatthana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satipatthana), [Anapanasati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anapanasati), and [Satipatthana Sutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satipatthana_Sutta)
While originally, in Yogic practice, *sati* may have meant to remember the meditation object, to cultivate a deeply absorbed, secluded state of mind, [\[87\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-95) in the oldest Buddhism it has the meaning of "retention", being mindful of the *dhammas* (both wholesome states of mind, and teachings and practices that remind of those wholesome states of mind) that are beneficial to the Buddhist path. [\[88\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTESharf2014942%E2%80%9343-96) According to Gethin, *sati* is a quality that guards or watches over the mind; [\[47\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGethin200332-51) the stronger it becomes, the weaker unwholesome states of mind become, weakening their power "to take over and dominate thought, word and deed." [\[48\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGethin200343-52) According to [Frauwallner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Frauwallner), mindfulness was a means to prevent the arising of craving, which resulted simply from contact between the senses and their objects. According to Frauwallner this may have been the Buddha's original idea. [\[49\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEWilliams200045-53) According to Trainor, mindfulness aids one not to crave and cling to any transitory state or thing, by complete and constant awareness of phenomena as impermanent, suffering and without self. [\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-trainor2004p74-97) Gethin refers to the *Milindapanha* , which states that *sati* brings to mind the *dhammas* and their beneficial or unbeneficial qualities, aiding the removal of unbeneficial dhammas and the strengthening of beneficial dhammas. [\[90\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGethin200337%E2%80%9338-98) Gethin further notes that *sati* makes one aware of the "full range and extent of *dhammas* ", that is, the relation between things, broadening one's view and understanding. [\[91\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGethin200339,_42-99)
The *Satipatthana Sutta* describes the contemplation of [four domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satipatthana), namely body, feelings, mind and phenomena. [\[note7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-100) The *Satipatthana Sutta* is regarded by the vipassana movement as the quintessential text on Buddhist meditation, taking cues from it on "bare attention" and the contemplation on the observed phenomena as *dukkha* , *anatta* and *anicca* . [\[92\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-101) [\[93\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThera2013-102) [\[note8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-104) [\[note9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-105) According to Grzegorz Polak, the four *upassanā* have been misunderstood by the developing Buddhist tradition, including Theravada, to refer to four different foundations. According to Polak, the four *upassanā* do not refer to four different foundations of which one should be aware, but are an alternate description of the *jhanas* , describing how the *samskharas* are tranquilized: [\[95\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolak2011153%E2%80%9356,_196%E2%80%9397-106)
\* the [six sense-bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayatana) which one needs to be aware of ( *kāyānupassanā* );
\* contemplation on [vedanās](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedan%C4%81), which arise with the contact between the senses and their objects ( *vedanānupassanā* );
\* the altered states of mind to which this practice leads ( *cittānupassanā* );
\* the development from the [five hindrances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_hindrances) to the [seven factors of enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_factors_of_enlightenment) ( *dhammānupassanā* ). [\[note10\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-107)
In the [vipassana movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vipassana_movement), mindfulness ( *samyak-smṛti* / *sammā-sati* ) is interpreted as "bare attention": never be absent minded, being conscious of what one is doing. [\[96\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTESharf2014941-108) [Rupert Gethin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Gethin) notes that the contemporary vipassana movement interprets the *Satipatthana Sutta* as "describing a pure form of insight ( *vipassanā* ) meditation" for which *samatha* (calm) and *dhyāna* are not necessary. Yet, in [pre-sectarian Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-sectarian_Buddhism), the establishment of mindfulness was placed before the practice of the *Dhyāna* , and associated with the abandonment of the [five hindrances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_hindrances) and the entry into the first *Dhyāna* . [\[28\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Gethin2008_p142-28) [\[note11\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-109)
The *dhyāna* -scheme describes mindfulness also as appearing in the third and fourth *dhyāna* , after initial concentration of the mind. [\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198813-110) [\[note12\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-111) Gombrich and Wynne note that, while the second *dhyāna* denotes a state of absorption, in the third and fourth *dhyāna* one comes out of this absorption, being mindfully aware of objects while being indifferent to them. [\[note13\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-112) According to Gombrich, "the later tradition has falsified the jhana by classifying them as the quintessence of the concentrated, calming kind of meditation, ignoring the other – and indeed higher – element".

##### Right samadhi (unification of mind)

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=16)]
Main article: [Dhyāna in Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhy%C4%81na_in_Buddhism)

###### *Samadhi*

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=17)]
*Samadhi* ( *samyak-samādhi* / *sammā-samādhi* ) is a common practice or goal in Indian religions. The term *samadhi* derives from the root sam-a-dha, which means "to collect" or "bring together". [\[98\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-113) It is often translated as "concentration" or "unification of mind". [\[99\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-114) In the early Buddhist texts, samadhi is also associated with the term " [samatha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samatha)" (calm abiding). [\[100\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-115)

###### Dhyana

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=18)]
Bronkhorst notes that neither the Four Noble Truths nor the Noble Eightfold Path discourse provide details of right *samadhi* . [\[101\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-116) Several *Suttas* , such as the following in *Saccavibhanga Sutta* , equate it with *dhyana* : [\[68\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BSac-76) [\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-bucknellkangp12-83)
And what is right concentration?
[i] Here, the monk, detached from sense-desires, detached from unwholesome states, enters and remains in the first *jhana* (level of concentration, Sanskrit: *dhyāna* ), in which there is applied and sustained thinking, together with joy and pleasure born of detachment;
[ii] And through the subsiding of applied and sustained thinking, with the gaining of inner stillness and oneness of mind, he enters and remains in the second *jhana* , which is without applied and sustained thinking, and in which there are joy and pleasure born of concentration;
[iii] And through the fading of joy, he remains equanimous, mindful and aware, and he experiences in his body the pleasure of which the Noble Ones say: "equanimous, mindful and dwelling in pleasure", and thus he enters and remains in the third *jhana* ;
[iv] And through the giving up of pleasure and pain, and through the previous disappearance of happiness and sadness, he enters and remains in the fourth *jhana* , which is without pleasure and pain, and in which there is pure equanimity and mindfulness.
This is called right concentration. [\[75\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-bucknellkangp12-83) [\[102\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Bronkhorst2009p16-117)
Bronkhorst has questioned the historicity and chronology of the description of the four *jhanas* . Bronkhorst states that this path may be similar to what the Buddha taught, but the details and the form of the description of the *jhanas* in particular, and possibly other factors, is likely the work of later scholasticism. [\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-bronkhorst2009p17-118) [\[104\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-119) Bronkhorst notes that description of the third *jhana* cannot have been formulated by the Buddha, since it includes the phrase "Noble Ones say", quoting earlier Buddhists, indicating it was formulated by later Buddhists. [\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-bronkhorst2009p17-118) It is likely that later Buddhist scholars incorporated this, then attributed the details and the path, particularly the insights at the time of liberation, to have been discovered by the Buddha. [\[103\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-bronkhorst2009p17-118)

###### Concentration

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=19)]
In the Theravada tradition, *samadhi* is interpreted as concentration on a meditation object. [Buddhagosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhagosa) defines samadhi as "the centering of consciousness and consciousness concomitants evenly and rightly on a single object...the state in virtue of which consciousness and its concomitants remain evenly and rightly on a single object, undistracted and unscattered." [\[105\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-120)
According to Henepola Gunaratana, in the suttas samadhi is defined as one-pointedness of mind ( *Cittass'ekaggatā* ). [\[106\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-121) According to [Bhikkhu Bodhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhikkhu_Bodhi), the right concentration factor is reaching a one-pointedness of mind and unifying all mental factors, but it is not the same as "a gourmet sitting down to a meal, or a soldier on the battlefield" who also experience one-pointed concentration. The difference is that the latter have a one-pointed object in focus with complete awareness directed to that object – the meal or the target, respectively. In contrast, right concentration meditative factor in Buddhism is a state of awareness without any object or subject, and ultimately unto no-thingness and emptiness, as articulated in apophatic discourse.

###### Development into equanimity

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=20)]
Although often translated as "concentration", as in the limiting of the attention of the mind on one object, in the fourth *dhyana* "equanimity and mindfulness remain", [\[107\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBronkhorst199363-122) and the practice of concentration-meditation may well have been incorporated from non-Buddhist traditions. [\[108\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBronkhorst199353%E2%80%9370-123) Vetter notes that *samadhi* consists of the [four stages of awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_stages_of_awakening), but
...to put it more accurately, the first dhyana seems to provide, after some time, a state of strong concentration, from which the other stages come forth; the second stage is called samadhija. [\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198813-110)
Gombrich and Wynne note that, while the second *jhana* denotes a state of absorption, in the third and fourth *jhana* one comes out of this absorption, being mindfully awareness of objects while being indifferent to it. [\[109\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-124) According to Gombrich, "the later tradition has falsified the jhana by classifying them as the quintessence of the concentrated, calming kind of meditation, ignoring the other – and indeed higher – element."

##### Liberation

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=21)]
Following the Noble Eightfold Path leads to [liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha) in the form of [nirvana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirvana): [\[110\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTELopez2009136-137-125) [\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-auto-7)
And what is that ancient path, that ancient road, traveled by the Rightly Self-awakened Ones of former times? Just this noble eightfold path: right view, right aspiration, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. That is the ancient path, the ancient road, traveled by the Rightly Self-awakened Ones of former times. I followed that path. Following it, I came to direct knowledge of aging & death, direct knowledge of the origination of aging & death, direct knowledge of the cessation of aging & death, direct knowledge of the path leading to the cessation of aging & death. I followed that path. Following it, I came to direct knowledge of birth... becoming... clinging... craving... feeling... contact... the six sense media... name-&-form... consciousness, direct knowledge of the origination of consciousness, direct knowledge of the cessation of consciousness, direct knowledge of the path leading to the cessation of consciousness. I followed that path.
— The Buddha, Nagara Sutta, *Samyutta Nikaya ii.124* , Translated by Thanissaro Bhikkhu [\[111\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-126) [\[112\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-127)

#### Practice

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=22)]

##### Order of practice

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=23)]
Vetter notes that originally the path culminated in the practice of *dhyana/samadhi* as the core soteriological practice. [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9) According to the Pali and Chinese canon, the *samadhi* state (right concentration) is dependent on the development of preceding path factors: [\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BMaha-30) [\[113\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-cbeta.org-128) [\[114\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Taish%C5%8D_Tripi%E1%B9%ADaka_Vol_p._814-129)
The Blessed One said: "Now what, monks, is noble right concentration with its supports and requisite conditions? Any singleness of mind equipped with these seven factors – right view, right resolve, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, and right mindfulness – is called noble right concentration with its supports and requisite conditions.
— Maha-cattarisaka Sutta
According to the discourses, right view, right resolve, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, and right mindfulness are used as the support and requisite conditions for the practice of right concentration. Understanding of the right view is the preliminary role, and is also the forerunner of the entire Noble Eightfold Path. [\[29\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-BMaha-30) [\[115\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Cbeta-130)
According to the modern Theravada [monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhikkhu) and scholar [Walpola Rahula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walpola_Rahula), the divisions of the noble eightfold path "are to be developed more or less simultaneously, as far as possible according to the capacity of each individual. They are all linked together and each helps the cultivation of the others." [\[116\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-131) Bhikkhu Bodhi explains that these factors are not sequential, but components, and "with a certain degree of progress all eight factors can be present simultaneously, each supporting the others. However, until that point is reached, some sequence in the unfolding of the path is inevitable." [\[117\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-accesstoinsight.org-132)
The stage in the Path where there is no more learning in [Yogachara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogachara) Abhidharma, state Buswell and Gimello, is identical to *Nirvana* or *Buddhahood* , the ultimate goal in Buddhism. [\[118\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswellGimello1994204-133) [\[119\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-134)

##### Sila-samadhi-prajna

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=24)]
The Noble Eightfold Path is sometimes divided into [three basic divisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_disciplines_of_Buddhism), with right view and right resolve concluding the sequence: [\[120\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPrebish200040-135)

| Division | Eightfold Path factors |
| --- | --- |
| Moral virtue [\[121\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383-84-136) (Sanskrit: *śīla* , Pāli: *sīla* ) | 1. [Right speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_speech) |
| ^^ | 2. [Right action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_action) |
| ^^ | 3. [Right livelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_livelihood) |
| Meditation [\[121\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383-84-136) (Sanskrit and Pāli: *samādhi* ) | 4. [Right effort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_effort) |
| ^^ | 5. [Right mindfulness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_mindfulness) |
| ^^ | 6. [Right concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_concentration) |
| Insight, wisdom (Sanskrit: *prajñā* , Pāli: *paññā* ) | 7. [Right view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_view) |
| ^^ | 8. [Right resolve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#Right_resolve) |

This order is a later development, when discriminating insight ( *prajna* ) became central to Buddhist soteriology, and came to be regarded as the culmination of the Buddhist path. [\[97\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter198813-110) Yet, Majjhima Nikaya 117, *Mahācattārīsaka Sutta* , describes the first seven practices as requisites for right samadhi. According to Vetter, this may have been the original soteriological practice in early Buddhism. [\[9\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVetter1988-9)
The " [moral virtues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_ethics)" (Sanskrit: *śīla* , Pāli: *sīla* ) group consists of three paths: right speech, right action and right livelihood. [\[121\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHarvey201383-84-136) The word *sīla* , though translated by English writers as linked to "morals or ethics", states Bhikkhu Bodhi, is in ancient and medieval Buddhist commentary tradition closer to the concept of discipline and disposition that "leads to harmony at several levels – social, psychological, karmic and contemplative". Such harmony creates an environment to pursue the meditative steps in the Noble Eightfold Path by reducing social disorder, preventing inner conflict that result from transgressions, favoring future karma-triggered movement through better rebirths, and purifying the mind. [\[122\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTESpiro198244-48-137)
The meditation group ("samadhi") of the path progresses from moral restraints to training the mind. [\[123\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTESpiro198244-53-138) Right effort and mindfulness calm the mind-body complex, releasing unwholesome states and habitual patterns and encouraging the development of wholesome states and non-automatic responses, the *bojjhaṅga* (seven factors of awakening). The practice of *dhyāna* reinforces these developments, leading to *upekkhā* (equanimity) and mindfulness. [\[50\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPolak2011-55) According to the Theravada commentarial tradition and the contemporary vipassana movement, the goal in this group of the Noble Eightfold Path is to develop clarity and insight into the nature of reality – *dukkha* , *anicca* and *anatta* , discard negative states and dispel *avidya* (ignorance), ultimately attaining *nirvana* . [\[89\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-trainor2004p74-97)
In the threefold division, *prajna* (insight, wisdom) is presented as the culmination of the path, whereas in the eightfold division the path starts with correct knowledge or insight, which is needed to understand why this path should be followed. [\[124\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAnderson2013-139)

#### Schools of Buddhism and their views of the Eightfold Path

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=25)]

##### Theravada presentations of the path

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=26)]
Theravada Buddhism is a diverse tradition and thus includes different explanations of the path to awakening. However, the teachings of the Buddha are often encapsulated by Theravadins in the basic framework of the Four Noble Truths and the Eighthfold Path. [\[125\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-140) [\[126\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-141)
Some Theravada Buddhists also follow the presentation of the path laid out in [Buddhaghosa's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhaghosa) [Visuddhimagga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visuddhimagga). This presentation is known as the "Seven Purifications" ( *satta-visuddhi* ). [\[127\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-142) This schema and its accompanying outline of "insight knowledges" ( *vipassanā-ñāṇa* ) is used by modern influential Theravadin scholars, such [Mahasi Sayadaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahasi_Sayadaw) (in his "The Progress of Insight") and [Nyanatiloka Thera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyanatiloka) (in "The Buddha's Path to Deliverance"). [\[128\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-143) [\[129\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-144)

##### Mahayana presentations of the path

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=27)]
[Mahāyāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana) Buddhism is based principally upon the path of a [Bodhisattva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodhisattva). [\[130\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTENattier2003137%E2%80%93138,_142%E2%80%93146-145) A *Bodhisattva* refers to one who is on the path to buddhahood. [\[131\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGyatso19951-146) The term *Mahāyāna* was originally a synonym for *Bodhisattvayāna* or "Bodhisattva Vehicle". [\[132\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTENattier2003174-147) [\[133\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHirakawa1993297-148) [\[134\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEConze20012001-149)
In the earliest texts of Mahāyāna Buddhism, the path of a bodhisattva was to awaken the *bodhicitta* . [\[135\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinsonJohnson199799-150) Between the 1st and 3rd century CE, this tradition introduced the *Ten Bhumi* doctrine, which means ten levels or stages of awakening. [\[135\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinsonJohnson199799-150) This development was followed by the acceptance that it is impossible to achieve Buddhahood in one (current) lifetime, and the best goal is not nirvana for oneself, but Buddhahood after climbing through the ten levels during multiple rebirths. [\[136\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTENattier2003142%E2%80%93152-151) Mahāyāna scholars then outlined an elaborate path, for monks and laypeople, and the path includes the vow to help teach Buddhist knowledge to other beings, so as to help them cross samsara and liberate themselves, once one reaches the Buddhahood in a future rebirth. [\[130\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTENattier2003137%E2%80%93138,_142%E2%80%93146-145) One part of this path are the *pāramitā* (perfections, to cross over), derived from the *Jatakas* tales of Buddha's numerous rebirths. [\[137\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinsonJohnson1997101%E2%80%93102-152) [\[138\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswell2004631%E2%80%93632-153)
The doctrine of the bodhisattva bhūmis was also eventually merged with the [Sarvāstivāda Vaibhāṣika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaibh%C4%81%E1%B9%A3ika) schema of the "five paths" by the [Yogacara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogachara) school *. [139]* This Mahāyāna "five paths" presentation can be seen in [Asanga's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asanga) *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* . [\[139\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-:02-154)
The Mahāyāna texts are inconsistent in their discussion of the *pāramitās* , and some texts include lists of two, others four, six, ten and fifty-two. [\[140\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTENattier2003151%E2%80%93154-155) [\[141\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeown2003212-156) [\[142\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Ichimura2001p28-157) The six paramitas have been most studied, and these are: [\[137\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinsonJohnson1997101%E2%80%93102-152) [\[142\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Ichimura2001p28-157) [\[143\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGyatso19954%E2%80%9312-158)
1. *Dāna pāramitā* : perfection of giving; primarily to monks, nuns and the Buddhist monastic establishment dependent on the alms and gifts of the lay householders, in return for generating religious merit; [\[144\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswell2004196-159) some texts recommend ritually transferring the merit so accumulated for better rebirth to someone else
1. *Śīla pāramitā* : perfection of morality; it outlines ethical behaviour for both the laity and the Mahayana monastic community; this list is similar to Śīla in the Eightfold Path (i.e. Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood) [\[145\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-MizunoSekimori1996p28-160)
1. *Kṣānti pāramitā* : perfection of patience, willingness to endure hardship
1. *Vīrya pāramitā* : perfection of vigour; this is similar to Right Effort in the Eightfold Path [\[145\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-MizunoSekimori1996p28-160)
1. *Dhyāna pāramitā* : perfection of meditation; this is similar to Right Concentration in the Eightfold Path
1. *Prajñā pāramitā* : perfection of insight (wisdom), awakening to the characteristics of existence such as karma, rebirths, impermanence, no-self, dependent origination and emptiness; [\[142\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-Ichimura2001p28-157) [\[146\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEBuswell2004631%E2%80%93632,_664%E2%80%93665,_809-161) this is complete acceptance of the Buddha teaching, then conviction, followed by ultimate realisation that "dharmas are non-arising". [\[137\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTERobinsonJohnson1997101%E2%80%93102-152)
In Mahāyāna Sutras that include ten *pāramitā* , the additional four perfections are "skillful means, vow, power and knowledge". [\[141\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeown2003212-156) The most discussed *pāramitā* and the highest rated perfection in Mahayana texts is the "Prajna-paramita", or the "perfection of insight". [\[141\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKeown2003212-156) This insight in the Mahāyāna tradition, states Shōhei Ichimura, has been the "insight of non-duality or the absence of reality in all things". [\[147\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-162) [\[148\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-163)

###### East Asian Buddhism

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=28)]
[East Asian Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism) is influenced by both the classic Indian Buddhist presentations of the path such as the Eightfold Path as well as classic Indian Mahāyāna presentations such as that found in the [Da zhidu lun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da_zhidu_lun). [\[149\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-164)
There are many different presentations of [soteriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soteriology), including numerous paths and vehicles ( *yanas* ) in the different traditions of East Asian Buddhism. [\[150\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-165) There is no single dominant presentation. In [Zen Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen_Buddhism) for example, one can find outlines of the path such as the [Two Entrances and Four Practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Scroll_of_the_Treatise_on_the_Two_Entrances_and_Four_Practices) *,* The [Five ranks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Ranks), The [Ten Ox-Herding Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Bulls) and [The Three mysterious Gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linji_Yixuan#Expressing_the_inexpressible) of Linji.

###### Indo-Tibetan Buddhism

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=29)]
In Indo-Tibetan Buddhism, the path to liberation is outlined in the genre known as [Lamrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamrim) ("Stages of the Path"). All the various Tibetan schools have their own Lamrim presentations. This genre can be traced to [Atiśa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atisha)'s 11th-century *A Lamp for the Path to Enlightenment* ( *Bodhipathapradīpa* ). [\[151\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-thubten-166)

#### Cognitive psychology

[ [edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Noble_Eightfold_Path&action=edit&section=30)]
The noble eightfold path has been compared to [cognitive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychology); Gil Fronsdal says the right view factor can be interpreted to mean how one's mind views the world, and how that leads to patterns of thought, intention and actions. [\[152\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-167) Peter Randall states that it is the seventh factor or right mindfulness that may be thought in terms of cognitive psychology, wherein the change in thought and behavior are linked. [\[153\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path#cite_note-168)

1. **^** Note how *kāyānupassanā* , *vedanānupassanā* , and *cittānupassanā* , resemble the [five skandhas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skandha) and the chain of causation as described in the middle part of [Pratītyasamutpāda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prat%C4%ABtyasamutp%C4%81da); while *dhammānupassanā* refers to mindfulness as retention, calling into mind the beneficial *dhammas* which are applied to analyse phenomena, and counter the arising of disturbing thoughts and emotions.
