Ethics provides a set of standards that helps determine how people ought to act and guides us in making choices [1, 2]. The field is traditionally divided into three areas: meta-ethics (which studies the nature of ethical claims), normative ethics (the principles used to determine what is right or wrong), and applied ethics (the application of those principles to specific, real-world situations) [3, 4]. Within normative ethics, three primary frameworks exist [5, 6]:

* **Consequentialism:** Most notably represented by utilitarianism, this theory judges the morality of an action based on its outcomes, aiming to produce the greatest balance of good over harm for the greatest number of people [5, 7, 8].
* **Deontology (Duty-Based Ethics):** Championed by Immanuel Kant, this approach argues that moral actions are those taken out of duty and adherence to universal rules, completely independent of their consequences [9-11].
* **Virtue Ethics:** This approach shifts the focus away from specific rules or consequences, concentrating instead on the overall moral character and intentions of the individual acting [12, 13].

Virtue ethics originated with ancient philosophers like Plato and Aristotle, who argued that virtue is an acquired state of character developed through habit, repetition, and practice [14, 15]. A central idea of this tradition is Aristotle's "doctrine of the mean," which describes virtue as a balanced middle ground between the extremes of excess and deficiency [16, 17]. **Modern virtue ethics relies on three foundational Greek concepts: *arête* (excellence), *phronesis* (practical wisdom), and *eudaimonia* (human flourishing or true happiness)** [18]. While modern translations sometimes view "virtue" passively as rule-following or restraint, the original Greek concept of *arete* actually emphasized active striving and the full realization of one's potential [19, 20]. Furthermore, historical and cross-cultural surveys identify six core virtues that are ubiquitously valued: wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, and transcendence [21, 22]. What distinguishes these character strengths from mere natural talents or abilities is that exercising them requires willful choice and effort [23].

Truth is fundamentally defined as complete accuracy and conformity to facts or reality [24-26]. The sources draw sharp distinctions between truth, validity, and honesty. **While truth requires an accurate representation of objective reality, honesty simply requires expressing one's subjective beliefs sincerely** [27]. Because of this, a person can be completely honest while stating something that is factually untrue [27]. Validity, on the other hand, merely measures the internal consistency of an argument, meaning an argument can be logically valid without actually being true [24].

Philosophers propose several major theories to explain the fundamental nature of truth:

* **The Correspondence Theory:** Asserts that a belief or proposition is true if it perfectly mirrors, matches, or corresponds to an objective fact in reality [28-30].
* **The Coherence Theory:** Suggests that truth relies on a proposition's consistency with an entire body or system of knowledge; a belief is true if it coheres logically with other established beliefs [31-33].
* **The Pragmatic Theory:** Proposes that the truth of a belief is determined by its practical consequences, utility, and its ability to be verified through experience—essentially, truth is "what works" [34-36].
* **The Deflationary Theory:** Argues that truth has no substantial metaphysical nature. To say that a statement is "true" adds nothing new to its meaning and simply reasserts the statement itself [37, 38].

Other perspectives challenge the strict objectivity of truth. For instance, Friedrich Nietzsche viewed truth as "a mobile army of metaphors" and illusions whose human origins have been forgotten [39]. In our modern context, we are experiencing a "post-truth" era where the influence of objective facts is declining, and public opinion is increasingly shaped by subjective interpretations, emotions, and personal beliefs rather than factual accuracy [40-42].
