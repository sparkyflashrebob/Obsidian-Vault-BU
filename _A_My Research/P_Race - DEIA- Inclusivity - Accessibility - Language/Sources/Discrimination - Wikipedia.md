  **Discrimination** is the process of making unfair or prejudicial [distinctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinction_(sociology)) between people based on the groups, classes, or other categories to which they belong or are perceived to belong,[ 1 ] such as [race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_discrimination), [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexism), [age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ageism), [class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_discrimination), [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_discrimination), or [sexual orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_orientation_discrimination).[ 2 ] Discrimination typically leads to groups being unfairly treated on the basis of perceived statuses based on ethnic, racial, gender or religious categories.[ 2 ][ 3 ] It involves depriving members of one group of opportunities or privileges that are available to members of another group.[ 4 ]
  Discriminatory traditions, policies, ideas, practices and laws exist in many countries and [institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution) in all parts of the world, including some, where such discrimination is generally decried. In some places, countervailing measures such as quotas have been used to redress the balance in favor of those who are believed to be current or past victims of discrimination. These attempts have often been met with controversy, and sometimes been called [reverse discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_discrimination).

#### Etymology

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=1)]
The term *discriminate* appeared in the early 17th century in the English language. It is from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language) *discriminat-* 'distinguished between', from the verb *discriminare* , from *discrimen* 'distinction', from the verb *discernere* (corresponding to "to discern").[ 5 ] Since the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) the term "discrimination" generally evolved in [American English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English) usage as an understanding of prejudicial treatment of an individual based solely on their race, later generalized as membership in a certain socially undesirable [group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_group) or social category.[ 6 ] Before this sense of the word became almost universal, it was a synonym for discernment, tact and culture as in "taste and discrimination", generally a laudable attribute; to "discriminate against" being commonly disparaged.[ 7 ][ 8 ][page needed]

#### Definitions

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=2)]
Moral philosophers[who?] have defined[when?] discrimination using a [moralized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality) definition. Under this approach, discrimination is defined as acts, practices, or policies that wrongfully impose a relative disadvantage or deprivation on persons based on their membership in a salient social group.[ 9 ] This is a comparative definition. An individual need not be actually harmed in order to be discriminated against. They just needs to be treated *worse* than others for some arbitrary reason. If someone decides to donate to help orphan children, but decides to donate less, say, to children of a particular race out of a racist attitude, they will be acting in a discriminatory way even if they actually benefit the people they discriminate against by donating some money to them.[ 10 ] Discrimination also develops into a source of [oppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oppression), the action of recognizing someone as 'different' so much that they are treated inhumanly and degraded.[ 11 ]
This moralized definition of discrimination is distinct from a non-moralized definition—in the former, discrimination is wrong by definition, whereas in the latter, this is not the case.[ 12 ]
The [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations) stance on discrimination includes the statement: "Discriminatory behaviors take many forms, but they all involve some form of exclusion or rejection."[ 13 ] The [United Nations Human Rights Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Human_Rights_Council) and other international bodies work towards helping ending discrimination around the world.

#### Types

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=3)]

##### Age

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=4)] Main articles: [Ageism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ageism) and [Adultism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adultism)[Ageism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adultism) or age discrimination is discrimination and stereotyping based on the grounds of someone's age.[ 14 ] It is a set of beliefs, norms, and values which used to justify discrimination or subordination based on a person's age.[ 15 ] Ageism is most often directed toward elderly people, or adolescents and children.[ 16 ][ 17 ]
Age discrimination in hiring has been shown to exist in the United States. Joanna Lahey, professor at The [Bush School of Government and Public Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bush_School_of_Government_and_Public_Service) at [Texas A&M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_A%26M), found that firms are more than 40% more likely to interview a young adult job applicant than an older job applicant.[ 18 ] In Europe, Stijn Baert, Jennifer Norga, Yannick Thuy and Marieke Van Hecke, researchers at [Ghent University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghent_University), measured comparable ratios in Belgium. They found that age discrimination is heterogeneous by the activity older candidates undertook during their additional post-educational years. In Belgium, they are only discriminated if they have more years of inactivity or irrelevant employment.[ 19 ]
In a survey for the [University of Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kent), England, 29% of respondents stated that they had suffered from age discrimination. This is a higher proportion than for [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexism) or [racial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism) discrimination. [Dominic Abrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominic_Abrams), social psychology professor at the university, concluded that ageism is the most pervasive form of [prejudice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prejudice) experienced in the UK population.[ 20 ]

##### Caste

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=5)] See also: [Caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste)According to [UNICEF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNICEF) and [Human Rights Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Rights_Watch), [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste) discrimination affects an estimated 250 million people worldwide and is mainly prevalent in parts of Asia (India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nepal, Japan) and Africa.[ 21 ][ 22 ] As of 2011[update], there were 200 million [Dalits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalit) or [Scheduled Castes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_Castes) (formerly known as "untouchables") in India.[ 23 ]

##### Class

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=6)] These paragraphs are an excerpt from [Class discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_discrimination). [[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Class_discrimination&action=edit)]
[Class discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Class_discrimination&action=edit), also known as classism, is [prejudice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prejudice) or discrimination on the basis of [social class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_class). It includes individual attitudes, behaviors, systems of policies and practices that are set up to benefit the [upper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_class) at the expense of the [lower class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_class).[ 24 ]
Social class refers to the grouping of individuals in a hierarchy based on wealth, income, education, occupation, and social network.

##### Disability

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=7)] Main article: [Disability discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disability_discrimination)Discrimination against people with [disabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disability) in favor of people who are not is called [ableism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ableism) or [disablism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disablism). Disability discrimination, which treats non-disabled individuals as the standard of 'normal living', results in public and private places and services, educational settings, and social services that are built to serve 'standard' people, thereby excluding those with various disabilities. Studies have shown that disabled people not only need employment in order to be provided with the opportunity to earn a living but they also need employment in order to sustain their mental health and well-being. Work fulfils a number of basic needs for an individual such as collective purpose, social contact, status, and activity.[ 25 ] A person with a disability is often found to be socially isolated and work is one way to reduce his or her isolation. In the United States, the [Americans with Disabilities Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans_with_Disabilities_Act) mandates the provision of equality of access to both buildings and services and is paralleled by similar acts in other countries, such as the [Equality Act 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_Act_2010) in the UK.

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination_of_excellence&action=edit)] [Discrimination of excellence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_of_excellence)[\[ 26 \]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_of_excellence) is the violation of formal [equality of opportunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_of_opportunity)[\[ 27 \]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_of_opportunity) and [meritocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meritocracy),[ 28 ] which reward merits of individuals and [overachievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overachievement).[ 26 ] Discrimination of excellence can be caused by different reasons, including [legacy preferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_preferences),[ 29 ]nepotism, [substantive equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substantive_equality),[ 27 ] affirmative action[ 30 ] or random [luck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luck).[ 31 ]Language

This section is an excerpt from [Linguistic discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_discrimination). [[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Linguistic_discrimination&action=edit)]
[Linguistic discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Linguistic_discrimination&action=edit) (also called glottophobia, linguicism and languagism) is the unfair treatment of people based upon [their use of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usage_(language)) and the characteristics of their speech, such as their [first language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_language), their [accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accent_(sociolinguistics)), the perceived size of their [vocabulary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocabulary) (whether or not the speaker uses complex and varied words), their [modality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modality_(semiotics)), and their [syntax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax).[ 32 ] For example, an [Occitan speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language) in France will probably be treated differently from a [French speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language).[ 33 ]
Based on a difference in use of language, a person may automatically form judgments about another person's [wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wealth), [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education), [social status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_status), character or other traits, which may lead to discrimination. This has led to [public debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_debate) surrounding [localisation theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_assimilation#Willingness_to_assimilate_and_cultural_shock), likewise with overall [diversity prevalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiculturalism#Prevalence) in numerous nations across [the West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world).
Linguistic discrimination was at first considered an act of [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism). In the mid-1980s, [linguist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguist)[Tove Skutnabb-Kangas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguist) captured the idea of language-based discrimination as [linguicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguicism), which was defined as "ideologies and structures used to legitimize, effectuate, and reproduce unequal divisions of power and resources (both material and non-material) between groups which are defined on the basis of language".[ 34 ] Although different names have been given to this form of discrimination, they all hold the same definition. Linguistic discrimination is culturally and socially determined due to preference for one use of language over others.
Scholars have analyzed the role of [linguistic imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_imperialism) in linguicism, with some asserting that speakers of dominant languages gravitate toward discrimination against speakers of other, less dominant languages, while disadvantaging themselves linguistically by remaining monolingual.[ 35 ]
According to Carolyn McKinley, this phenomenon is most present in [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa), where the majority of the population speaks [European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_Europe) introduced during the [colonial era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonisation_of_Africa); African states are also noted as instituting European languages as the main medium of instruction, instead of [indigenous languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_language).[ 35 ]UNESCO reports have noted that this has historically benefitted only the African [upper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_class), conversely disadvantaging the majority of Africa's population who hold varying level of fluency in the European languages spoken across the continent.[ 35 ]

##### Scholars have also noted the influence of the linguistic dominance of English on [academic disciplines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_disciplines); [Anna Wierzbicka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Wierzbicka), professor of linguistics at the [Australian National University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_National_University), has described disciplines such as the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences) and [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities) as being "locked in a conceptual framework grounded in English", preventing [academia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academia) as a whole from reaching a "more universal, culture-independent perspective."[ 36 ]Name

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=10)]
Discrimination based on a person's name may also occur, with researchers suggesting that this form of discrimination is present based on a name's meaning, its pronunciation, its uniqueness, its gender affiliation, and its racial affiliation.[ 37 ][ 38 ][ 39 ][ 40 ][ 41 ] Research has further shown that real world recruiters spend an average of just six seconds reviewing each résumé before making their initial "fit/no fit" screen-out decision and that a person's name is one of the six things they focus on most.[ 42 ] France has made it illegal to view a person's name on a résumé when screening for the initial list of most qualified candidates. Great Britain, Germany, Sweden, and the Netherlands have also experimented with name-blind summary processes.[ 43 ] Some apparent discrimination may be explained by other factors such as name frequency.[ 44 ] The effects of name discrimination based on a name's fluency is subtle, small and subject to significantly changing norms.[ 45 ]

##### Nationality

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=11)] Further information: [Discrimination based on nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_based_on_nationality)The Anti-discrimination laws of most countries allow and make exceptions for discrimination based on nationality and immigration status.[ 46 ] The [International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Convention_on_the_Elimination_of_All_Forms_of_Racial_Discrimination) (CERD) does not prohibit discrimination by nationality, citizenship or naturalization but forbids discrimination "against any particular nationality".[ 47 ]
Discrimination on the basis of [nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationality) is usually included in [employment laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_discrimination)[\[ 48 \]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employment_discrimination) (see above [section for employment discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org#Employment) specifically). It is sometimes referred to as bound together with racial discrimination[ 49 ] although it can be separate. It may vary from laws that stop refusals of hiring based on nationality, asking questions regarding origin, to prohibitions of firing, forced retirement, compensation and pay, etc., based on nationality.
Discrimination on the basis of nationality may show as a "level of acceptance" in a sport or work team regarding new team members and employees who differ from the nationality of the majority of team members.[ 50 ]
In the [GCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperation_Council_for_the_Arab_States_of_the_Gulf) states, in the workplace, preferential treatment is given to full citizens, even though many of them lack experience or motivation to do the job. State benefits are also generally available for citizens only.[ 51 ] Westerners might also get paid more than other expatriates.[ 52 ]

##### Race or ethnicity

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=12)] Main articles: [Racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism), [Racial discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_discrimination), [Discrimination based on skin color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_based_on_skin_color), [Ethnic penalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_penalty), and [Racial segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation)[Anti-Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation) sign in [Pattaya Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattaya_Beach), [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand)German warning in [German-occupied Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Poland_(1939%E2%80%931945)) 1939 – "[No entrance for Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nur_f%C3%BCr_Deutsche)!" [Antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:37431682_eb5832e644.jpg) graffiti in [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuania). The words read *Juden raus* (German for *Jews out* ) and *Hasse* (presumably a misspelling of *Hass* , German for *hate* ). Racial and ethnic discrimination differentiates individuals on the basis of real and perceived racial and ethnic differences and leads to various forms of the [ethnic penalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_penalty).[ 53 ][ 54 ] It can also refer to the belief that groups of humans possess different behavioral traits corresponding to physical appearance and can be divided based on the [superiority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremacism) of one [race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_(human_categorization)) over another.[ 55 ][ 56 ][ 57 ][ 58 ] It may also mean [prejudice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prejudice), discrimination, or antagonism directed against other people because they are of a different race or [ethnicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnicity).[ 56 ][ 57 ]
Modern variants of [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism) are often based in social perceptions of biological differences between peoples. These views can take the form of [social actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_actions), practices or beliefs, or [political systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_systems) in which different races are ranked as inherently superior or inferior to each other, based on presumed shared inheritable traits, abilities, or qualities.[ 56 ][ 57 ][ 59 ] It has been official government policy in several countries, such as [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa) during the [apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid) era. Discriminatory policies towards ethnic minorities include the race-based discrimination against ethnic [Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysian_Indian) and Chinese in Malaysia[ 60 ] The concept of multiracism has been used to explain the varieties of race discrimination.[ 61 ] After the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War), many [Vietnamese refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_boat_people) moved to Australia and the United States, where they faced discrimination.[ 62 ]

##### Region

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=13)] See also: [Regional discrimination in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_discrimination_in_China)Regional or geographic discrimination is a form of discrimination that is based on the region in which a person lives or the region in which a person was born. It differs from national discrimination because it may not be based on national borders or the country in which the victim lives, instead, it is based on prejudices against a specific region of one or more countries. Examples include discrimination against Chinese people who were born in regions of the countryside that are far away from cities that are located within China, and discrimination against [Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans) who are from the [southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_United_States) or [northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_United_States) regions of the United States. It is often accompanied by discrimination that is based on accent, dialect, or cultural differences.[ 63 ]

  Religious discrimination is valuing or treating people or groups differently because of what they do or do not believe in or because of their feelings towards a given [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion). For instance, the Jewish population of Germany, and indeed a large portion of Europe, was subjected to discrimination under [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) and his Nazi party between 1933 and 1945. They were forced to live in ghettos, wear an identifying star of David on their clothes, and sent to concentration and death camps in rural Germany and Poland, where they were to be tortured and killed, all because of their Jewish religion. Many laws (most prominently the Nuremberg Laws of 1935) separated those of Jewish faith as supposedly inferior to the Christian population.
  Restrictions on the types of occupations that [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews) people could hold were imposed by Christian authorities. Local rulers and church officials closed many professions to religious Jews, pushing them into marginal roles that were considered socially inferior, such as tax and rent collecting and [moneylending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moneylending), occupations that were only tolerated as a "[necessary evil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_evil)".[ 64 ] The number of Jews who were permitted to reside in different places was limited; they were concentrated in [ghettos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghetto) and banned from owning land. In Saudi Arabia, non-[Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) are not allowed to publicly practice their religions and they cannot enter [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca) and [Medina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medina).[ 65 ][ 66 ] Furthermore, private non-Muslim religious gatherings might be raided by the [religious police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_police).[ 66 ] In Maldives, non-Muslims living and visiting the country are prohibited from openly expressing their religious beliefs, holding public congregations to conduct religious activities, or involving Maldivians in such activities. Those expressing religious beliefs other than Islam may face imprisonment of up to five years or house arrest, fines ranging from 5,000 to 20,000 rufiyaa ($320 to $1,300), and deportation.[ 67 ]
  In a 1979 consultation on the issue, the United States commission on civil rights defined religious discrimination in relation to the [civil rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_and_political_rights) which are guaranteed by the [Fourteenth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourteenth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution). Whereas religious civil liberties, such as the right to hold or not to hold a religious belief, are essential for [Freedom of Religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_Religion) (in the United States as secured by the [First Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution)), religious discrimination occurs when someone is denied "equal protection under the law, equality of status under the law, equal treatment in the administration of justice, and equality of opportunity and access to employment, education, housing, public services and facilities, and public accommodation because of their exercise of their right to religious freedom".[ 68 ]

##### Sex, sex characteristics, gender, and gender identity

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=15)] Main article: [Sexism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexism)See also: [Homophobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophobia), [Misogyny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misogyny), [Misandry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misandry), [Discrimination against intersex people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_against_intersex_people), [Transphobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transphobia), [Discrimination against non-binary gender persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_against_non-binary_gender_persons), and [Violence against LGBT people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violence_against_LGBT_people)[Sexism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violence_against_LGBT_people) is a form of discrimination based on a person's sex or gender. It has been linked to [stereotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype) and [gender roles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_role),[ 69 ][ 70 ] and may include the belief that one sex or gender is intrinsically superior to another.[ 71 ] Extreme sexism may foster [sexual harassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_harassment), [rape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rape), and other forms of [sexual violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_violence).[ 72 ] Gender discrimination may encompass sexism and is discrimination toward people based on their [gender identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_identity)[\[ 73 \]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_identity) or their gender or sex differences.[ 74 ] Gender discrimination is especially defined in terms of [workplace inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workplace_inequality).[ 74 ] It may arise from social or cultural customs and norms.[ 75 ]
Intersex persons experience [discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination_against_intersex_people) due to innate, atypical [sex characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_characteristics). Multiple jurisdictions now protect individuals on grounds of *intersex status* or sex characteristics. South Africa was the first country to explicitly add intersex to legislation, as part of the attribute of 'sex'.[ 76 ] Australia was the first country to add an independent attribute, of 'intersex status'.[ 77 ][ 78 ] Malta was the first to adopt a broader framework of 'sex characteristics', through legislation that also ended modifications to the sex characteristics of minors undertaken for social and cultural reasons.[ 79 ][ 80 ] Global efforts such as the United Nations [Sustainable Development Goal 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainable_Development_Goal_5) is also aimed at ending all forms of discrimination on the basis of gender and sex.[ 81 ]

##### Sexual orientation

They may experience hatred from others because of their sexuality; a term for such hatred based upon one's sexual orientation is often called [homophobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophobia). Many continue to hold negative feelings towards those with non-heterosexual orientations and will discriminate against people who have them or are thought to have them. People of other uncommon sexual orientations also experience discrimination. One study found its sample of heterosexuals to be more prejudiced against [asexual people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asexuality) than against homosexual or bisexual people.[ 83 ]
Employment discrimination based on sexual orientation varies by country. Revealing a lesbian sexual orientation (by means of mentioning an engagement in a rainbow organisation or by mentioning one's partner name) lowers employment opportunities in [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus) and Greece but overall, it has no negative effect in Sweden and Belgium.[ 84 ][ 85 ][ 86 ][ 87 ] In the latter country, even a positive effect of revealing a lesbian sexual orientation is found for women at their fertile ages.
Besides these academic studies, in 2009, [ILGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Lesbian,_Gay,_Bisexual,_Trans_and_Intersex_Association) published a report based on research carried out by Daniel Ottosson at Södertörn University College in [Stockholm, Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm,_Sweden). This research found that of the 80 countries around the world that continue to consider [homosexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality) illegal, five carry the [death penalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_punishment) for homosexual activity, and two do in some regions of the country.[ 88 ] In the report, this is described as "State sponsored homophobia".[ 89 ] This happens in [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) states, or in two cases regions under Islamic authority.[ 90 ][ 91 ] On February 5, 2005, the [IRIN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Humanitarian) issued a reported titled "Iraq: Male homosexuality still a taboo". The article stated, among other things that [honor killings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honor_killings) by Iraqis against a gay family member are common and given some legal protection.[ 92 ] In August 2009, Human Rights Watch published an extensive report detailing torture of men accused of being [gay in Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT_rights_in_Iraq), including the blocking of men's anuses with glue and then giving the men laxatives.[ 93 ] Although gay marriage has been legal in [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa) since 2006, same-sex unions are often condemned as "un-African".[ 94 ] Research conducted in 2009 shows 86% of black lesbians from the [Western Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Cape) live in fear of sexual assault.[ 95 ]

Regional variation exists with respect to [tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toleration), the [antithesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antithesis) of discrimination, in different parts of the world.
A number of countries, especially those in the [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world), have passed measures to alleviate discrimination against sexual minorities, including laws against anti-gay [hate crimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hate_crimes) and workplace discrimination. Some have also legalized same-sex marriage or civil unions in order to grant same-sex couples the same protections and benefits as opposite-sex couples. In 2011, the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations) passed its first resolution recognizing LGBT rights.

##### Reverse discrimination

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=17)] Main article: [Reverse discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_discrimination)See also: [Bumiputera (Malaysia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bumiputera_(Malaysia))Students protesting against [racial quotas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_quota) in Brazil: " *Quer uma vaga? Passe no vestibular!* " ("Do you want a spot? Pass the entrance exam!")
Reverse discrimination is discrimination against members of a dominant or majority group, in favor of members of a minority or historically disadvantaged group.[ 96 ]
This discrimination may seek to redress [social inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_inequalities) under which minority groups have had less access to privileges enjoyed by the majority group. In such cases it is intended to remove discrimination that minority groups may already face. Reverse discrimination can be defined as the unequal treatment of members of the majority groups resulting from preferential policies, as in college admissions or employment, intended to remedy earlier discrimination against minorities.[ 97 ]
Conceptualizing [affirmative action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affirmative_action) as reverse discrimination became popular in the early- to mid-1970s, a time period that focused on under-representation and action policies intended to remedy the effects of past discrimination in both government and the business world.[ 98 ]

##### United Nations documents

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=27)]
Important UN documents addressing discrimination include:

* The [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights) is a declaration adopted by the [United Nations General Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_General_Assembly) on December 10, 1948. It states that:" *Everyone is entitled to all the rights and freedoms set forth in this Declaration, without distinction of any kind, such as race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth or other status.* "[ 117 ]
* The [International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Convention_on_the_Elimination_of_All_Forms_of_Racial_Discrimination) (ICERD) is a United Nations convention. The Convention commits its members to the elimination of [racial discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_discrimination). The convention was adopted and opened for signature by the United Nations General Assembly on December 21, 1965, and entered into force on January 4, 1969.
* The [Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_Elimination_of_All_Forms_of_Discrimination_against_Women) (CEDAW) is an international treaty adopted in 1979 by the United Nations General Assembly. Described as an international [bill of rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_of_rights) for women, it came into force on September 3, 1981.
* The [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convention_on_the_Rights_of_Persons_with_Disabilities) is an international human rights instrument treaty of the United Nations. Parties to the convention are required to promote, protect, and ensure the full enjoyment of human rights by persons with disabilities and ensure that they enjoy full equality under the law. The text was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on December 13, 2006, and opened for signature on March 30, 2007. Following ratification by the 20th party, it came into force on May 3, 2008.

#### Theories and philosophy

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=29)]
[Social theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=29) such as [egalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egalitarianism) assert that [social equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_equality) should prevail. In some societies, including most developed countries, each individual's civil rights include the right to be free from government sponsored social discrimination.[ 120 ] Due to a belief in the capacity to perceive pain or suffering shared by all animals, [abolitionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_(animal_rights)) or [vegan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veganism) egalitarianism maintains that the interests of every individual (regardless of their species), warrant [equal consideration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_consideration_of_interests) with the interests of humans, and that not doing so is [speciesist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speciesist).[ 121 ]
Philosophers have debated as to how inclusive the definition of discrimination should be. Some philosophers have argued that discrimination should only refer to wrongful or disadvantageous treatment in the context of a socially salient group (such as race, gender, sexuality etc.) within a given context. Under this view, failure to limit the concept of discrimination would lead to it being overinclusive; for example, since most murders occur because of some perceived difference between the perpetrator and the victim, many murders would constitute discrimination if the social salience requirement is not included. Thus this view argues that making the definition of discrimination overinclusive renders it meaningless. Conversely, other philosophers argue that discrimination should simply refer to wrongful disadvantageous treatment regardless of the social salience of the group, arguing that limiting the concept only to socially salient groups is arbitrary, as well as raising issues of determining which groups would count as socially salient. The issue of which groups should count has caused many political and social debates.[ 12 ]
Based on realistic-conflict theory[ 122 ] and social-identity theory,[ 123 ] Rubin and Hewstone[ 124 ] have highlighted a distinction among three types of discrimination:

1. *Realistic competition* is driven by self-interest and is aimed at obtaining material resources (e.g., food, territory, customers) for the in-group (e.g., favoring an in-group in order to obtain more resources for its members, including the self).
2. *Social competition* is driven by the need for self-esteem and is aimed at achieving a positive social status for the in-group relative to comparable out-groups (e.g., favoring an in-group in order to make it better than an out-group).
3. *Consensual discrimination* is driven by the need for accuracy[ 125 ] and reflects stable and legitimate intergroup status hierarchies (e.g., favoring a high-status in-group because it is high status).

##### Labeling theory

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=30)] An anti-discrimination education workshop at the [Auschwitz Jewish Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auschwitz_Jewish_Center_in_O%C5%9Bwi%C4%99cim), Poland, 2019
Discrimination, in [labeling theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labeling_theory), takes form as mental categorization of minorities and the use of [stereotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotype). This theory describes difference as deviance from the norm, which results in internal devaluation and [social stigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stigma)[\[ 126 \]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_stigma) that may be seen as discrimination. It is started by describing a "natural" social order. It is distinguished between the fundamental principle of fascism and social democracy.[ 127 ][clarification needed] The [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazis) in 1930s-era Germany and the pre-1990 [Apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid) government of South Africa used racially discriminatory agendas for their political ends. This practice continues with some present day governments.[ 128 ]

##### Game theory

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=31)]
Economist [Yanis Varoufakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanis_Varoufakis) (2013) argues that "discrimination based on utterly arbitrary characteristics evolves quickly and systematically in the experimental laboratory", and that neither classical [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory) nor [neoclassical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_economics) can explain this.[ 129 ]
In 2002, Varoufakis and Shaun Hargreaves-Heap ran an experiment where volunteers played a computer-mediated, multiround [hawk-dove game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawk-dove_game). At the start of each session, each participant was assigned a color at random, either red or blue. At each round, each player learned the color assigned to his or her opponent, but nothing else about the opponent. Hargreaves-Heap and Varoufakis found that the players' behavior within a session frequently developed a discriminatory convention, giving a [Nash equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibrium) where players of one color (the "advantaged" color) [consistently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_strategy) played the aggressive "hawk" strategy against players of the other, "disadvantaged" color, who played the acquiescent "dove" strategy against the advantaged color. Players of both colors used a [mixed strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_strategy) when playing against players assigned the same color as their own. The experimenters then added a [cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoners%27_dilemma) option to the game, and found that disadvantaged players usually cooperated with each other, while advantaged players usually did not. They state that while the equilibria reached in the original hawk-dove game are predicted by [evolutionary game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_game_theory), game theory does not explain the emergence of cooperation in the disadvantaged group. Citing earlier psychological work of [Matthew Rabin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Rabin), they hypothesize that a norm of differing entitlements emerges across the two groups, and that this norm could define a "fairness" equilibrium within the disadvantaged group.[ 130 ]

#### Effects on health

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Discrimination&action=edit&section=32)] This section is an excerpt from [Psychological impact of discrimination on health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_impact_of_discrimination_on_health). [[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Psychological_impact_of_discrimination_on_health&action=edit)]
The [psychological impact of discrimination on health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_impact_of_discrimination_on_health) refers to the cognitive pathways through which discrimination impacts mental and physical health in [marginalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_exclusion), and lower-status groups (e.g. racial and sexual minorities).[ 131 ] Research on the relationship between discrimination and health became grew in the 1990s, when researchers proposed that persisting racial/ethnic disparities in health outcomes could be explained by racial or ethnic differences in experiences with discrimination.[ 132 ] While much research focuses on the interactions between [interpersonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpersonal_relationship) discrimination and health, researchers studying discrimination and health in the United States have proposed that [institutional discrimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_discrimination) and [cultural racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_racism) also create conditions that contribute to persisting racial and economic health disparities.[ 133 ][ 134 ]

#### A stress and coping framework[ 135 ] is applied to investigate how discrimination influences health outcomes in racial, gender, and sexual minorities, as well as on immigrant and indigenous populations.[ 136 ][ 137 ] The research indicates that experiences of discrimination are associated with worse physical and mental health conditions and lead to increased participation in unhealthy behaviors.[ 138 ] Evidence of the inverse link between discrimination and health has been observed consistently across multiple population groups and various cultural and national contexts.[ 139 ]See also
