Unconscious bias and prejudice are deeply ingrained human tendencies with significant implications for individuals and society, particularly in the context of social justice and the pursuit of belonging. The sources define these concepts, detail their pervasive impacts, and offer strategies for addressing them on both individual and systemic levels.

### Understanding Unconscious Bias

**Unconscious bias (or implicit bias)** refers to attitudes and beliefs that occur without our conscious awareness or control [1]. This term was introduced by psychologists Mahzarin Banaji and Anthony Greenwald in 1995, who theorized that social behavior is profoundly influenced by unconscious associations and judgments [2]. Research consistently demonstrates that everyone possesses some form of implicit bias, often without even realizing it [1, 2].

Key characteristics and origins of unconscious bias include:

* **Formation through conditioning** Unconscious biases are formed throughout our lifetimes through societal and parental conditioning [3]. Our brains process vast amounts of information, categorizing it into patterns based on characteristics like gender, profession, sexuality, ethnicity, disability, and body size. These patterns then have assessments imprinted on them, forming the basis of our relationships with others [3].
* **System 1 thinking** Implicit bias is an example of "System 1" thinking, which is automatic and occurs without conscious effort [1]. This means a person can publicly express positive views towards a social group while simultaneously holding unconscious biases against them [1].
* **Psychological roots** The formation of unconscious biases is deeply rooted in psychology, using knowledge and past experiences from social situations, stereotypes, attitudes, cultures, and emotional reactions [4]. Daily activities and media exposure contribute to this knowledge and experience [4].
* **Impact on self-awareness** Many people find it difficult to believe that their behavior can be guided by mental content they are unaware of [5]. These "social mindbugs" can even affect decisions about oneself, potentially leading to self-sabotaging behaviors, and psychologists refer to this as dissociation—the presence of mutually inconsistent ideas in the same mind that remain isolated from each other [6].

While distinct, implicit bias can evolve into **explicit bias** when these unconscious prejudices and beliefs become consciously known, allowing an individual to choose whether to act on them [7].

### Understanding Prejudice

**Prejudice** is broadly defined as a preconceived judgment about a certain group of people, based solely on their membership in that group [8]. It encompasses the opinions and attitudes directed towards these individuals [8]. Historically, early psychological research in the 1920s even sought to prove white supremacy, viewing prejudice as a "natural response" to supposedly inferior races. This perspective shifted in the 1930s and 1940s with growing concern over anti-Semitism, leading theorists to view prejudice as a pathological trait linked to authoritarian personalities [9].

Key aspects of prejudice include:

* **Tendency to over-categorize** Prejudice is often seen as an unjustified and incorrect tendency to over-categorize people, providing reasons for discrimination based solely on social group membership [8].
* **Distinction from discrimination** While closely related, prejudice is not always discrimination [10]. Prejudice involves the affective (feelings), behavioral (tendencies), and cognitive (thoughts) components of an attitude, whereas discrimination specifically involves **negative behaviors or actions** directed towards an individual or group based on their social group [10]. Unconscious prejudices, for instance, can exist without overt discriminatory actions [10].
* **"Othering" as a root** Prejudice often stems from a process called "othering," which involves picking up on biases based on what one perceives as "normal" [11]. This can lead to the dehumanization of entire groups, fueling intolerance and exclusion [12]. Othering is considered the antonym of belonging, implying a lack of acceptance and inclusion [12].

### The Impact of Unconscious Bias and Prejudice on Social Justice and Belonging

Unconscious bias and prejudice have far-reaching negative consequences, profoundly affecting the pursuit of social justice and the fundamental human need for belonging.

* **Hindering organizational capabilities** In workplaces, unconscious bias can significantly hinder an organization's capabilities and negatively impact employee experience [13]. It leads to unfair judgments, overlooked talent, and outright discrimination [14]. Common workplace biases include affinity bias, attribution bias, beauty bias, conformity bias, confirmation bias, contrast bias, gender bias, the halo effect, and the horns effect [14].
* **Systemic and structural discrimination** Racism, whether explicit or unconscious, always harms, discriminates, and offends [15]. It has evolved to operate subtly through implicit bias, contributing to systemic racism that impacts wealth, education, healthcare, employment, police surveillance, criminal justice, and housing [15]. Such institutional practices routinely produce racially inequitable outcomes for people of color and advantages for white people [16]. An example of this is redlining, a form of structural racism that has lasting effects on housing, lending, and education [17].
* **Impact on interactions and opportunities** Unconscious bias negatively affects interactions with people of color, manifesting as subtle cues like body language, awkward conversations, patronizing comments, and tension [18]. It reinforces structural racialization and creates barriers to opportunities [18]. Studies using the Implicit Association Test (IAT) have shown that implicit racial biases held by physicians can predict the quality of medical care they provide, favoring white patients over Black patients [19, 20]. Similarly, evidence suggests employment discrimination against Black candidates, even those with clean records, compared to white candidates with criminal records [21, 22].
* **The "good white self" and denial** Many individuals, particularly white people, consciously strive to be seen as "good people" and to not appear racist [23]. This desire can lead to the "good white self" defense mechanism, where individuals deny the existence or importance of racial oppression, claiming "I don't see color" or adhering to notions of a meritocracy [24, 25]. This defense prevents self-reflection, learning, and acknowledging how one enacts white privilege [23].
* **"Spiritual bypass"** In some spiritual circles, the concept of "we are all one" or "spiritual bypass" is used to deny the pain of racial oppression, suggesting a move beyond racial differences. However, this neglects the very real harm inflicted on Black, Indigenous, and people of color in relative reality [24-26].
* **Othering and its consequences** The process of othering plays a significant role in forming prejudices and can lead to the dehumanization of entire groups [12]. On a societal level, it contributes to institutional discrimination and political policies that target those perceived as "different" or "less deserving" [27]. Authoritarian leaders often exploit this by stoking fear and resentment of "others" to gain support [27]. Ultimately, "othering" is seen as the antonym of belonging, fostering exclusion and intolerance [12].

### Addressing and Reducing Unconscious Bias and Prejudice

Addressing unconscious bias and prejudice requires multifaceted approaches that span individual self-awareness, interpersonal interactions, and systemic change.

* **Individual self-awareness and practice**
  + **Contravening negative patterns:** Actively challenge and replace stereotypical information in your mind with verified facts. Learning about the similarities between stereotyped individuals and the general population can help solidify new knowledge in the subconscious [28].
  + **Conscious overriding:** While biases may never be fully eliminated from the subconscious, the key is to use conscious thought to override them [29].
  + **Examining belief systems:** Our belief systems, shaped by upbringing and experiences, often override logical thinking and justify how we treat others. Examining these systems is a crucial step in beginning allyship [30].
  + **Mindfulness and self-reflection:** Integrating mindfulness into social justice work can shift training spaces and help individuals recognize their racialized self [24, 31]. It involves turning mindful attention to experiences that arise in racially charged situations and choosing how to respond [24].
  + **Cultural humility:** Practice consistent self-evaluation, acknowledge limitations in understanding cultural differences, increase self-awareness of biases, and seek to fix imbalances [32].
  + **Not taking cultural conditioning personally:** Recognize that cultural conditioning is powerful but does not define one's inherent goodness [23, 33]. Building capacity to tolerate discomfort when engaging with diverse cultures and people is essential [33].
* **Interpersonal strategies**
  + **Creating awareness:** In workplaces, this can be achieved through presentations, circulating articles, encouraging Implicit Association Tests (IATs), and organizing workshops [34].
  + **Empathy and perspective-taking:** Research indicates that consciously taking another person's point of view reduces bias against stigmatized groups and suppresses unconscious prejudices [35]. Exposure to counter-stereotypical information (e.g., successful women in male-dominated fields) also helps reduce prejudice [36].
  + **Encouraging intergroup interaction:** Forming relationships with members of different groups is a powerful antidote to bias, widening social networks, decreasing stress, and reducing prejudices [37]. This aligns with the "contact hypothesis," which suggests that prejudice can be reduced when in-group and out-group members spend time together [38, 39]. Such contact enhances knowledge, reduces anxiety, and increases empathy [39].
  + **Mindful communication:** Use "I" statements to avoid generalizing (e.g., "white people") [40]. Listen, learn, and consider the perspectives of people of color, allowing for open conversation without dominating the discussion [41]. When addressing harmful comments, "call people in" with compassion and connection rather than shaming or blaming [42].
  + **Avoiding "white savior complex":** Allies should not aspire to lead but rather work in solidarity with and follow the lead of people of color [43]. Avoid seeking approval or redemption from people of color, or assuming familiarity and trust [25, 44].
* **Systemic and institutional interventions**
  + **Embedding diversity and inclusion:** The importance of diversity and overcoming implicit bias should be integrated into every process, decision, engagement, and interaction within organizations [45].
  + **Targeted universalism:** This strategy addresses social problems by being inclusive of all groups, but paying particular attention to the needs of marginalized groups [46]. It evaluates policies based on their outcomes, not just their intent, recognizing that formal equality can deny existing differences [46].
  + **Addressing structural racialization:** This involves understanding how institutions and policies, regardless of original intent, create and maintain racialized outcomes [47, 48]. The focus should be on *what structures are doing* and intervening to produce better outcomes, rather than just their motivations [48].
  + **Reframing DEI:** Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) initiatives are crucial for fostering inclusion and benefiting all individuals [49].

### Interconnectedness and Complexity

The sources emphasize that understanding unconscious bias and prejudice requires acknowledging the complex interplay of individual psychology, social conditioning, and systemic structures.

* **Constructed identity:** Human identity, including racial identity, is socially constructed rather than an inherent, fixed "real thing" [50, 51]. However, this social construction does not mean it is nonexistent or easily willed away; it has profound descriptive validity in individuals' lives [51]. Those in privileged positions are often taught not to see this constructed nature, leading to unnecessary suffering [50].
* **The social nature of the unconscious:** Our unconscious biases are not merely private thoughts but are heavily influenced by our environment, social structures, and cultural meanings [52, 53]. Images and metaphors from our environment act as cues, shaping our unconscious associations [52, 53].
* **Challenging the "separate self":** Modern Western thought often promotes an "isolated self" or "separate self," particularly associated with white racial consciousness, that excludes and distances itself from the "other" [54, 55]. This view hinders efforts to address suffering by ignoring our profound interconnectedness and interbeing [54, 56, 57]. Buddhism, for example, offers a perspective of the "multiple self" that accepts conflicting elements and promotes self-acceptance, which extends to others [58].
* **Beyond individualism:** A focus solely on individual feelings or conscious intent is insufficient for achieving justice [59, 60]. True social justice requires examining institutional arrangements and structures, as the "whole is greater than the sum of its parts" [61].
* **Global perspective:** While racism is a profound issue in the United States, it is a global problem, rooted in historical events like the slave trade and colonial practices that shaped white supremacy culture in European countries and beyond [50, 62]. The dynamics of privilege and oppression extend across various dimensions of identity, including socioeconomic class, gender, and disability, not just race [50].

In conclusion, addressing unconscious bias and prejudice is essential for advancing social justice and fostering a true sense of belonging. This involves continuous self-reflection, empathetic engagement with others, and a deep, critical understanding of how historical and structural factors perpetuate inequalities, moving beyond denial and simplistic solutions to embrace the complexity of our shared human experience [15, 22-25, 29, 30, 32, 33, 35-46, 48, 53, 63-80].
