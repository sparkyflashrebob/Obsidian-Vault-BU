### Outline
### The Nature of Loving Awareness as the Foundation of Practice

- Underlying all body, mind, and thought is what Jack Kornfield calls loving awareness, described as an untangled, unconditioned knowing that is no different from the awareness through which one perceives the present moment.

- Any moment of noticing what is happening places one in the realm of Buddha nature and unconditional loving awareness.

- This recognition is not historical or conceptual but immediate and direct, requiring personal verification rather than adoption as a belief system or sentiment.

- Participants at the retreat are described as having settled enough over three days that they are available to a direct recognition of this nature within themselves.

- The brightness and presence observed in retreat participants is offered as evidence that each moment of mindful attention connects a person with an inexhaustible resource of immediate energy.

### The Metaphor of Plugging Into Presence

- Each moment of attention is likened to plugging into a wall socket and benefiting from the current of immediate energy inherent in being present.

- Unknowingly and innocently pulling the plug out of the socket represents the habitual loss of vitality that comes from drifting away from present-moment awareness.

- The past and future are described as purely mental constructs, while the present moment is identified as the only place where real vitality and connection with life exist.

- Hafez is quoted as encouraging practitioners to keep squeezing drops of the sun, meaning to stay present, continue practicing, and not abandon the richness of immediate experience.

- Counterfeit coins are described as those momentary pleasures that offer brief satisfaction but drag a person into prolonged suffering, serving as a warning against mistaking fleeting gratification for genuine well-being.

### The Imagined Self Versus Actual Present Experience

- A distinction is drawn between one's actual lived experience in the present moment and the version of oneself that plays continuously in the mind.

- The imagined self is described as a mental construct built on the foundational belief that something is wrong, which then becomes organized around the idea that something is wrong with oneself.

- Life oriented around the imagined self becomes consumed with fixing, solving, and figuring out, all in service of a problem that, upon direct investigation, cannot be found in present-moment experience.

- In moments of genuine mindful attention, the idea of being a problem to solve is described as having no evidence to support it.

- The imagined self operates from the core assumption of not being there yet, perpetuating a cycle of searching that overlooks the completeness already present.

### Direct Inquiry Into Present Experience

- Howard Cohn invites participants to speak into the silence and report what they find in the gap between the end of one narrative and the beginning of the next.

- Responses from participants include words such as home, peace, precious, breath, content, and loving, pointing to the natural qualities of awareness when stories about the self are temporarily absent.

- These qualities of completeness, love, contentment, and calm are identified as one's true nature, described as hiding in plain view rather than being something to be created or attained.

- Hakuin's verse is cited to illustrate that all beings are by nature Buddha, just as ice is by nature water, and that searching afar for what is already near is the fundamental error of the unexamined life.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's image of the richest person on earth going around begging for a living is used to describe the unnecessary poverty of a mind that does not recognize its own inherent wholeness.

### The Samsaric Loop and the Habit of Searching Elsewhere

- The samsaric loop is described as a self-reinforcing cycle in which the mind, oriented around a historical self moving from past to future, treats the present moment as either a passthrough or an obstacle.

- Eckhart Tolle's teaching is referenced to explain that when life is viewed through the lens of a self passing through the present on the way to somewhere else, the present becomes either irrelevant or an enemy to well-being.

- Living within the narrative of a historical self means one must live that life entirely, depriving oneself of the richness of connecting with life exactly where it touches the senses.

- The Dalai Lama is quoted as observing that people sacrifice health for money, then money for health, live anxiously about the future, and die having never truly lived in the present.

- Craving for becoming, craving for pleasure, and craving for more existence are identified as the root causes of mental stress, rooted in the chronic tendency to want things to be other than they are.

### The Role of Feeling Tones as the Ground Zero of Reactivity

- Every moment of sensory experience carries a feeling tone that is either pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral, and this is identified as the ground zero of the entire cycle of reactivity and suffering.

- When a pleasant feeling tone goes unrecognized, it triggers a small mental flinch of liking, which if unnoticed, develops into wanting and then into compulsive strategizing about how to obtain or keep the pleasant experience.

- Unpleasant feeling tones similarly trigger aversion, which if followed, leads to the compulsion to escape, avoid, or eliminate the source of discomfort.

- The Buddha's teaching is presented as an invitation to recognize these feeling tones before they fully flower into hindrances such as desire, aversion, restlessness, and doubt.

- Recognizing feeling tones as they arise is described as heading off the cycle at the pass, preventing the chain of reactivity from taking root and spreading into prolonged mental suffering.

### Working With the Hindrances as a Path

- Rather than being obstacles to practice, hindrances such as desire, aversion, and worry are described as the very material through which practice deepens.

- When the feeling of desire meets the light of awareness and is simply held without being followed, it fades away without needing to be satisfied, returning the practitioner to a sense of well-being independent of circumstances.

- Awareness of worry is described as not itself being worried, and awareness of desire as not itself desiring, pointing to the quality of knowing that remains undisturbed by the changing weather of mental states.

- Placing attention in the body rather than following the mental image associated with a desired outcome allows one to feel directly the physical tension of suspended happiness that accompanies states of wanting.

- Letting go is clarified as not meaning getting rid of, but rather loosening the grip, opening the hands, and allowing desire, aversion, and all changing conditions to simply be what they are.

### The Story of Rohitasa and the End of the World Within the Body

- The story of Rohitasa the Skywalker is presented as an encapsulation of the mind's tendency to search outside itself for freedom from suffering.

- Rohitasa, possessing the superpower of extraordinary speed, attempted to walk to the end of the physical world in order to transcend the cycle of birth, stress, and death, but walked for a hundred years and died without reaching it.

- Upon meeting the Buddha, Rohitasa asked whether it was possible to reach the end of the world by going, and the Buddha replied that it was not, but that only those who come to the end of the world become liberated.

- The Buddha then offered a condensed version of the four noble truths by stating that within this fathom-long body, with its senses and perceptions, lies the world, the cause of the world, the end of the world, and the path leading to the end of the world.

- This teaching is presented as a direct pointer to the fact that the path begins in the body, the length of the path is staying present in the body, and the end of the path is found within this same fathom-long body rather than anywhere else.

### Stabilizing Presence and the Ongoing Commitment to Practice

- Practice is described as not creating loving awareness but rather as developing the capacity to recognize, accommodate, and stabilize what is already naturally present.

- Ajahn Chah's instruction not to let the mind leave the body is offered as a practical summary of the entire path, pointing to the importance of remaining anchored in immediate sensory experience.

- The liberating shift from being carried along by the story of oneself to simply noticing that story is identified as something that becomes increasingly possible through sustained mindfulness practice.

- Mindfulness of thinking is described as the recognition that the story of myself is not myself, allowing the practitioner to enjoy the story without being imprisoned by it.

- Sri Ramakrishna is referenced as an example of a practitioner whose mind had largely ceased its habitual movement toward searching elsewhere, resting instead in an unconditioned sense of well-being.

### Next Steps

- Practitioners are encouraged to continue their walking meditation period of fifteen minutes as an extension of the present-moment awareness cultivated during the talk.

- Sustaining the clarity and presence developed over the retreat is identified as the primary ongoing commitment, with the understanding that the tools for recognizing one's true nature have been meaningfully cultivated.

- Continued investigation into the difference between actual present experience and the narrative version of oneself is recommended as the central direction of ongoing practice.

### Stories
**Howard Cohn's Daughter Molly — The Comparing Mind**
- Shared by Howard Cohn about his daughter Molly
- Molly turned 23 the day before the talk; her birthday often falls during this retreat
- When Molly was approximately two and a half to three years old, she attended nursery school for the first time
- Howard and his wife Annie had marveled at Molly's unique expression of life, her ringlet curls, blueberry eyes, and what Howard called "molliness"
- At nursery school there were two blonde, straight-haired twin girls in her class
- Molly came home and was seen standing in front of the mirror pulling on her hair
- Howard uses this as an illustration of the moment the comparing mind begins and the natural self is replaced by a version that says "I should be more like that person"
**Howard Cohn's Own Retreat Experience — Staying with Difficulty**
- Shared by Howard Cohn about himself
- Set in the early 1980s at the Insight Meditation Society in Barre, Massachusetts
- Howard was on a long practice retreat; his attention had become very immediate and subtle
- He began noticing the rapid dissolution of all phenomena, which produced fear and aversion
- He felt a strong compulsion to roll up his mat and go home, with his mind rationalizing that he had done enough
- While walking down the steps at IMS, he ran into Jack, who was visiting the retreat while on the East Coast to see his dying father
- Howard described his experience to Jack; Jack looked at him and said simply, "Stay"
- Howard stayed and the practice opened up significantly
- He was simultaneously working with teacher Deepa Ma, who was guiding him through this practice period
- He told Deepa Ma the same thing; she took his hand, caressed it, and said, "If you stay with this, you will earn great merit"
- He stayed, and the practice opened up
**The Story of Rohitasa the Skywalker**
- Cited by Howard Cohn from Buddhist scripture
- Rohitasa was a celestial being with the superpower of walking extremely fast
- He suffered from chronic dissatisfaction and decided to use his power to walk to the end of the physical world to find freedom
- He walked for a hundred years in celestial time and died without reaching the end of the world
- He was reborn and went to the Buddha, asking whether it was possible to reach the end of the world by going
- The Buddha said no, and that only those who come to the end of the world become liberated
- The Buddha then gave a condensed version of the four noble truths, stating that within the fathom-long body lie the world, its cause, its end, and the path to its end
