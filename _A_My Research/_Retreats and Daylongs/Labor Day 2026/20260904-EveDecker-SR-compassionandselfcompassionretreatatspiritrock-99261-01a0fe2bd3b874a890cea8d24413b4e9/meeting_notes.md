### Outline
### The Purpose and Foundation of Buddhist Teachings

- Buddhist teachings are described as serving three core functions: one's own welfare, the welfare of others, and leading to peace.

- The Buddha's spiritual intention originated from witnessing suffering in the forms of an aged person, an ill person, and a corpse, which inspired his aspiration to understand and alleviate human suffering.

- The broad geography of Buddhism is introduced as containing many teachings, with the five-day retreat focusing primarily on mindfulness as well as the Brahma Viharas, the divine abodes of the awakened heart.

- All teachings, whether wisdom-based mindfulness practices or heart-based practices, are described as supporting clarity, well-being, peace, and courage.

### The Four Brahma Viharas and Their Relationships

- The four Brahma Viharas are introduced as Metta (loving kindness), Karuna (compassion), Mudita (sympathetic joy), and equanimity, collectively known as the divine abodes or places of the awakened heart.

- An analogy using rectangles and squares is offered to explain the relationship between these practices, where loving kindness is a general rectangle and compassion and joy are squares, meaning they are specific types of loving kindness.

- Karuna is defined as showing friendliness and care specifically in the face of suffering, while Mudita is described as showing friendliness and care in the context of joy, particularly the joy of others.

- Loving kindness is distinguished from compassion by its scope, with loving kindness being a general well-wishing and radiation of love, while compassion is that same well-wishing directed specifically toward pain, challenge, loss, stress, and distress of all kinds and degrees.

### The Two Forms of Compassion and Their Importance on Retreat

- Compassion is described as existing in two interrelated forms, compassion for others and self-compassion, with the practice of either form strengthening both.

- Both forms are emphasized as essential during retreat, with compassion for the community and compassion for oneself being equally important in meeting the inevitable challenges of daily retreat practice.

- Practicing meeting challenges, whether as minor as an itch or as significant as a broken heart, with care and kindness rather than aversion or shame is presented as wise training of the mind.

- The Buddha's observation that what we practice we strengthen is connected to the modern concept of neuroplasticity, highlighting that compassion is a skill that can be developed and taken beyond retreat into daily life.

### The Definition and Practice of Compassion

- Compassion is formally defined as a sensitivity to the suffering of self and others combined with a commitment to try to alleviate and prevent it.

- Two components are identified within this definition: the opening to suffering to a tolerable degree, and the sensing into wise action or wise response toward oneself and others.

- Cultivating compassion involves developing awareness of suffering without moving to blame or aversion, followed by identifying one or two caring responses available in the moment.

- Compassion is clarified as being more than a concept or a feeling, described instead as clarity, wise understanding, and aspiration that can meet suffering with calm even when it cannot ease it.

- The image of holding a crying baby is used to illustrate that the compassionate presence itself, even without resolving the suffering, carries meaning and value for both the giver and the receiver.

### The Poem Kindness by Naomi Shihab Nye

- The poem Kindness by Palestinian American poet Naomi Shihab Nye is introduced as a work that speaks to the essential nature of compassion as a response to the ubiquity of suffering.

- Loss, desolation, and the witnessing of another's death are described in the poem as necessary experiences before one can truly understand kindness in its deepest form.

- Sorrow is presented in the poem as the companion to kindness, with the understanding of sorrow's full scope being the prerequisite for kindness to become the only sensible response.

- Kindness is ultimately portrayed as an ever-present companion that goes with a person everywhere, tying their shoes and sending them out into the day, representing an internalized and embodied state of being.

### The Buddha's Instruction on Radiating Compassion

- The Buddha's instruction for compassion practice is presented as dwelling with a heart filled with compassion pervading all directions, above, below, and around, encompassing the entire world.

- This radiation of compassion is described as abundant, measureless, and free from enmity and ill will, representing a wholesome and beautiful state even when bittersweet due to its contact with suffering.

- Compassion is identified as the root source of courage, patience, perseverance, forgiveness, and kindness, making it a foundational quality for the spiritual journey.

- Practitioners are encouraged to note and savor moments of compassion when they arise, while also extending compassion to themselves during contracted or non-compassionate states, which arise from conditioning and are not to be blamed.

### Larry Yang's Graduated Approach to Compassion

- A quote from teacher Larry Yang is shared to encourage realism and a long-view perspective on the journey toward compassion and wisdom.

- The graduated aspiration moves from being loving, open, and aware, to being kind, to being non-judgmental, to not causing harm, and finally to causing the least harm possible when full compassion is not accessible.

- This progression is likened to turning a large ocean liner, where the movement may be imperceptible but still matters and represents meaningful progress in shifting deep-seated emotional and mental patterns.

- The reality of often tying shoes and purchasing bread with self-criticism or distraction is acknowledged as part of the human journey, with the compassion of patience offered as the appropriate response to this reality.

### Sharon Salzberg's Teaching on Acceptance and the Second Arrow

- A quote from Sharon Salzberg clarifies that acceptance of painful circumstances does not mean passivity, isolation, or demeaning oneself while facing difficulty.

- Acceptance is described as opening the door to seeking change from a place of love for oneself, distinguishing it from resignation or defeat.

- The Buddhist concept of the second arrow is introduced, where the first arrow represents the original challenge and the second arrow represents the self-aversion strategies that compound the suffering.

- Retreat practice is framed as an opportunity to begin letting go of these second-arrow strategies through the cultivation of self-compassion.

### The Three Components of Self-Compassion Practice

- Self-compassion practice is described as having three components: mindfulness, common humanity, and self-kindness, which together form a complete response to difficulty.

- Mindfulness in this context means seeing clearly what is happening without the extremes of rumination and storytelling on one hand or denial on the other, allowing for an accurate and present-moment response.

- The default mode network of the thinking mind is described as having a negativity bias and a tendency toward worst-case scenarios, making mindfulness a necessary counterbalance to rumination.

- Common humanity involves reminding oneself that whatever challenge is being faced, one is not alone with it, countering the isolating tendency to feel uniquely singled out for suffering.

- Evolutionary biology is referenced to support the importance of common humanity, noting that humans evolved as creatures of community and that feelings of isolation activate stress responses in the limbic brain.

- Self-kindness involves speaking to oneself with kind language, using phrases such as "May I be safe," "May I be peaceful," "I have your back," and "May I accept myself just as I am."

- All mammals, not only humans, are noted to respond positively to safe, supportive touch and calming vocal tone, while humans additionally respond powerfully to language, making the crafting of personal kind phrases a meaningful practice.

### Traditional Compassion Phrases and Their Application

- The traditional loving kindness phrases of safety, peace, health, and ease are distinguished from the traditional compassion phrase, which is "May all living beings be freed from all stress and pain."

- The Pali phrase "Sata Saba Dukkha Amuchan too" is offered as the traditional formulation, with a simpler version being "May you be free from your pain."

- These phrases are described as cultivating in the heart a wish for freedom and peace, with the understanding that beings are on their own journeys and that one's capacity to alleviate suffering has limits.

- Practitioners are encouraged to find or create their own language that resonates personally, to be brought in when the waters of experience become choppy.

### Guided Self-Compassion Practice

- A guided self-compassion practice based on the work of Kristin Neff and Christopher Germer, rooted in Buddhist practice and known as the Self-Compassion Break, is led for the final portion of the session.

- Participants are invited to bring to mind a workable challenge from retreat or life, such as physical pain, a recurring difficult thought, or the general challenge of sustained sitting practice.

- The mindfulness component is practiced by naming the experience simply, using phrases such as "This is a moment of stress" or "This is a moment of suffering," without added story or suppression.

- Common humanity is then applied by reminding oneself that suffering is a part of life for every living being and that others in the community would feel similarly under similar circumstances.

- Self-kindness is introduced somatically by bringing awareness to the sensations in the hands and arms as a form of soothing presence, with the option to place a hand on the heart or belly.

- The neuroscience of safe, soothing touch is referenced, noting that when such touch is received, the body releases feel-good chemicals such as oxytocin, making this a somatic self-compassion practice.

- Kind phrases such as "May I hold myself in kindness," "May I accept myself as I am right now," and "May I be present for the joys and the sorrows" are offered as the verbal dimension of self-kindness.

- The practice concludes with the invitation to extend this same compassion outward to others whenever it becomes available and whenever one remembers to bring it in.

### Next Steps

- Practitioners are encouraged to continue developing compassion and self-compassion as ongoing skills beyond the retreat, applying the three-component practice of mindfulness, common humanity, and self-kindness to challenges as they arise in daily life.

- Further exploration of the Self-Compassion Break developed by Kristin Neff and Christopher Germer is recommended as a resource for those who find the practice supportive.

- The evening schedule includes a sitting session followed by a talk at 7, continuing the retreat's structured program of practice and teaching.
