### Opening Night Retreat at Spirit Rock — Labor Day Retreat
📅 October 2, 2026
👥 Main Teacher: James Baraz | Teachers: Eve Decker, Howie Cohn, Dawn Mauricio, Terry Vandiver
### Outline
### Introduction of the Teaching Team at Spirit Rock Labor Day Retreat

- James Baraz opened the retreat by welcoming all participants and introducing the teaching team, emphasizing the sense of friendship and shared joy among the group.

- Eve Decker was introduced as a co-guiding teacher for the Insight Berkeley community, praised for her ability to meet people where they are and for her many years of sharing the Dharma.

- Her role for the retreat was specified as leading each evening's late-night talk, late-night sit, and musical chanting sessions.

- Howie Cohn was introduced as a decades-long collaborator, noted as one of the original teachers at Spirit Rock, having led the Labor Day retreat for approximately 35 of its 40 years.

- Dawn Mauricio was described as a radiant being and core teacher for Sacred Mountain Sangha, based in Montreal, who has been part of the teaching team for approximately seven to eight years.

- Terry Vandiver was introduced as a movement, yoga, and wisdom teacher with roughly 25 years of involvement in the retreat, whose role would be to support participants in becoming embodied and present.

### Purpose and Value of Intensive Retreat Practice

- James Baraz described the Labor Day retreat length as ideal for both newer and experienced practitioners, as it allows sufficient time for mindfulness to develop through sustained, consecutive moments of presence.

- Participants were encouraged to release the sense of controlling their retreat experience and instead simply show up with openness to whatever arises.

- The retreat was framed not as a goal-oriented endeavor but as a journey with natural ups and downs, where each participant would receive exactly the lessons they needed.

- Baraz emphasized that there is no way to fail the retreat experience, offering this as a source of comfort and relief for all attendees.

- The retreat was described as a rare and precious opportunity, supported by the teaching of the Mangala Sutta, which identifies being with spiritual people and hearing the Dharma at the right time as a supreme blessing.

- Participants were invited to reflect inwardly on all the circumstances, people, and intentions that made their attendance possible, acknowledging the extraordinary good fortune of being present together.

### The Nature of True Self and Buddha Nature

- Baraz shared a teaching attributed to the Buddha stating that within this fathom-long body, the whole of life is revealed, framing the body and mind as a personal laboratory for understanding the human experience.

- Understanding oneself deeply was presented as a path toward greater compassion and recognition of the same qualities in others.

- A passage from Tibetan teacher Nyoshul Kempo was read, stating that Buddha nature, the essence of awakened enlightenment, is present in everyone and is forever flawless.

- Those who recognize their true nature were described as enlightened, while those who overlook it remain deluded, with no path to enlightenment other than recognizing one's own nature.

### Foundations of Mindfulness Practice

- Baraz described the core task of mindfulness practice as simply knowing what is happening now, whether sitting, walking, eating, or experiencing emotion.

- Most people were noted to live predominantly in the past or future, and returning to the present moment requires consistent practice and patience.

- Sincerity of heart was identified as the key ingredient in practice, meaning a genuine willingness to be present rather than following rigid rules about how practice should look.

- Participants were encouraged to listen inwardly to what they need in each moment to support presence, rather than defaulting to should or should not thinking.

- A balance between ease and effort was highlighted, with high achievers advised to soften and relax, while more laid-back practitioners were encouraged to bring greater intentionality and sincerity.

### Eve Decker's Introduction and Aspiration as a Practice Anchor

- Eve Decker introduced herself as a co-leader of the Berkeley Insight community and a core teacher for Asaya Sangha, a self-identified women's sangha operating online in California.

- Her musical background was noted as central to her role at the retreat, where she would lead evening chanting sessions with guitar.

- Identifying and returning to a personal aspiration or intention was offered as a key practice tool, described as a guiding light amid the constantly changing phenomena of retreat life.

- Her own aspiration was shared as being for the sake of all beings, drawn directly from the Buddha's teachings.

### Retreat Yoga and Movement Practice Overview

- Terry Vandiver described retreat yoga as the practice of paying caring and gentle attention to the body, framing this simple gesture as a form of love.

- Morning movement sessions were outlined as 15 to 20 minutes of flowing, gentle, and expansive movement in the hall, designed for all participants including those who need chair support.

- Floor-based yoga was described as the practice for days one and three, emphasizing gentleness and accessibility, with no advanced poses included.

- Days two and four were designated for chair-based gyrokinesis practice, described as a combination of yoga, qigong, dance, and swimming, beneficial for spinal health.

- Accessibility was stated as a central goal, with chair alternatives offered for anyone who finds floor-based movement difficult.

- A brief guided movement sequence was led in the hall, including arm sweeps, a gesture of hope, hip circles, and a longevity sequence attributed to Ajahn Jhamani, intended to ground participants in their bodies at the start of the retreat.

### The Three Refuges as a Foundation for Retreat Practice

- Howie Cohn introduced the concept of taking refuge as a ritual that distinguishes retreat from ordinary daily life and orients practitioners toward reliable sources of support.

- The three refuges in Buddhist teaching were identified as the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha, each offering an alternative to the habitual refuges of devices, food, entertainment, and imagined futures.

- The historical Buddha was described as a human being who, through years of seeking, realized that what he had been looking for was the natural state of his own mind, and upon awakening simply became known as one who is awake.

- Taking refuge in the Buddha was reframed in an immediate sense as placing one's faith and attention on the innate capacity to be aware, described as completely portable and accessible in every moment.

- Refuge in the Dharma was explained as turning toward whatever is actually being experienced right now, rather than placing hope in a future that never arrives.

- Taking refuge in the Sangha was described both as drawing on the lifting power of practicing with others and as recognizing the gift one's own practice offers to the community simply by showing up.

- The refuges were chanted in Pali in a call-and-response format, with Cohn explaining that the Pali language closely approximates the scriptural language of the Buddha and carries an evocative quality in its tones.

### The Five Precepts as Mindfulness Trainings for Community Living

- Dawn Mauricio introduced the five precepts not as commandments or restrictions but as mindfulness trainings and invitations to live harmoniously with oneself and others, both on retreat and in daily life.

- Abiding by the precepts was described as offering the gift of blamelessness, meaning that when practitioners follow them as best they can, the heart and mind are clearer and less encumbered during formal practice.

- The first precept of non-harming was presented on a spectrum ranging from honoring all life to at minimum refraining from harm, with the acknowledgment that practitioners move between these poles throughout the day.

- Non-stealing was framed as creating an environment where others do not need to feel possessive, with the observation that even this seemingly basic standard is not upheld by many world leaders.

- The third precept regarding sexual energy was explained as allowing fellow practitioners to relax, knowing they are not being pursued with romantic or sexual intent.

- Right speech was described as building trust through honesty, with the additional nuance that on retreat it manifests primarily in how practitioners speak to themselves internally.

- Mauricio shared that harsh self-talk during practice is a form of violating right speech, and that retreat is an opportunity to notice and shift toward greater tenderness and self-compassion.

- The fifth precept regarding intoxicants was explained as supporting clarity of mind and heart, with the clarification that prescription medications should continue to be taken as part of non-harming.

- The Buddha's motivation for teaching the precepts was described not as policing behavior but as creating conditions for awakening and freedom from suffering.

### Technology Renunciation Ceremony

- Participants were invited to participate in a ritual renunciation of their devices, framed as a mindfulness training and an opportunity to step across a threshold into retreat.

- Devices were described as dukkha machines and mental proliferation machines that scatter attention and ultimately fail to satisfy, making their temporary surrender a gift to oneself.

- The ceremony involved participants forming two lines, walking to the front of the hall, placing their devices in baskets, and returning to their seats while Eve Decker led a chant.

- Those who needed to retain their devices for medical or essential reasons were invited to participate symbolically by bowing or making a gesture toward the basket, ensuring no one felt excluded or shamed.

- Surrendering devices was framed as honoring the effort already made to attend the retreat and as protecting the opportunity for inner quiet, presence, and ease.

### Community Introduction Exercise and Closing Meditation

- Participants were organized into small groups of three to briefly introduce themselves before entering noble silence, sharing their name, pronouns, location of origin, and intention for the retreat.

- Intentions were encouraged to be honest and unpretentious rather than lofty, acknowledging that a wide range of motivations for attending are equally valid.

- A guided closing meditation of approximately ten minutes was led by Mauricio, inviting participants to settle into a comfortable posture and notice whatever was present in their experience.

- The meditation drew together the evening's teachings by returning to James Baraz's instruction to notice what is happening now as the essential practice of taking refuge.

- Participants were guided to bring to mind their personal intention and to extend wishes for all intentions in the room to come to fruition, using a visualization of gathering and releasing blessings.

- The close of the meditation marked the official beginning of noble silence for the retreat.

### Next Steps

- Participants were to begin noble silence immediately following the closing meditation, with the option to remain in the hall for continued practice or retire for the evening.

- Morning movement sessions were planned to begin the following day in the hall, with Terry Vandiver leading gentle flowing movement before breakfast.

- Daily practice discussions, meditation instructions, and evening Dharma talks were confirmed as ongoing supports throughout the retreat period.

- Eve Decker was to lead late-night chanting and sitting sessions each evening as part of the retreat schedule.

- Devices were to remain in the office for the duration of the retreat, with access available through the manager for genuine essential needs.

### Stories
**Dawn Mauricio — First Retreat in Thailand**
- Dawn shared a personal story about her first retreat, held in Thailand
- She saw the teacher for only five minutes a day and received one instruction, then was left to practice alone for 24 hours
- When her mind wandered, she would berate herself, saying "You have one job, mind — to stay on the breath. You can't even do that."
- She reflected that she was unknowingly cultivating harshness toward herself alongside mindfulness and concentration
- She used this story to illustrate the fourth precept around speech, specifically how one speaks to oneself on retreat, and the importance of tenderness and compassion
- She noted it took her a few years to shift this pattern and expressed hope that others would find it more quickly
**Howie Cohn — The Historical Buddha**
- Howie recounted the story of the historical Buddha as a prince-like person who sought relief from suffering
- He tried many teachers and many practices before realizing that what he had been looking for was the natural state of his own mind
- Upon awakening, he did not become somebody — he simply became awake
- This was followed by 45 years of teaching, shared heart to heart and mind to mind, forming the stream of the Dharma and the Arya Sangha

### Physical, Mental Challenges, Affects on Practice
**Over-Efforting**
- James cautioned that over-efforting works against practice, particularly for high achievers who want to be good meditators
- The antidote described is softening, relaxing, and cultivating spaciousness and ease
**Difficulty Being Present**
- James acknowledged that most people habitually live in the future, the past, or fantasy
- Being present takes practice and patience, with repeated returning to the moment with kindness
**Harshness Toward Oneself**
- Dawn described the tendency to be mean to oneself when the mind wanders during practice
- This was identified as something that can be cultivated inadvertently alongside mindfulness if not addressed
- Tenderness and compassion toward oneself were offered as the corrective
**Physical Accessibility in Movement**
- Terry noted that getting up and down from the floor may be difficult for some participants
- Chair yoga was offered as an accessible alternative for floor-based sessions
- Chairs were also made available for support during standing morning movement sessions
**Tiredness at the Start of Retreat**
- Terry acknowledged that participants may be tired in the mornings but encouraged attendance at the early movement session
- Dawn acknowledged that some participants would be ready for bed immediately after the opening session
**Technology as a Mental Challenge**
- Dawn described devices as dukkha machines and papancha machines — suffering and mental proliferation machines
- The constant barrage of discursive thought associated with technology was identified as a challenge to settling the mind
- The renunciation ceremony was framed as a support for creating conditions for the mind to settle and the heart to clear
