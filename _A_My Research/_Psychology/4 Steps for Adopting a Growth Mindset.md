
Use the following four steps to develop a growth mindset, based on research by Dweck and colleagues:

1. Listen to the voice of a fixed mindset in your head. This is about being mindful of your own thoughts when faced with a challenge. Notice if the thoughts are telling you that you don’t have the talent, the intelligence or if you find yourself reacting with anxiety or anger when someone offers feedback to you.
2. Notice that you have a choice. You can accept those fixed mindset thoughts or question them. Take a few moments to practice a mindful pause.
3. Question the fixed mindset attitudes. When your fixed mindset says “What if I fail? I’ll be a failure,” you can ask yourself “Is that true? Most successful people fail. That’s how they learn.” Or if fixed mindset says “What if I can’t do this project? I don’t have the skills,” reply with “Can I be absolutely sure I don’t have the skills? In truth, I can only know if I try. And if I don’t have the skills, doing this will help me to learn them.”
4. Take action on the growth mindset. This will make you enjoy the challenges in the workplace, seeing them as opportunity to grow rather than avoid. Use the above system if you mind starts leaning towards the fixed mindset.

Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck and her colleagues pioneered the psychological theory of two core mindsets: the fixed mindset and the growth mindset. The Two Mindsets

- Fixed Mindset: The belief that abilities, intelligence, and talents are static, innate, and unchangeable. People with this view avoid challenges and give up easily when facing failure.
- Growth Mindset: The belief that talents and intelligence can be developed through dedication, effort, and hard work. People with this view embrace challenges and persist after setbacks. [[1](https://teachingcommons.stanford.edu/teaching-guides/foundations-course-design/learning-activities/growth-mindset-and-enhanced-learning#:~:text=A%20growth%20mindset%20is%20the%20belief%20that,mindset:%20*%20Challenge%20themselves%20*%20Believe%20th), [2](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/growth-mindset#:~:text=According%20to%20Stanford%20psychologist%20Carol%20Dweck%2C%20a,improved%20over%20time.%20People%20with%20a%20g), [4](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/psychology/mindset-theory#:~:text=Key%20Terms%20*%20Implicit%20Theories:%20Beliefs%20about,range%20of%20different%20implicit%20theories%20about%20the), [5](https://www.facebook.com/DrJulieSmith/videos/the-research-conducted-by-stanford-psychologist-carol-dweck-looked-into-the-prof/1293651331136121/#:~:text=The%20research%20conducted%20by%20Stanford%20psychologist%20Carol,have%20on%20our%20lives.%20More%20specifically%2C%20she)]

Key Studies on Praise

- The Praise Study: Dweck and her team studied hundreds of fifth-graders after giving them an easy puzzle test.
- Intelligence vs. Effort: Students praised for being "smart" mostly chose easier follow-up tests. Students praised for working hard ("effort") chose harder, more challenging tests.
- Performance Impact: When given very hard tests later, the effort-praised group showed higher resilience and improved their scores on later tests by nearly 30%, whereas intelligence-praised scores dropped. [[6](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NWv1VdDeoRY#:~:text=so%20check%20this%20out%20carol%20Dwek%20and,they%20did%20is%20they%20took%20over%204)]

Important Nuances

- Process Praise: Dweck emphasizes praising the _process_ (strategies, focus, and hard work) rather than fixed traits like intelligence.
- Misapplications: Dweck has cautioned against misusing the concept as simple motivational cheerleading or blaming individuals for lacking a growth mindset without providing supportive environments.
