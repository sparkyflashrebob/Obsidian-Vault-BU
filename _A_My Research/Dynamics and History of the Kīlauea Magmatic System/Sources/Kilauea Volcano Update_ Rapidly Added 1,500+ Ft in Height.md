A portion of Hawaii's famed Kilauea volcano is
more than 1,000 feet, yes 1,000 feet higher than
it was a mere 6.5 years ago. You might be
wondering what exactly I am referring to,
because you don't see any new towering mountains
on Hawaii's Big Island. And you'd be right. What
I am referring to is instead Kilauea's summit
caldera, which lately has been filling at an
absurdly fast rate. To show you exactly what I
mean, here is the same exact caldera as it stood
in 2019, marking a spot where Hawaii's most active
volcano lost 1,640 feet in height due to the
removal of around 800 million
cubic meters of molten rock much of which ended up
erupting near Leilani Estates in 2018. And now,
here's the same caldera at the end of 2020. Now
mid 2021. 2022. 2023. 2024. And now finally, 2025.
So, exactly how much lava has Kilauea erupted in
this timespan, and how much has the crater floor
risen by? Based on a combination of my own and
U.S. Geological Survey estimates, since the end of
2018, Kilauea's deep caldera has been filled by a
maximum of 1,526 feet of fresh lava which has
a combined total volume of 305.1 million cubic
meters of molten rock. Wow, so whereas Kilauea's
caldera in late 2018 was for a time up to 1,821
feet deep, it is today only up to 295 feet deep.
With that in mind, how long will it take for the
lava lake to rise to its pre-eruption level? For
this question, it truly does depend on the average
rate of lava the volcano erupts each month since
it is not always actively erupting or when it is
erupting, erupting at the same rate. Here is a
quick breakdown of how much lava was emplaced at
Kilauea's summit caldera each year. For example,
if we assume that Kilauea is going to continue
erupting at the exact same rate that it has this
month, May 2025, then lava will completely refill
its youngest caldera and spill onto the wider
caldera floor on May 13th, 2027. A Thursday by the
way. If we expand it to the broader 2025 average,
then that date is put back a few months to
September 15th 2027. If we take the average since
the modern trend of moderately to fully cyclical
eruptions initiated on the first day of 2023,
that date falls all the way to September 14th,
2033. And, if we take the entire duration since
Kilauea's first post 2018 caldera collapse
eruption, we get February 11th 2033. Since the two
longest term averages suggest a 2033 date, that is
probably about when the deepest part of Kilauea's
2018 caldera for all intents and purposes will
essentially cease to exist. What I mean is the
caldera will still exist, but it won't be obvious
having been almost entirely if not entirely
filled with lava in the prior years. It will
simply look like Kilauea did pre-2018 collapse.
Now, I do want to note that the depth figures I
just stated truly are educated guesses but with
a decent amount of supporting math involved. The
Hawaiian Volcano Observatory has not been taking
sensor measurements of the crater depth since
late March of this year. The last lava lake top
elevation data they got was 964 meters elevation
which is only 19 meters lower than I interpret
it to be today. Yet, while it only took less than
48 hours to fill the first 95 meters of the 2018
caldera, it is going to take significantly longer
to fill the last 95 meters. And this comes down to
simple length and width. The deepest 95 meters of
the original 2018 caldera were 312 feet or less
wide. Yet, at the present, when lava erupts at
Kilauea, it spreads out to a much larger area
spanning 7,907 feet wide. While the caldera
isn't a circle, if it was that would mean that it
takes 642.27 times more lava to add one foot of
new elevation than it did for adding one foot new
elevation to the crater bottom when the lava had
only filled it to 95 meters or 312 feet deep.
This is why only 36.3% of the lava necessary
to reach the top has erupted since the 2018
collapse, despite it looking deceptively close.
The process of a caldera forming due to a large
volume flank eruption and then refilling of
said area has occurred numerous times throughout
the long history of the Kilauea shield volcano,
most recently in 200 BCE, 1500, and of course
2018. This is just a friendly reminder of how
dynamic the Kilauea volcano still is.
