# Ajahn Sumedho on Fear: A Path to Fearlessness

Ajahn Sumedho offers extensive insights into the nature of fear and how to approach it through Buddhist practice, primarily by cultivating awareness and understanding its conditioned nature.

Here's a comprehensive overview of his teachings on fear:

\*\*1. The Nature and Origin of Fear:\*\*
\*   \*\*Fear is inherent in the conditioned realm\*\*: The world we experience through our body and senses is inherently frightening, this is its nature \[1-3\]. It is a "fear-ridden realm" \[3\].
\*   \*\*Fear arises from the unknown and imagination\*\*: When we think about the future, which is inherently unknown, our imagination creates scenarios (e.g., operations, incurable diseases, death, war, global catastrophes like climate change, nuclear war, meteorites) that evoke fear \[1, 4-6\].
\*   \*\*Fear is linked to identification with the mortal form\*\*: When we identify with our physical body and its vulnerabilities – aging, sickness, disability, and eventual death – we create a lot of fear around these natural processes \[7-10\]. Thinking of oneself as a "mortal, sensitive form" is what causes fear \[10\].
\*   \*\*Fear is a primal, conditioned emotion\*\*: Anger, greed, sexual desire, and fear are primal emotions, part of the human and mammalian condition \[2\]. They are natural to this sensitive realm we live in, where survival, insecurity, and the desire for safety and certitude are paramount \[5, 11\].
\*   \*\*Fear is created by ignorance and attachment\*\*:
    \*   Fear arises from \*\*not knowing or not understanding\*\* \[8, 12\]. Life can be "built on fear just through this ignorance of our true nature" \[7\].
    \*   \*\*Attachment to memories, perceptions, views, and opinions\*\* creates fear \[1, 12\].
    \*   \*\*The ego and self-view (sakkayaditti)\*\* contribute significantly to fear, as the ego is constantly threatened by potential injury, humiliation, or failure \[10, 13\]. It seeks security and happiness by wanting the world to conform to its desires \[14\].
    \*   \*\*Emotional habits and tendencies to resist or control\*\* based on fear, ignorance, and desire perpetuate suffering \[15, 16\].

\*\*2. The Problem with Common Reactions to Fear:\*\*
\*   \*\*Trying to get rid of fear is often counterproductive\*\*: People generally try to avoid or suppress fear \[12, 17\]. However, Ajahn Sumedho emphasizes that "annihilation takes you to confusion and suppression and fear" \[18\]. Trying to "conquer" fear is a "self-scenario" driven by ego, not true liberation \[19\].
\*   \*\*Suppression and denial don't work\*\*: When one tries to make oneself unafraid by suppressing fear, it's merely a pretense, and the fear remains \[17\]. Repressing or denying emotions keeps one from learning from them \[20, 21\].
\*   \*\*Distraction offers only temporary relief\*\*: Distracting oneself with activities is a common way to "get rid of fear," but it doesn't address the underlying issue \[22\].
\*   \*\*Thinking and analyzing perpetuates fear\*\*: Endlessly analyzing "why" one has fears or irrational moods reinforces the delusion of a self and makes things more complicated \[23\]. Worry, anticipation, hope, and speculation are mental functions "produced in the present about the future" \[4\]. Over-thinking can cause one's "brain into a real twist" \[24\].
\*   \*\*Idealism and external validation\*\*: Wanting to appear brave or constantly seeking affirmations from others prevents facing fear directly \[25, 26\]. Projecting ideals (e.g., saints who are never angry or afraid) onto oneself or others leads to judgment and further suffering \[11\].

\*\*3. The Path to Fearlessness and Liberation from Fear:\*\*
\*   \*\*Shift from "I am afraid" to "fear is like this"\*\*: Instead of personalizing fear and saying "I'm afraid" or "I'm frightened," recognize that "fear is like this" \[7, 17, 22\]. This is a crucial shift in perspective \[22, 27\].
\*   \*\*Welcome and accept fear\*\*: Ajahn Sumedho strongly advises welcoming fear rather than feeling aversion toward it \[12\]. This means allowing fear to be conscious, feeling its physical manifestations (e.g., in the chest or stomach), and not judging it or making a problem about it \[28-30\].
\*   \*\*Be the knower/witness of fear\*\*: The fundamental practice is to be the "knower" or "witness" of fear, not the frightened person \[7, 26, 31\]. This awareness itself is not fear; it is clear and unconditioned \[32, 33\].
\*   \*\*Trust in awareness as refuge\*\*: \*\*Ultimate safety is found in awareness\*\*, which is a pure, unconditioned state, distinct from thoughts, emotions, and personal identity \[1, 22, 34, 35\]. This awareness is always present and can be trusted, even in the midst of danger or impossible situations \[36, 37\].
    \*   \*\*The "sound of silence"\*\* serves as a direct and immediate reference point for this pure, unbound awareness, helping to stop the thinking mind and provide a stable ground for observation \[36, 38-43\].
\*   \*\*Discernment (Pañña)\*\*: Develop the wisdom faculty to discern the nature of conditions without judgment \[44, 45\]. This means recognizing that fear, like all conditioned phenomena, arises and ceases \[9, 30, 46\].
\*   \*\*Non-attachment\*\*: Realizing non-attachment brings peace and is the essence of Nibbana \[13, 47-49\]. By witnessing fear without grasping or clinging to it, one disempowers it \[50\]. When attachment to desire (the cause of suffering) is relinquished, suffering ceases, and fear no longer has power \[51, 52\].
\*   \*\*Embracing life's fullness\*\*: Instead of running away from unpleasantness, one learns to embrace and understand suffering in all its forms (pain, loss, aging, sickness, criticism) \[21, 53-55\]. This willingness to be fully sensitive, to feel everything without picking and choosing, leads to fearlessness \[21, 25\].
\*   \*\*Death as a gateway\*\*: Contemplating cessation and the impermanence of the body and emotions allows for the realization that "we don't really die" in an ultimate sense \[8\]. This transforms death into a "door to the deathless" rather than something to be feared \[56\]. Ajahn Chah's challenging question, "Did you come here to die?" emphasizes confronting the impermanence of the self \[57\].
\*   \*\*Letting go of self-identity\*\*: The self-view (sakkayaditti) creates the illusion of being a limited, vulnerable person \[10, 58\]. Letting go of these illusions, recognizing that "whatever you think you are, you're not that," leads to liberation \[59-61\]. Not having a self is ultimately a relief, freeing one from the burden of ego and constant worry \[61\].

In essence, Ajahn Sumedho teaches that \*\*fear is a powerful, natural, but ultimately impermanent condition arising from ignorance and attachment to a perceived self.\*\* By cultivating a profound, non-judgmental awareness and trusting in its unconditioned nature, one can witness fear without being overwhelmed by it, leading to a state of \*\*fearlessness and inner peace that transcends the changing conditions of life\*\* \[7, 22, 25, 62\].
