# Ajahn Sumedho: Understanding Suffering as a Noble Truth

Based on the sources, Suffering (Dukkha) holds a central and foundational position in Ajahn Sumedho's teachings. It is presented not merely as a negative experience, but as a \*\*universal reality\*\* that serves as the \*\*starting point and primary subject of investigation\*\* on the Buddhist path to liberation.

Here's a breakdown of what the sources say about Suffering (Dukkha):

1.  \*\*Suffering as a Universal and Ordinary Reality:\*\*
    \*   The Buddha's first sermon was about \*\*suffering (Dukkha)\*\*, a reality that is \*\*so common, so ordinary, so now, and yet so everpresent\*\* in every human being and creature \[1\].
    \*   It is described as the \*\*existential anguish\*\* of our human condition \[2\].
    \*   Suffering is the \*\*common bond of humanity\*\*, something everyone can recognize in their lives, regardless of wealth, gender, race, nationality, or religion \[1-3\].
    \*   Examples of Dukkha include wanting something we don't have, not wanting what we have, fears, anxieties, sickness, old age, death, sorrows, and grief \[2\].
    \*   The nature of this realm (Samsara) is described as being like this: \*\*problematic, endless, a rising, ceasing, changing conditions\*\* \[4\]. Dukkha itself implies \*\*change, unsatisfactoriness, lacking, incompleteness, and insecurity\*\* \[3, 5\].

2.  \*\*The Nobility of Suffering and the Starting Point of the Path:\*\*
    \*   The Buddha took the reality of suffering and placed it in the category of a \*\*Noble Truth\*\* \[1, 6\].
    \*   To a Western mind, this can seem mystifying, as the usual approach is to try to \*\*get rid of or escape suffering\*\* \[1\].
    \*   However, the \*\*nobility of suffering is to understand it\*\* \[1, 2, 5-11\]. The aim is not to dismiss, run away from, destroy, reject, or deny suffering, but to \*\*understand it\*\* \[1\].
    \*   The Four Noble Truths begin with Dukkha precisely because it is the most \*\*ordinary common experience\*\* to everyone \[7\]. It is something that \*\*we must accept before we can experience the other truths\*\* \[7\].
    \*   The teaching is based on the common experience of suffering; by \*\*investigating that, we realize ultimate truth\*\* \[12\].

3.  \*\*Suffering We Create vs. Inherent Unpleasantness:\*\*
    \*   While experiences like old age, sickness, pain, loss, and frustration are part of this realm and unavoidable \[13-15\], the suffering primarily focused on is the \*\*suffering we create around these experiences\*\* \[2, 13-18\].
    \*   This created suffering arises from our reactions and interpretations, often influenced by our conditioning and ignorance \[2, 5, 16, 18-20\].
    \*   Resisting, fighting, or not liking what we experience \*\*creates suffering\*\* \[16, 17\]. Trying to run away from unpleasant things only makes life more difficult \[21\].
    \*   Creating "Dukkha upon Dukkha" means adding mental suffering (like resentment or resistance) to the natural unpleasantness of a situation \[7\].

4.  \*\*The Cause of Suffering (Samudaya):\*\*
    \*   The Second Noble Truth is the \*\*origin (Samudaya)\*\* or beginning to suffering \[22\]. It arises; it's not ultimate or absolute \[22, 23\].
    \*   The cause of suffering is found in \*\*our own mind\*\* \[5, 22\].
    \*   It is \*\*attachment to desire\*\*, not desire itself, that is the cause of suffering \[8, 12, 24, 25\]. When we attach to desires, we are caught in this suffering \[8, 26\].
    \*   This \*\*grasping at desire\*\* is identified as the problem \[22\].
    \*   Ignorance (Avijja) and self-view (Sakaya Ditti) are fundamental to creating suffering and attachment \[2, 5, 10, 13, 18-20, 27-30\]. The \*\*basic sense of a self that's created out of ignorance and the grasping that operates from that position is the cause of suffering\*\* \[27\].
    \*   The sense of "I am" is how suffering is generally interpreted, relating everything to a personal, separate self \[15, 31\]. Blaming others or oneself for suffering is part of this ignorance and self-view \[2, 10, 18\].

5.  \*\*The Cessation of Suffering (Nirodha):\*\*
    \*   The Third Noble Truth is the \*\*cessation of suffering (Dukkha Nirodha Sacca)\*\* \[12, 14, 23, 24, 32, 33\].
    \*   This cessation is \*\*realized by laying aside, letting go of, or abandoning desire\*\* \[10, 12, 22, 24, 32-36\].
    \*   Letting go is \*\*not getting rid of or throwing away\*\*, but allowing things to go, allowing them to be \[21, 23, 24, 37\]. Trying to get rid of suffering or desire is often another form of desire (Wipahava Dunha) and creates more suffering \[23-26, 35\].
    \*   When we let go, suffering ceases, and we realize this cessation \[23, 24\]. This end of suffering is \*\*always here\*\*, in the Jitta (heart/mind) \[34\]. Reflective awareness helps us observe \*when\* there is suffering and \*when\* there is none \[9, 14, 23\].
    \*   Ultimately, suffering is described as being based on illusions, and through investigation, understanding, and letting go of the illusion, one realizes \*\*non-suffering\*\* \[9\].

6.  \*\*The Path to the Cessation of Suffering:\*\*
    \*   The Fourth Noble Truth is the \*\*way or path of non-suffering\*\* (the Eightfold Path) \[8, 12, 33\].
    \*   This path should be \*\*cultivated or developed\*\* \[33\].

7.  \*\*The Role of Awareness (Sati) and Reflection:\*\*
    \*   Mindfulness or Awareness (Sati) is presented as the \*\*only way we can deal with worldly conditions\*\* and our experience of suffering \[38\].
    \*   It allows us to \*\*see suffering as it is\*\* \[2, 4, 15\], without judging or criticizing it \[17, 21, 39, 40\].
    \*   The practice is to \*\*be the observer of Dukkha rather than the owner\*\* \[39\]. Instead of saying "I am suffering," we can reflect "There is this suffering" or "There is this feeling of..." \[15, 31\]. This takes the personal identification out \[15\].
    \*   Through awareness, we can \*\*recognize the suffering that we create\*\* through resistance, grasping, aversion, stubbornness, conceit, or identifying with the self-view \[16, 17\].
    \*   Awareness allows us to see that the \*\*knower, that which is aware of suffering, is not suffering\*\* \[20, 40, 41\]. It is distinct from the conditioned phenomena of suffering \[20, 41\].
    \*   We use the Four Noble Truths as a \*\*reflective form\*\* and \*\*tool\*\* to contemplate our experience of suffering and its causes \[3, 6, 10, 11, 26, 33, 35, 40\]. This reflection is not just intellectual analysis but a \*\*direct investigation (Yonisomanasikara)\*\* of what is happening in the present moment \[3, 11, 12, 20, 35, 40, 42, 43\].
    \*   \*\*Understanding suffering\*\* through this awareness allows us to investigate its causes, let go of the attachment to desire, and realize cessation \[9, 10, 12, 33\].

In summary, Ajahn Sumedho's teachings portray suffering not as a problem to be eliminated or escaped from, but as a \*\*noble truth to be deeply understood through the cultivation of awareness\*\*. By observing suffering directly, recognizing how we create it through attachment and ignorance, and letting go of that grasping, we realize its cessation and walk the path of non-suffering. The core Buddhist teachings and practices serve as skillful means to guide this process of understanding and liberation from suffering.
