# Paths to Purification: Understanding Defilements and Liberation

According to the sources, a monk with destroyed taints (defilements) who has completed the holy life and sees nothing further to be done is dedicated to renunciation, solitude, and non-harming not merely by faith, adherence to rules, or seeking gain, honor, and fame. Instead, this dedication stems from being free from lust, hatred, and delusion through their destruction. It is this absence of defilements that fuels their dedication to the destruction of craving, clinging, and confusion. Even when faced with strong sensory inputs (forms, sounds, odors, tastes, tactile objects, and mind objects), their liberated mind remains untinged, firm, and imperturbable, allowing them to contemplate the transient nature of these phenomena.

The sources highlight a number of imperfections that defile the mind and act as obstacles to spiritual development. These include covetousness and unrighteous greed, ill will, anger, revenge, contempt, a domineering attitude, envy, avarice, deceit, fraud, obstinacy, presumption, conceit, arrogance, vanity, and negligence. Furthermore, sensual lust, ill will, sloth and torpor, and restlessness and remorse are described as hindrances that can overwhelm the mind and prevent clear seeing and understanding of what is truly beneficial for oneself and others. These defilements, when present, can make the mind impure, leading to unhappy destinations.

Becoming is explained as a process where karma serves as the field, consciousness is the seed, and craving is the moisture. For beings hindered by ignorance and fettered by craving, this process leads to consciousness becoming established in various realms (lower, intermediate, or lofty), thus resulting in re-becoming in the future. Karma itself is declared to be volition (intention), and it is through contact that karma originates. The diversity of karma leads to rebirth in different realms, from hells and animal realms to human and heavenly realms. The outcome of karma can manifest in this life, the next life, or subsequent future lives. The cessation of karma comes about through the cessation of contact, and the Noble Eightfold Path is the way leading to this cessation.

The concept of "divine messengers" (such as infancy, sickness, old age, punishment, and death) serves as reminders of impermanence, suffering, and the consequences of unwholesome actions. The sources emphasize that if a person, when confronted with these messengers, does not reflect on their own mortality and susceptibility to suffering and subsequently fail to do good by body, speech, and mind due to negligence, they will face the results of their evil actions. King Yama's questioning of those who have engaged in misconduct underscores the principle of personal responsibility, stating that evil actions are done by oneself and their results will be experienced by oneself alone.

The development of concentration is akin to the purification of gold. Just as gross impurities (earth, sand, etc.), moderate impurities (fine grit, coarse sand), and subtle impurities (fine dust, black dust) must be removed from gold through washing and smelting, so too must gross impurities (bad conduct), moderate impurities (sensual thoughts, ill will, violent thoughts), and subtle impurities (thoughts about relatives, home country, reputation, and even higher mental states) be abandoned by a monk training in higher mind. Initially, concentration might be maintained by strenuous suppression of defilements. However, through continued development, the mind becomes inwardly steadied, composed, unified, and concentrated, resulting in calm and refined concentration that has attained full tranquility and mental unification without strenuous suppression. This refined state of mind then becomes pliant, workable, and bright, enabling various spiritual attainments.

Mindfulness and clear comprehension are presented as essential for cultivating wholesome states and abandoning unwholesome ones. A monk should dwell mindful and clearly comprehending, observing body, feelings, mind, and phenomena internally and externally. Clear comprehension involves understanding feelings, thoughts, and perceptions as they arise, remain present, and pass away, without annihilating them. A "wise, competent, skillful" practitioner is one who picks up the "sign of their own mind," meaning they are attentive to the results of their practice and understand where their meditation is going and what needs to be developed or relinquished. This mindful and clear awareness, when grounded in purified virtue and right view, provides a foundation for developing wholesome states and expecting growth rather than decline.

The path to liberation involves understanding karma, its origin, diversity, outcome, and cessation, as well as the Noble Eightfold Path. Ultimately, the holy life is lived with Nibbana as its ground, destination, and final goal. The sources distinguish between worldly and unworldly forms of joy, happiness, equanimity, and freedom. Worldly forms are tied to sensual pleasures and desires, which are seen as impermanent, hollow, false, and deceptive, belonging to Mara's realm and leading to suffering. Unworldly joy and happiness arise from seclusion from sensual desires and unwholesome states, as experienced in meditative absorptions (jhanas). A still greater unworldly joy, happiness, equanimity, and freedom arise when a taint-free monk looks upon their mind freed from greed, hatred, and delusion. The destruction of these defilements is the ultimate liberation from suffering.

The five "wildernesses" in the heart that a practitioner must abandon are: doubt about the Buddha, doubt about the Dharma (teachings), doubt about the Sangha (community), doubt about the training, and being angry and displeased with companions in the holy life. These hinder inclination towards ardor, devotion, perseverance, and striving. The five "shackles" in the heart that must be severed are: lust for sensual pleasures, lust for one's own body, lust for outer forms and external things, indulging in excessive eating, sleeping, lolling, and drowsing, and living the holy life aspiring to some order of gods (seeking rebirth in heavenly realms). Severing these shackles is crucial for the mind to incline towards ardor and striving for liberation.
