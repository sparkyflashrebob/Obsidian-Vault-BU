GENERAL GUIDELINES: LEADING MEDITATIONS 

Create a safe and protected space that is without unnecessary distraction.This might mean asking people to turn off cell phones, closing doors, and putting up signs at an entrance letting people know that a meditation is going on. 

Before starting the meditation, tell them what you will be doing-e.g., a six-minute guided meditation to help you establish awareness of breath. 
Invite them to find a meditation posture that allows them to be comfortable and at ease. 
Give them choices sitting on a cushion on the floor, sitting on a bench or the floor, in a chair, and (if appropriate or needed) standing or lying down. 

Have them close their eyes ifthey are comfortable, or alternately, have them slightly open, with downward gaze. Again, it is wise to give choices. 

For many meditations, it's helpful to begin by inviting them to sense their body and to relax areas of tightness and tension where it's easy to do so. 

Guide their attention to the subject ofthe meditation, whether body, breath, lovingkindness, etc. 

Be attentive to your pacing, speaking slowly and pausing regularly. Allow them ample time to open to their own experience, but not so much time (especially for beginners) that their minds wil drift and lose track ofthe instructions. 

Be attentive to the tone and quality of your voice. Speak from presence and heart in a soft, clear, connected voice. 

Make your language in the present moment: "Sensing your belly moving in the palm of your hand noticing how thoughts arise and pass like clouds 

For many teachers, it increases safety to make language invitational rather than directive: "You might explore bringing your attention inside your hands, feeling sensations from the inside out," versus, "Bring your attention inside your hands and feel sensations from the inside out." 

Where appropriate, you might deepen engagement by asking questions: "Where is it easiest to feel your breath?" "What do you notice when you soften your hands? Tingling? Vibration? Pulsing? Warmth?" 

Stay in touch with yourself AND with them throughout the meditation: inner attention that is mindful of your own sensations, etc. AND outer attention that is tracking how they are doing (i.e., restlessness, agitation, sleepiness, collectedness, calm). 

Use phrases that evoke the spirit of care, tending love and kindness and compassion e.g., 'Bring a gentle and kind attention to any areas thatfeel unpleasant or difficult." 

Speak to them periodically during the silent stretches of practice just to remind them gently to reconnect with what is here now. "You might notice what is happening right now," or, Just this breath and now this one " 

Give a one- to two-minute notice, e.g., "Soon we will be ending," or, "Just a couple minutes more." 

Find a simple way to end. Include a bell if you like. 

Check in with them afterwards with the intention of normalizing difficulty, addressing confusion, and encouraging and affirming their process.
