MMR#60 The Predicting Brain
I came across an article that appears in the June edition of Mindful Magazine that just came out
about on how Our Brain Predicts (Almost ) Everything We Do. It’s an excerpt from a book
called, “Seven and a Half Lessons About the Brain” by Lisa Feldman Barrett. Lisa is currently
one of the leading researchers in psychology and neuroscience.
The article itself fascinated me with its detail into how the mind works.

● Particularly with processes that are, for the most part and a certain extent, out of
our range of direct control.
● So I want to thank her and neuroscience for shining the light on our practice. For
adding to this practice of insight - to awakening.

She mentions that we are continually receiving sense data from the outside world via our eyes,
ears, nose and other sense organs. And that this data does not come in the form of meaningful
sights, sounds, smells, and other sensations that we experience, that it is a barrage of light
waves, chemicals, and changes in air pressure with no inherent significance. The brain's most
important job is to control our body so that we can stay alive and well. So how does it take this
sea of ambiguous information and figure out what’s the best response? It uses an additional
source of information: memory.
Our brains can draw upon our life time of experiences - things that have happened to us
personally and things that we have learned from friends, teachers, books, including our
conditioning from our families, community and society as a whole. Almost instantly our brain
reconstructs bits and pieces of our past experience to infer the meaning of the sense data and
guess what to do about it.
Our past experiences include not only what happened externally, in the world, but also inside
our bodies. Was our heart beating quickly? Were our breathing heavily?
Your brain asks itself in every moment, figuratively
speaking, The last time I encountered a similar
situation, when my body was in a similar state, what
did I do next?
The answer need not be a perfect match, just close enough for the brain to provide an
appropriate plan of action that helps us survive.
Not only that. It also creates what she calls “high-fidelity experiences” out of the raw data.
Again, she says the brain does this by “recreating the past from memory by asking itself, “The
last time I encountered a similar situation, when my body was in a similar state and was
preparing an action similar to this one, what did I see next? What did I feel next? The answer

becomes your experience. In other words, our brain combines information from outside and
inside our head to produce everything we see, hear, smell, taste, and feel.hear, smell, taste, and
feel.

But then she asks us to see what happens when she gives our brain some more information.

When we look back at the line drawings, we see familiar objects instead of lines and blobs. Our
brains assembled memories from bits and pieces of past experience to go beyond the visual
data in front of us and make meaning. Objects that we may have not seen before now leap
from the page.
The kicker and one of the things I’d like to explore as we go on is - the line and blobs have not
changed - we have!!!
The brain actively constructs our experience from our sensory organs but we do not. We sense
with our brain.
Same thing happens with what we feel, our aches and pains and other inner sensations of
different combinations of what is actually happening with our lungs and heart and gut and
muscles and so on. Our brains also add information about our past experiences to guess what
those sensations mean. She gives the example of “when people haven’t slept enough and are
fatigued or low energy, they may feel hungry (because they’ve been hungry before when their
energy was low) and may think that a quick snack will boost their energy. In fact, they’re just
tired from lack of sleep.”

She also brings up us having had the experience where the information inside our head
triumphs over the data from the outside world. Seeing a face in the crowd of someone you think
you know and realizing it is a different person, or feeling sensations in your pocket and thinking
it might be your phone when it wasn’t. Songs playing in our heads.
Neuroscientists call our day-to-day experience a carefully controlled hallucination - one that
creates and guides all of our actions.
But there is more - she says that this whole process of construction in our head happens
predictively. Science is fairly certain that the brain begins to sense the moment-to-moment
changes in our experience before those light waves, chemicals and other sense data hit our
brains.
She asks us to think about the last time we were thirsty and drank a glass of water. Within
seconds we probably felt less thirsty. In fact it takes about 20 minutes for the water to reach our
bloodstream. It can’t possibly quench our thirst in seconds. So what did? Prediction. As the
brain plans and executes the plan to drink water, it simultaneously anticipates the
consequences of that - causing us to feel less thirsty before the water has reached our organs.
These predictions are not linear, the brain is actually creating a series of predictions and
probabilities based on this data and makes its best guess of which prediction to choose. Often,
it’s the prediction that best matches the incoming sense data, but not always. Either way, the
winning prediction becomes our actions and our sensory experience.
She says that “What happens next still astounds me, even as a neuroscientist. If your brain has
predicted well, then your neurons are already firing in a pattern that matches the incoming
sense data. That means this sense data itself has no further use beyond confirming your brain’s
predictions. What you see, hear, smell, and taste in the world and feel in your body in that
moment are completely constructed in your head. By prediction, your brain has efficiently
prepared you to act. When your predicting brain is right, it creates your reality. When it’s wrong,
it still creates your reality, and hopefully it learns from its mistakes: Your brain incorporates the
prediction errors and updates its predictions, so it can predict better next time around.”
Even more astonishing she says that all of this predicting happens backwards from the way we
experience it. We seem to sense first and then act second but in our brains sensing actually
come 2nd. Our brains are wired to prepare for action first. Our brains are wired to initiate
actions before we are aware of them. Our actions are under control of memory and our
environment. So this begs the question, we feel that we are doing things by choice, right?
Does this mean that we have no free will? Who is responsible for our actions? We are not
really here to debate this as it has been debated for a long time. We will not clear this all up.
She suggested that we look at a piece of the puzzle that is often ignored. Despite these facts,
she believes we have more control over our actions and experiences than we might think and

more responsibility than we might want. Our predictions do not come out of nowhere. Our past
actions and memories inform us in the now. If we could magically reach back in time and
change our past our brains would predict differently and we would experience the world
differently as a result.
We cannot change the past but with some effort we can change how our brains predict in the
future. We can invest a little time and energy to learn new ideas, to evaluate what we think we
know and how we know it. We can curate new experiences and means of behavior. The idea is
that everything we learn today seeds our brain to predict differently tomorrow.
If we embrace this possibility how might our lives change? How about the world?
Turning back to our practice.
How might we use our power of observation, this awareness of our direct experience in ways
that can benefit us? How can we become more aware of the information that our brains use to
make this predictive reality? How can we become more intimately aware of our sense
experience and aware of the results of our actions?
We might want to reflect on this in terms of our the views and opinions, our beliefs, our priorities,
our intentions, and the aspirations we hold. How might we look at them differently based on our
past experience?
