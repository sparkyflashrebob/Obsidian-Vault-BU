MMR#61 - Sankharas
This week I found myself intrigued by Ajahn Sumedho’s talk on 
[[Asking Ourselves Questions]]
which is actually an extensive expose on sankharas.

[[Saṅkhāra]] - is something that that is put together - formed - a formation -
thru [[causes and conditions]] 
- things that are conditioned 
- which includes everything that appears in the mind. 

Saṅkhāra’s are:
- things that are made up, 
- prepared, 
- concocted by way of intention 
- That come and go. 
- That have no real substance to them. 
- That cannot be possessed. 
- Cannot be considered ours 
- not who we are.

And that when we do try to take hold - to grasp - to cling to - Is a cause of our suffering.
And when we see clearly and understand this - that we let go and turn to what is not
conditioned, not created. That is our true refuge. This knowing
So what is are these sankharas and why is this so important to understanding and to our
freedom?
I would like to read a large portion of his talk in the hopes of solidifying and clarifying this. In a
couple of places I have slightly modified it to make it more personal to us. But I ask that you try
not to take anything of what you are about to hear personally, for, if you do, you may miss the
point that this teaching is pointing to. Nothing here is intended to create more suffering but it is
intended to point to our realizing the ending of suffering.
He begins:
“Ask yourself the question:
What is the nature of existence?
What is the universe?
Who am I?
Why was I born?
How should I live my life?
What happens when I die?
These are the ultimate questions.
And the answers? (pause)
All we can know is it is a present moment. (pause)
We get an answer to the question, “Who am I - I am Sumedho Bhikkhu. And we see that is a
conceptualization. It’s anicca, dukkha, anatta.
In our minds we see, “Who am I?” and the answer? I am Sumedho Bhikkhu! Or XXXXX
Why was I born? I was born to be in harmony with the ultimate truth, to be one with nature, or
whatever clever answers you can think up to the ultimate question.
But seeing that the nature of existence is as it really is - we talk about Saṅkhāra’s, compounded
things, conditioning or phenomena. All compounded things -all Saṅkhāra’s are impermanent.

They are imperfect, they're not self, they're not me, they're not mine. They're annicha, anatta,
dukkha.
All compounded things - what we mean by that? All things that begin and end, arise and fall,
come and go, are born and die. Such as our bodies. They are Saṅkhāra’s.
Everybody's body is a Saṅkhāra. When we look at our own body with our physical eye, eye
consciousness it is a sankhara. When we look at the eye - the organ it is a Sankhara.
When we look at somebody else's body - it is a Sankhara. All sense consciousness - these are
all compounded things. Consciousness is a Sankara.
Memory, perceptions, conception, all thoughts, good and evil, all the heavenly realms and all the
hellish ones. All the concepts we have. England is a Sankhara. France, Russia, America.
These are all Sankharas. Male and female, young and old. Andy McGuire is a Sankhara. We
are all Sankharas. All compounded. We are all born and all die. Begin and end. Come and go.
Arise and fall.
So when we recognize the significance of this and understand sankharas - carrying it to its
furthest extremes. All the stars in the skies, when you look up at night and you see the stars
and the moon - the eye that sees - the consciousness that arises - the objects themselves - the
stars and moon - space - all are Sankharas.
So by knowing one sankhara we can know the nature of all sankharas - that they are conditions
that come and go. The thoughts, perceptions, feelings, sensations, consciousness - all these
are sankharas. They have the characteristics of anicca, dukkha, anatta! Then we understand
that the whole universe is a Sankhara. Concepts about the universe are sankharas.
Concepts about anything and everything - knowing - not knowing - sense - nonsense
Sanity - insanity.
The ultimate questions are sankharas. So it leaves us nothing to depend on!!! You see things
just as they are. Knowing the nature of one compounded thing we know the nature of all
compounded things. Like knowing one grain of sand we can know what a million grains of sand
are like.
So in our practice and meditation we observe this particular thing that we have within us. This
microcosmic universe we call body and mind. Namarupa. And through seeing that these too
are conditioned we can get beyond it.
We think of the universe as something outside - out there - like a scientist - as an object.
That's another Sankara - that's a thought, conception. Memories, all the memories we have, all
the perceptions we have, they’re sankharas. Keep going on like this, seeing everything just in
his way, a simplification of all the complexities. Seeing everything and nothing as anicca,
dukkha, anatta! Seeing out own conceptions about meditation, about Buddhism, about practice,
about methods, about yourself, about people you live with.

He asks us: Where is your mother right now? In that very doubt of not knowing for sure - that
very image - that very memory - the perception that comes is a sankhara. It's not your mother.
Then you go home and you look right directly at your mother with your physical eye and you
say, ah - that's my mother, you're my mother. That’s also a sankhara. It's consciousness of a
physical object. It’s impermanent. And then you think about your concept of mother and then
you start thinking mother means this - means that. Mother did this. Mother was that way. On
and on like this. Memory of history, of past experience, is pleasant, and unpleasant. And so we
create mothers in our minds out of all these perceptions, imaginations.
When, in actuality, all of these are the five Khandas in their natural state of changing. Form,
feelings, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness
We take all this complexity out of the universe by seeing it in a simple way. When we see the
universe as a simple thing then it doesn't seem so hopeless.
Are there any exceptions to this rule? Can we find anything that isn't a sankhara? Think, think of
something that isn't a sankhara. That is not compounded. In all the theories and all the thoughts
and all these concepts that you might form - those are sankharas. So you begin to see the
limitations of conceptualization.
All thinking, all thought is not profound - even this talk. Thinking profound thoughts, great
intellects thinking profound thoughts. Complex theories about existence. Trying to think that they
are somehow profound and deep. But they're only thought. Even the most profound complex
theory of - philosophy of life - is only a superficial thing. It’s a sankhara. It begins and ends. It’s
anicca, dukkha, anatta! It has no real substance - it has no inner core.
In this way we can empty out the accumulation of years of conceptualization, biases, prejudices,
opinions, fears, worry, doubt, all our hang-ups and our neuroses. We need just be. We can see
this as superficial clouds, phantoms, soap bubbles, things that have no real essence. Our most
sacred and cherished images of ourselves as being a deep sensitive person, gifted or whatever,
if we like ourselves or we don't like ourselves, or we think of ourselves as negative, hopelessly
incapable failures, can't do anything right. These are sankharas - superficial - with no essence,
no core.
You see some people suffering - you look around you - look at people in their state of agony, the
state of anguish and despair. You begin to see it's only because they're ignorant of their
suffering. Not seeing it as superficial. They identify with the surface, with the floating clouds, the
phantoms, the mist. They clutch at foam, the bubbles. All their desires, wonderful, well analyzed
conceptions of themselves. Their problems. We go on endlessly about how we are this way
because of this and that way because of that.
But in the state of awareness, what is there? A passing cloud, a sankhara just floating by. One
needn't attach to it, one needn't reject. It's like the clouds in the sky. You can watch them float
by without getting involved in their movement. The thing with all your thoughts, views, opinions,

the body you have. Consciousness comes and goes to the eye, the ear, the nose, the time, the
body, the mind, memories, changing. And through this practice of seeing things just as they are,
you become non-attached or you are one who does not attach to any of these superficial
sankharas.
Being wise means that you know the nature of ignorance and you're not identified with it
anymore. There's no more doubt. Is this ignorant thing really me? Is this great fear, this great
ultimate terror, this great profound whatever it is that you think, is it your ultimate real self? This
too is only a conception. It’s a sankhara floating by.
Well how do you explain human suffering in this way? What about all the people that have been
murdered or brutally treated victims of circumstances - all kinds of human misery one can see
around oneself - one can remember all kinds of things that have happened in the past -wars and
persecutions. Not taking away these things that have caused great suffering. But recognizing
that these are also sankharas. Changing sometimes high, sometimes low, good and bad - war
and peace.
But with awareness we get beyond these dualisms. Mindfulness is the path to immortality. It is
that that you can't conceive. Immortality. In the moment you try to conceive it you're creating
another concept, another soap bubble, another phantom arises in your mind. But if you can just
watch that movement with a calm, composed mind and heart - then you're being wise right now.
The minute you indulge in your doubts, your joys, your fears - then you're going to get pulled
back into the samsara and suffer some kind of disappointment, despair, anguish, lamentation
and sorrow.
The question: You mean my mother is only a soap bubble? You're trying to think that my mother
doesn't really exist? And that's - thinking. It is a conceptualization. But surely how can you just
dismiss the whole universe as a soap bubble. And that's doubting, not being sure, feeling angry,
worried, or fearful, feeling aversion. Whatever you want to call it.
And maybe we want some, maybe, comforting answers - to explain that, well, my mother really
does exist in this sense but in others they don't - and go into intellectual explanations of mothers
and the universe and what not. And that's just thinking again. Trying to make ourselves
understand and feel comfortable because we want to understand symbols rather than let go of
all that symbolization and dwell in that state of unbounded, unrestricted freedom, balance, and
sanity.
We cherish certain concepts about the universe, about mothers and fathers, about ourselves,
about husband and wives, the nature of existence. And when those concepts are threatened -
somebody says it's only in delusion - we feel upset. But that feeling upset is a sankhara. Fear.
Doubt. Just keep that constant reflection. Looking at closely - rather than trying to figure out and
rationalize, justify, explain. Both doubting and certainty are sankharas.
------

So this conditionality - all the ways that we think about the world. The things we grasp onto as
our lives. These things that are the subject of our suffering. When we realize that they are
impermanent, not something we can place our trust in, cannot possess, and a cause of our
suffering and even we do try to grasp them, but we are mindful - then that is our way out - our
way towards freedom, towards the ending of grasping, towards cessation, towards peace.

Mindfulness is getting beyond the conditioning of the mind - to the true nature of the mind
The purity of knowing before conditions arise before you become anything.
In this way we achieve peace
To achieve peace within a person, the Buddhist approach is to observe and reflect upon the
conditions externally and internally within us, and then decide on the most appropriate course of
action as response to the outer and inner environments.
