This comes up another way in the pronouncement that the ENTIRE WORLD IS EMPTY  
	Consider this statement:
> 	The eye is empty; it is empty of being permanent, of being perpetual, and of having an unchanging nature, and it is empty of what belongs to a self. Why is that? That is its intrinsic nature. Forms ... eye-consciousness ... eye-contact ... (the) feeling arisen in dependence on eye-contact that is painful, pleasant, or neutral, is also empty; it is empty of being permanent, of being perpetual, and of having an unchanging nature, and it is empty of what belongs to a self. Why is that? That is its intrinsic nature. (Likewise) The ear ... the nose ... the tongue ... the body ... the mind are also like that. This is [the implication] of the saying: “The world is empty.”

Analayo. Compassion and Emptiness in Early Buddhist Meditation . Windhorse Publications Ltd. Kindle Edition. 

So the World is Empty in the sense in that the six senses are empty of self and of anything that could belong to a self.
	In early Buddhist thought the qualification of being empty of a self applies to everything.
	Once the six senses and their objects are mentioned, there is nothing left for anything to be excluded from emptiness.
		All things,
		all phenomena,
		all aspects of experience 

This same idea also applies to the doctrinal equivalent to emptiness 
	which is the teaching of not-self.

There are a series of helpful similes on the empty and not-self nature of the whole world which approach this from the perspective of the five aggregates.

> It is just as a drifting collection of foam, following a great wave on the river Gaṅges, which a clear-sighted person carefully examines, [attends to], and analyses. When carefully examining, [attending to], and analysing it, [the clear-sighted person finds that] there is nothing in it, nothing stable, nothing substantial, it has no solidity. Why is that? It is because there is nothing solid or substantial in a collection of foam. 
> 
> In the same way one carefully examines, attends to, and analyes whatever bodily form, past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, sublime or repugnant, far or near. [When] carefully examining, attending to, and analysing it ... [one finds that] there is nothing in it, nothing stable, nothing substantial, it has no solidity; ...., it is impermanent, dukkha, empty, and not-self.5 Why is that? It is because there is nothing solid or substantial in bodily form... 
> 
> It is just as when during a great rain there are bubbles on the [surface] of water,6 arising and ceasing one after another, which a clear-sighted person carefully examines, attends to, and analyses. When carefully examining, attending to, and analysing them, [the clear-sighted person finds that] there is nothing in them, nothing stable, nothing substantial, they have no solidity. Why is that? When carefully examining, attending to, and analysing it, [the clear-sighted person finds that] there is nothing in it, nothing stable, nothing substantial, it has no solidity. Why is that? Now the issue that is problematic with these aggregates is that they provide a basis for clinging and identification
	Analayo points out that "This is what needs to be recognized in actual practice."
	Whenever there is clinging, (we can ask) which aggregate(s) are we clinging to?
	Once this is recognized then the similes I mentioned can be used as aids.
	We can deconstruct the basis for how clinging, attachment and identification thrive.

Take for instance:
BODY LIKE FOAM
	it has a lack of solidity 
	fragile - easily affected by outside circumstances
	just like the foam that is carried away by the ocean or wind
	this Body, which provided us with the illusion of being a stable basis for one's sense of identity,
	is being carried away by the current of change. 

FEELINGS are much simpler to recognize their passing nature
	here the simile equating feelings to bubbles on the waters surface when it rains 
	these bubbles come to the end when the rain is over 
	likewise feelings are merely conditioned result of various type of stimulation
	they come and they go

PERCEPTION 
Perception is where we Recognize things commited to our memories - 
	it is our accumulated knowledge and experiences, which we turn into "my" knowledge and "my" experience
		what we take as apparently objective perceptions are mostly subjective opinions and value judgements based on prior conditioning 
		according to the simile any perception is merely a mental projection comparable to a mirage 
	
(do not say) MENTAL FORMATIONS 
	Below the threshold of conscious awareness, 
		ignorance infiltrates our perceptions, 
		and from there spreads to our thoughts and views, 
		resulting in a distorted mode of understanding
	Coupled with Intention and will power, 
		and the ability to make decisions which is partnered with the feeling of being in control, 
		this can become central to one’s notion of who one is and what one does. 
	But on closer inspection this turns out these mental formations lack any essence or core, 
		comparable to a plantain tree, which also has no core. 
		Layers upon layers of conditioning make up one’s apparently so well-informed decisions. 
	When unrolled one after the other, no solid core is found. 
	No real person or self stands behind intentions and the decision-making process. Just sheaths over sheaths of conditioning accumulated from the past. 

Consciousness 
And then with consciousness, this provides the ground of mental experience 
and easily tends to be appropriated as a subtle basis for the notion of a self. 
	On examination it turns out to be just like a magical illusion. 
	The creations of such a magical illusion have no basis whatsoever in reality. 
		Whereas perception is comparable to a mirage and thereby, connected to what soemthing we can beleive to be real, 
	consciousness is so thoroughly delusive that it is like a hallucination, totally void of anything real at all. 

All five of these aggregates are central aspects of what is usually taken as "I", "me" and "mine" 
	And when comtemplated and investigated
	they are shown to be totally empty of any real substance. 
	As the similies describe
	Like foam, bubbles, a mirage, plaintain sheaths, and a magic trick. 
	That is all.

The powerful images that emerge from these passages offer themselves as aids for the contemplation of emptyness.

> Any instance of craving or attachment will in some form or another involve an obsession with the body, or a yearning for pleasure, or a holding on to one’s preconceived notions, or the expectation or hope that things will always go the way one wants them to, or to a clinging to a particular experience, or else a combination of these.
