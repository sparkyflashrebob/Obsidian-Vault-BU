Something I have not brought up in the past, but have inferred many times, is a method of
inquiry that the Buddha brought up with dealing with the hindrances over and over.
He called these “The 4 supreme efforts”. He asks us to use our mindfulness and wisdom:
1. To not to let an unwholesome thought arise which has not yet arisen. To prevent it from
arising.
2. To not to let an unwholesome thought continue which has already arisen. Which is to cut
it off as soon as it arises.
3. To make a wholesome thought arise which has not yet arisen. To cultivate good
thoughts.
4. To make a wholesome thought continue which has already arisen. To keep good
thoughts going.
Two points: the obvious part to me is this idea that these are goals are things we can keep in
mind, to be aware of. These are skillful and beneficial in themselves. In Recovery = Doing the
right next thing.
Secondly, I thought we might take a minute to silently contemplate how we might practice these
efforts.
What might these four methods of dealing with the Hindrances look like. REREAD THEM
SLOWLY. FEEDBACK?
What thought would you consider to be either greedy, hateful or deluded?
Before we leave this and go to the specifics of the RAIN practice, I would also like us to
recognize how, in the Theravada Tradition, the tradition that I am most familiar with, there are
five mental factors that counteract the five hindrances in meditation. I mention these because
these are buddhist terms that are mentioned when dealing with the Hindrances and in the path
of awakening. They can also be added to our repertoire of concepts and used as tools for
contemplation.
In reference to the Hindrances these 5 Mental Factors can be used to counteract them
There is:
1. Coarse examination (vitakka) which counteracts sloth-torpor (lethargy and drowsiness)

2. Precise investigation (vicāra) which counteracts doubt (uncertainty)
3. Well-being (pīti) which counteracts ill-will (malice)
4. Bliss (sukha) which counteracts restlessness-worry (excitation and anxiety)
5. Single-pointed attention (ekaggatā) which counteracts sensory desire
As we briefly review these again I will ask some questions and make some statements. You do
not have to answer them out loud, but the process of contemplation is a means for raising and
idea and paying attention to how it makes you feel and the thought they can generate. All we
have to do is to pay attention to this. Like clouds floating by.
So hear we go.
A course examination is like taking final. It means to use all of our knowledge in applying
wisdom to the object. In this case sloth-torpor. What might we know about the dangers of sloth
and torpor? What might counteract this? A suggestion is to think of generating energy or to raise
the idea of perceiving light. How do these two ideas feel to you? (pause)
Precise investigation is like going over something with a fine tooth comb. Looking at the
evidence. Think of doubt and then how we might investigate it. What would evidence of doubt
be? How would that feel?
Vitakka and vicara are frequently thought of as companions, as “directed thought and
evaluation”.
Well-being is bringing in good thoughts and feelings. We can contemplate this can’t we? Can
we bring well-being - thoughts for our well being to heart? What about accessing feeling good.
Do we know what that feels like? Can you experience ill-will and well-being at the same time?
Likewise with bliss. Bliss is feeling peaceful. Can we imagine ourselves being peaceful? What
does that feel like. Can we be at peace and at the same time be restless?
And finally, what might single-pointed attention be like? Focused, in the zone, extremely
interested in our practice - in what we are trying to accomplish?
These five counteracting factors arise during the first stage of concentration known as jhāna, so
you can cultivate these in your meditation. In this practice we use mindfulness to focus on an
object, usually the breath, using vitakka and vicara to guide us to ekaggata, which is
single-pointed attention to our object. As we deepen into this during meditation we can come to
a point of, what I believe to be “access concentration”. We are at the doorway. As this
continues to deepen, at some point, grace enters the picture, the dharma is revealed and we
are able to access both piti and sukha alos. It is an extremely pleasant and blissful feeling with
one pointeness born of seclusion. Eventually we talk through the one of the gateless gates and
we enter into first jhana.

Jhana is a temporary way of suppressing the Hindrances. To eradicate them, take them out by
their roots, is what means to have full enlightenment.

In the preface to Tara Branch's book on RAIN practice called “Radical Compassion” she talks
about reading an article by a hospice caregiver who had sat with many, many people during
their final days. In that article she found one phrase in particular that stayed with her. It was the
“...wish (that) the (caregiver) had the courage to live a life true to (herself).”
To honest with you, that is my wish too - to be honest!, compassionate, and caring for others - to
serve the world to the best of my abilities - without necessarily being concerned for my own
needs above others. Having the strength to grow beyond my own self-imposed limitations - what
I call “being genuine to my higher and true self.”
RAIN PRACTICE
Tara has a few web pages dedicated to RAIN that include talks on how to use RAIN and a list
detailing its use. I encourage you to visit her website yourself to gain more familiarity with the
practice. But I offer the foundations here for our benefit at this time.
The acronym RAIN is an easy-to-remember tool for bringing mindfulness and compassion, the
two wings of awakening, to emotional difficulty that might have, either as a spot check
stand-alone meditation or by going thru all of the steps by setting aside a period of
contemplation whenever challening feelings arise.
RAIN means to Recognize what is going on; Allow the experience to be there, just as it is;
Investigate with interest and care; and to Nurture with self-compassion.
So the R
Recognize What’s Going On
Recognizing means consciously acknowledging, in any given moment, the thoughts, feelings,
and behaviors that are affecting you. This can be a done with a simple mental whisper, noting
WHAT you are most aware of. You can circle anxious thoughts and guilty feelings, anger,
craving to feeling not just right, not feeling motivated, or knowing that you feel lost. Anything that
concerns you.
So what are some of the scenarios that we might think of this practice.
1. Being Demanding and Judgemental. Story of the US fleet of ships and a light house.
2. Being hard on ourselves. This trance of unworthiness. Identified with being small and
deficient.

3. Showing signs of being Restless, cause we just don't want to be with what is here.
Obvious addictive behaviors but also things like going on the internet, TV, daydreaming
or shopping. Means of getting out of ourselves.
4. Noticing how we avoid engaging others. She gave this example of someone not wanting
to look at the homeless guys eyes when she was putting money in his cup. Avoid feeling
what we feel. Noticing when we are distancing ourselves from our experience.
5. I like to simply state it as, whenever we are disturbed.
A—Allow the Experience to be There, Just as It Is
Allowing means letting the thoughts, emotions, feelings, or sensations you have recognized
simply be there, without trying to fix or avoid anything.
You might recognize fear, and allow by mentally whispering “it’s ok” or “this belongs” or “yes.”
Allowing creates a pause that makes it possible to deepen attention.
Another way of allowing might be to consciously allow what's happening by breathing and letting
be.
Recognizing and allowing is the core of Mindfulness. Noticing moment to moment without any
judgement
● What is happening right now? Close your eyes and ask right now. You might try it.
Asking the question provides you with a natural way to be intact with what is going on with you
right now Naming allows for deeper connection.
● B. Can I be with this?
If we pause and ask these two questions we will be more awake.
It is really import that we have that pause.

I—Investigate with Interest and Care. Friendliness.
To investigate, call on your natural curiosity—the desire to know truth—and direct a more
focused attention to your present experience.

You might ask yourself: What most wants attention? How am I experiencing this in my body?
What am I believing? What does this vulnerable place want from me? What does it most need?
Whatever the inquiry, your investigation will be most transformational if you step away from
conceptualizing and bring your primary attention to the felt-sense in the body. This is one of the
main reasons we can practice the 1st Foundation of Mindfulness, to be Mindful of the Body, or
to Mindfulness Friendliness.
There is a story about George Schaller, who was one of the most prominent researchers in
animal behaviour, who studied Gorillas. People were amazed by the detail in his research on
the and when asked how he did it he said, “I didn't carry a gun”. Previous researchers had
come to study the gorillas with guards and weapons. It is no wonder that their observations
were limited. How would you reach when threatened? The idea here is that we check our
attitude that we can bring to mindfulness. Are we bringing in a gun when simple curiosity and
openness will do, will actually bring us better results.
Related to this is also watching out for the Domain of thinking that can spiral our of control.
When we are suffering there is a limiting belief. COME BACK TO THE BODY. THE FELT
SENSE. We are not searching for answers.

N—Nurture with Self-Compassion
Self-compassion begins to naturally arise in the moments that you recognize you are suffering.
It comes into fullness as you intentionally nurture your inner life with self-care.
To do this, try to sense what the wounded, frightened or hurting place inside you most needs,
and then offer some gesture of active care that might address this need. Does it need a
message of reassurance? Of forgiveness? Of companionship? Of love?
Experiment and see which intentional gesture of kindness most helps to comfort, soften or open
your heart. It might be the mental whisper, I’m here with you. I’m sorry, and I love you. I love
you, and I’m listening. It’s not your fault. Trust in your goodness.
In addition to a whispered message of care, many people find healing by gently placing a hand
on the heart or cheek; or by envisioning being bathed in or embraced by warm, radiant light. If it
feels difficult to offer yourself love, bring to mind a loving being—spiritual figure, family member,
friend or pet—and imagine that being’s love and wisdom flowing into you.
After the RAIN
When you’ve completed the active steps of RAIN, it’s important to notice the quality of your own
presence and rest in that wakeful, tender space of awareness.
The fruit of RAIN is realizing that you are no longer imprisoned in or identified with any limiting
sense of self. Give yourself the gift of becoming familiar with the truth and natural freedom of
your being; it is mysterious and precious!

SNAPPING
Here is a variation of RAIN. It is an example of how you might “SNAPP” awake.
1. Stop what you’re doing for just a moment when you can tell you’re getting triggered by
something in your environment.

2. Notice with curiosity what is happening in your body and your mind. Witness and watch the
energetic play of bodily sensations and the continuous flow of thoughts and mental images as
each is born, lives, and passes on its own.
3. Allow these experiences to be just as they are, without judgment or attempts to control them.
This doesn’t mean you’re signing up for discomfort or pain; you’re choosing to recognize what’s
happening in the moment without trying to change it.
4. Penetrate uncomfortable sensations in the body with full, deep belly breaths, and continue to
breathe in this way until you notice your experience shifting and your negative patterns of
thought and feeling beginning to dissolve and become more flexible.
5. Prompt yourself to move or act with intention in the direction that feels most important and
reflects compassionate care for others. Pause to remember to be kind to yourself and to
appreciate your efforts in working with your habitual patterns.
