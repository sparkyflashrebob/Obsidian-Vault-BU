Comes from the Majjhima Nikaya, Sutta #10

In Insight meditation, our endeavor is to contemplate, from a position of detached observation, the ever-shifting flux of experience itself in order to penetrate through to the essential nature of bodily and mental phenomena - to awaken to this truth. Knowledge and vision of things as they truly are. The Buddha teaches us that the craving and clinging that hold us in bondage are sustained by a network of “conceivings” (maññita)— deluded views, conceits, and suppositions that the mind fabricates by an internal process of mental commentary or “proliferation” (papañca) and then projects out upon the world, taking them to possess objective validity.

I raised one aspect of these conceivings, sakaya ditthi or identification view, a few weeks ago.

The task of insight meditation is to weaken and sever our attachments by enabling us to pierce through this net of conceptual projections in order to see things as they really are.

Mindfulness is a principal tool, among many others, that the Buddha used to aid us in this search out. He details the foundations of mindfulness in the Satipatthanna Sutta. As a result, it is one of the most important suttas in the Pali Canon and contains the most comprehensive statement of the most direct way to the attainment of the Buddhist goal, which is freedom from suffering. This is what is referred to as Nibanna or Enlightenment.

It does so by emphasizing the development of insight, which I mentioned earlier, as the endeavor to contemplate with nonreactivity the essential nature of phenomena.

This is called Vipassana in Buddhist lingo. Insight Meditation, Mindfulness, and Vipassana are terms that are confusing to many people but this sutta is where this is defined by the Buddha. It is a rich and detailed sutta but today I'd like to give a very brief overview of the sutta itself, talk about the first couple of sentences and then provide a condensed summary of the essential aspects of mindfulness as a take away from our gathering today. In future talks, I will follow up with a detailed look at specific aspects of this sutta.

This is THE SUTTA of the TWO SUTTAS that describes his definition of MINDFULNESS, the other one being the MahaSatipatthana Sutta. Both are essentially the same. As we shall see, all of the Buddhas' teachings are contained within this sutta, so there will be plenty to discuss moving forward.

In the Satipatthana Sutta, he provides us with the basic instructions on being mindful, what we should be mindful of*****, an insight on their meaning, specific details to pay attention to and to be mindful of, and what we should expect by doing so.

The Four Foundations of Mindfulness themselves are:

being mindful of the body (which includes the breath and many other practices),

mindful of feelings (whether they are pleasant, unpleasant or neither pleasant or unpleasant),

mindful of the mind,

and mindful of mental-objects, which include the hindrances to our practice, the aggregates, the six bases, the seven factors of enlightenment (which I spoke about a few weeks ago), and the Four Noble Truths (which is his treatise on suffering, the origin of suffering, the cessation of suffering and the way leading to the cessation of suffering.

 

There are a few statements at the beginning, what is deemed the definition of Satipatthana, that I would like to highlight and to have us discuss this evening.

First of all, the Buddha makes a very profound statement:

“Bhikkhus, this is the direct path for the purification of beings, for the surmounting of sorrow and lamentation, for the disappearance of pain and grief, for the attainment of the true way, for the realization of Nibbāna— namely, the four foundations of mindfulness.

First of all, he clearly calls this as the "direct path". A path that is a single path vs being a divided path, the direct path for purifying the mind and thus proceeding towards liberation. facing things directly with awareness. a path that has to be walked alone, and as a way that goes all the way to one goal, Nibanna. Nibanna means to blow out or quenching, as in quenching our thirst. It means liberation from repeated rebirth in a samsaric existence which is an endless cycle of stress or suffering, unsatisfactoriness and pain existence perpetuated by our own desire and ignorance.

SATIPATTHANA is a compound word of sati or memory and upatthana or setting up - establishing or alternatively patthana - meaning domain or foundation again of mindfulness. So satipatthana means either the four ways of setting up mindfulness or as the four objective domains of mindfulness that are spelled out in the rest of the sutta. According to Buddhist historians, it appears that the first definition is the most accurate but the second one is more preferred by commentators of the teachings.

In the next paragraph he describes what the four satipatthanas are:

"one abides contemplating the body as a body, ardent, fully aware, and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world. He abides contemplating feelings as feelings, ardent, fully aware, and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world. He abides contemplating mind as mind, ardent, fully aware, and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world. He abides contemplating mind-objects as mind-objects, ardent, fully aware, and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world.

So Mindful of the Body, Mindful of Feelings, Mindful of Mind, & Mindful of Mind-Objects.

This “definition” specifies the mental qualities that are instrumental for each of these satipaṭṭhāna: one should be diligent (ātāpī), clearly knowing (sampajāna), mindful (sati), and free from desires and discontent (vineyya abhijjhādomanassa).

1. diligence or ardency is the quality we need to maintain an active interest in the the present moment, just as it is. We want to be skillful with our attention and therefore should be held with Mindfulness.

2. Clear knowing (fully aware) means knowing what the body, breath or other of the satipatannas are doing, how well you are staying with it and acknowledges the changing nature of our experience.

3. Perceiving the changing nature of our experience allows us to see and acknowledge dukkha, which can lead us to free ourselves from desires and discontent (covetousness and grief) with regard to the world.
