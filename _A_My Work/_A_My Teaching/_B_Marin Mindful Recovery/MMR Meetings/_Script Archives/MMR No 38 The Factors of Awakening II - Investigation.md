Last week we dove into The Seven Factor of Awakening which are - mindfulness - investigation
of qualities - energy - rapture - concentration - equanimity.
Several years back, my teacher, Thanissaro Bhikkhu, was asked to give a talk on the factors for
awakening, which he defines as: mindfulness, analysis of qualities, persistence, rapture, calm,
concentration, and equanimity. It turned out that the people who’d organized the event had
asked for that topic under the impression that the factors for awakening were a description of
the awakened mind, the goal toward to which we are aiming. Whereas he emphasizes that
these states are actually a path leading to awakening.
As explained in his Study Guide on this subject, he says "part of the misunderstanding was
based on the question of how to translate the Pali term bojjhanga: “awakening-factors.”
Because it’s a compound, it could be factors of awakening, but if you actually look at how these
factors are explained in the texts, the meaning of the term is very much factors for awakening.
These are qualities of mind you develop in order to become awakened. You can’t take
awakening and develop it. Saying the word - awaken - awaken - awaken won’t take you to full
awakening. You can’t clone the awakened state. But you can work on developing the everyday
factors of the mind that eventually will lead to awakening."
He also emphasizes, and I would like to reiterate here that, is another important aspect of the
Buddha’s teaching: The path to awakening is built out of very ordinary, day-to-day things, like
mindfulness and alertness. There is no special intellect that is required. In fact, intellectualizing
things actually can lead us down the wrong path or get us caught in endless proliferation.
It’s simply that when you learn how to look at them in a new way, to analyze them in a new way,
and to develop and use them in a new way, they can take you all the way to awakening. The
investigation of phenomena involves having enough awareness to be able to observe without
judging.
Last week we discussed how these Factors of Awakening are presented in a couple of ways. As
a group they are called the Factors of Awakening or Factor for Awakening as I just mentioned -
so they are considered causes for Awakening. This is how Thanissaro Bhikkhu was referring to
them.
Secondly, the pali word bojjhanga is a compound of bodhi which means enlightenment. and
anga which means limbs or factors. Enlightenment here refers to knowledge that comprehends
the Four Noble Truths: “ the truth of universal suffering or unsatisfactoriness; the truth that
desire is the cause of this suffering and dissatisfaction; the truth that there can be an end to this
suffering; and the truth of the path to the end of this suffering, also known as the Noble Eightfold
Path.”
In our practice of Vispassana or Insight Meditation we can these factors in many ways.
As a part of a progressive path one would hear the teachings, develop some faith in the
teachings, adopts precepts, begins to practice these satipatthanas - which includes mindfulness
of the body, mindfulness of feeling, mindfulness of mind, and mindfulness of mental states. By
doing so consistently one would learn how to purify one’s mind of the hindrances and be able to

gain insight into the way things are in nature, to come into alignment with this nature, otherwise
known in buddhist terms as dhamma, and to free ourselves from the cycle of suffering.
Although we hear the teachings, practicing the four foundations of mindfulness does not mean
studying them, thinking of them, listening to discourses about them, nor discussing them. It is
recommended that what we must do is to be directly and experientially aware of the four
foundations of mindfulness, the four bases on which mindfulness can be established, and try to
be aware of them persistently. Practice with the Four Foundations of Mindfulness in this manner
will automatically and fully develop these Seven Factors of Awakening. This is one way.
In the Fire Sermon, the Buddha identifies “mindfulness” as always useful. When one’s mind is
sluggish we should develop the enlightenment factors of investigation, energy, and joy, and
when we are excited we should develop the enlightenment factors of tranquility, concentration,
and equanimity. In our meditation, the idea is that we learn to both cultivate them and also to
keep them in balance.
This was also expressed in Bojjhanga Sutta, the Buddha says "Just as ... in a peaked house
all rafters whatsoever go together to the peak, slope to the peak, join in the peak, and of
them, all the peak is reckoned chief: even so one who cultivates and makes much of the
seven factors of wisdom, slopes to Nibbana, inclines to Nibbana, tends to Nibbana."
In another sutta, the Seven Factors for Awakening were taught as a means of healing. The idea
being that our minds can profoundly influence and affect the body. One who is optimistic has
more of a chance of getting well than one who is worried and unhappy.
In fact all of the hindrances have a role keeping us in delusion and ignorance because they
hinder, close in, cut off, and obstruct our ability to understand the way for us to release from
suffering.

Thanissaro Bhikkhu calls Investigation of Qualities the heart of the set of Seven Factors. As I
mentioned above, the path to awakening is built out of ordinary, day to day things such as
mindfulness and alertness. This investigation of qualities (the pali term dhammavicaya) is a
matter of paying appropriate attention to what is skillful and what is not. It is the compound of
two words dhamma, which means “to uphold” or phenomena or teachings of the buddha. But it
also refers to the nature of things. Vicaya mean search, or investigation or examination. So that
is how we get investigation of qualities. It can also be referred to as investigation of the nature
of things.

There is a very good analogy we can use to that of a mind that is enveloped by darkness “and
as soon as insight or wisdom arises, we say that the light has come. This light reveals physical
and mental phenomena so that the mind can see them clearly. It is as if you were in a dark room
and were given a flashlight. You can begin to see what is present in the room.” This image
illustrates this second enlightenment factor perfectly. Awareness and mindfulness is the light.
Our looking is the investigation.
This investigation here is not a thinking process. In meditation it is the process of seeing things
as they arise, are present for awhile, and disappear. It is one that characterizes phenomena
that we experience. Vicaya means investigation - I think of it as gathering evidence. But it is
also connected with insight. Most of us have heard the phrase Ah ha! Or Eureka!!! That is
understanding, that is insight, that is seeing clearly.
What do we investigate? We investigate dhamma, the things that we have been hearing here in
our review of the satipatthanas: having a body, breathing, this form, feeling itself, mental
formations (sankharas - our ways of fabrication & constructing activities of body, speech or
mind, our intentions). Our perceptions (how we label things - how we grasp as distinguishing
features of things. What we conscious of - the things that arise as a result of our sense
perception - conscious of seeing, hearing, tasting, touching, and conscious of the activities of
the mind.
We investigate phenomena, mind, and matter. The laws that govern the behavior of
phenomena. Karma, cause, and effect, our direct experience of things.
I’d like again to emphasis that this is not intellectual - thinking about the way things should be or
we want them to be. But when we know these mind states are present we can know and
investigate them also. It is this knowing that makes the light brighter, that leads us onto the other
factors. So these investigations can be used to dispell darkness. This investigation lights up
the field of awareness.
Analysis of qualities is the discernment factor in the factors of awakening. It is the antidote to the
Hinderance of Doubt. Discernment is a matter of appropriate attention, it is the kind of attention
where we basically learn how to ask the right questions, focusing on the issue of what you are
doing and the results of what you are doing.
In one sutta call Two Kinds of Thought, the buddha noticed that he could divide his thoughts into
two sorts: thoughts of sensuality, ill-will and cruelty in one hand and those with letting go,
goodwill and harmlessness on the other. He didn’t divide them into thoughts he liked and those
he didn’t like. He thought of them in terms of where they would lead him. The unskillful side
lead him to unskillful behavior and the skillful side led to skillful behavior.
There is another analogy for the way we can look at our practice that illustrates this. In the
olden days it was a big concern for keeping the cows in check. In Asis, during the rainy season,
the cowherder would need to be vigilant to ensure that the cows would not go off into areas

where they were growing rice. But in the summer when there was not danger of this happening
then the cowherder doesn’t have this concern. This suggests how we might look at what we are
experiencing in the moment. When things appear to be going smoothly, when we are having
skillful thoughts, we might just let things go on. Even if we thought skillful thoughts 24 hours
straight we would eventually get tired so the Buddha discovered that if he brought his mind to
concentration his mind would become calm and still. But even then appropriate attention is
required.
Last week I described how applying the Seven Factors in a progressive manner can bring us to
release. The Buddha actually presents us with a few ways that this can happen. Traditionally he
actually uses the 16 steps of Mindfulness of Breathing as a way of fulfilling the Seven Factors.
These steps are:
1. Breathing in a long breath, I know I am breathing in a long breath. Breathing out a long
breath, I know I am breathing out a long breath.
2. Breathing in a short breath, I know I am breathing in a short breath. Breathing out a short
breath, I know I am breathing out a short breath.
3. Breathing in, I am aware of my whole body. Breathing out, I am aware of my whole body.
4. Breathing in, I calm my whole body. Breathing out, I calm my whole body.
The first two steps as awareness of the breath itself and the second two on awareness of the
body and calming the body.
In the second tetrad the instructions are:
5. Breathing in, I feel joyful. Breathing out, I feel joyful.
6. Breathing in, I feel happy. Breathing out, I feel happy.
7. Breathing in, I am aware of my mental formations. Breathing out, I am aware of my mental
formations.
8. Breathing in, I calm my mental formations. Breathing out, I calm my mental formations.
Here we are to develop our feelings in order to bring about joy, and happiness, and then to bring
our attention to our perceptions.
He goes on,
9. Breathing in, I am aware of my mind. Breathing out, I am aware of my mind.
10. Breathing in, I make my mind happy. Breathing out, I make my mind happy.
11. Breathing in, I concentrate my mind. Breathing out, I concentrate my mind.
12. Breathing in, I liberate my mind. Breathing out, I liberate my mind.
This group is directing us towards our mind and direrecting it towards concentration and letting
go.
And in the final tetraid, he ask us to note:

13. Breathing in, I observe the impermanent nature of all dharmas. Breathing out, I observe the
impermanent nature of all dharmas.
14. Breathing in, I observe the disappearance of desire. Breathing out, I observe the
disappearance of desire.
15. Breathing in, I observe cessation. Breathing out, I observe cessation.
16. Breathing in, I observe letting go. Breathing out, I observe letting go.
This section points us towards impermanence, and to further letting go.
He mentions these steps as bringing the Seven Factors to fruition and in order to do so the
questions of discernment persist. What is the state of our mind right now? Is it balanced or
unbalanced? Is their too much effort taking place or not enough? What do we need to do to
bring it back into balance?
Thanissaro Bhikkhu suggests that these instructions give us some advice on how to answer
these questions.
When discussing the section on The Aggregates a few weeks back we talked about the power
of creation in the mind, what, in Buddhism, are called sankharas. We have a mental tendancy
to put things together. This is what the mind does. It shapes things. Sankharas are all the
things that we conceptualize. They include our memories, perceptions, views and opinions
about ourselves and everything that we know. As we meditate we can begin to understand the
process of how we create our experience of life thru these processes of fabrication.
In this process of working with the Seven Factors we can use this process of Fabrication - bodily
fabrication (which is our breathing), verbal fabrication (which is how we talk to ourselves
internally) and mental fabrication (which includes our perceptions and feelings).
Verbal Fabrication is technically called directed thought and evaluation (vitaka and vicaya). This
is where we direct our thoughts, say on our meditation object, and evaluate what we are
experiencing. This is where we can become more skillful with our practice. We try one thing
and then another until we find something that works.
In order to gladden the mind we require developing skillful qualities and letting go of unskillful
means. How we do this is to look at what we are doing. We set our awareness into being
mindful of the body and our breathing. We give ourselves a verbal pep talk. We look at our
mental attitudes that are present in the moment. In order to be skillful, and especially if we are
aware of any suffering, and we need to continually ask ourselves, are we causing more
suffering?
We can think about the way we approach our breathing in a manner that gives us a perception
of pleasure. This breathing ..... feels really good. We drink it up and spread this to the whole
body. We let it fill the mind.

If we notice that we are doubtful we can simply ask ourselves “Is the breath coming in, or is the
breath coming out, .... or is it still? That’s something right there that we can know and not
doubt. If you even doubt this you are most likely going to doubt everything in life. So it is with
this knowledge of knowing that we are breathing in or or out or knowing stillness, then, we can
at least begin with this. This is why the suggestion is to use our breath as our foundation, it is
always available if we look for it, and it also allows us to look at all 4 satipatthana (mindfulness
of the body, feel, mind and mind-objects) all at once. When the body feels good, we feel good.
When the mind feels good the body feels good. Mind and body are connected. And as we do
this we get a better understanding of how to fabricate the path.
Last week Pat asked the question if the Seven Factors of Awakening are considered a
progressive path. At that time I answered yes. But, in truth, it depends. It can be a progressive
path, if that is how it develops in you. I talked last week about how one path factor leads to the
other in meditation. However there are other ways. As has been mentioned repeatedly and
emphasised, Mindfulness is always helpful and required.
As far as a path of practice, it is usually spoken of in two ways. There is one process that begins
with mindfulness, which then leads to discernment and then to concentration. This is the
process that follows the order of these factors. We use mindfulness to whittle down our
distractions which leads to feeling good (meaning we experience joy and calm in our practice)
which leads to concentration. This requires discernment. We will be looking at the Noble 8fold
path in the coming weeks. Of the eight though this path requires right mindfulness which leads
to right concentration and also shows how discernment fosters concentration.
There is another path where mindfulness directly leads to concentration which then leads back
to discernment. The two paths are actually described as the Insight Path (now described as
discernment) or the Samatha (Joy and Concentration) Path. The understanding is that we need
both in order to realize Nibbana.
As I mentioned, in Buddhist circles it comes in both flavors. Some people have the propensity
to developing concentration, in others discernment leads. Concentration can lead to deep states
of rapture and this can become addicting. It can lead us to thinking that this is nibanna. But in
reality this is just another condition, one that is impermanent, it arises and passes away. By
definition Nibbana means freedom from attachments - the descriptive term being to blow them
out, or quenching our thirst.
We cannot fall for attaching to these meditative attainments for if we do then we will suffer. This
is why we turn back to discernment, to knowing and letting go. The “best” description of the path
is envisioned as where we use discernment which develops concentration and this
concentration further develops even more discernment.
The factors for enlightenment are progressive but also support each other. They all work
together just like the beams of a ceiling.
The point of all of this through is how the mind creates unnecessary suffer and we turn away
from this. In order to do this we use our practice to begin to see clearly how the mind creates
this unnecessary suffering. Why is there suffering? It is because of craving, this wanting.

Where is it grabbing? It is in the mind. So we have to look at what the mind is doing. This is
the point of our meditation. We become sensitive to this fact. We are learning to become more
skillful and stop creating suffering for ourselves. When our activity is based on ignorance we
create suffering, with wisdom and discernment we don’t suffer.
