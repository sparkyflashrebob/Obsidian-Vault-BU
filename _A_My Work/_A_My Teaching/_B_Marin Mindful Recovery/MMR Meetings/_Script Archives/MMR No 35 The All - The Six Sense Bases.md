As I mentioned, this week I have decided to move forward with the teachings of the
Satipatthana Sutta - going into the next section after The Aggregates, which is called the Six
Bases. The reason that I have done so is because the teachings included in the Six Bases
continue to further refine and to expand upon the previous teachings that we have covered so
far and they provide us with yet another avenue for our understanding how we experience the
world. By doing so, we are given another means for letting go, for releasing from the bonds that
cause us to suffer.
This section of the Sutta goes like this:
“Again, bhikkhus, a bhikkhu abides contemplating mind-objects as mind-objects in terms of the
six internal and external bases. And how does a bhikkhu abide contemplating mind-objects as
mind-objects in terms of the six internal and external bases? Here a bhikkhu understands the
eye, he understands forms, and he understands the fetter that arises dependent on both; and
he also understands how there comes to be the arising of the unarisen fetter, and how there
comes to be the abandoning of the arisen fetter, and how there comes to be the future
non-arising of the abandoned fetter. “He understands the ear, he understands sounds... He
understands the nose, he understands odours... He understands the tongue, he understands
flavours... He understands the body, he understands tangibles... He understands the mind, he
understands mind-objects, and he understands the fetter that arises dependent on both; and he
also understands how there comes to be the arising of the unarisen fetter, and how there comes
to be the abandoning of the arisen fetter, and how there comes to be the future non-arising of
the abandoned fetter.
You may have noticed that method of analysis contains a common theme that we have run into
a few times - in particular when we were dealing with the Hindrances. Knowing whether
something is present or not, being aware of the causes and conditions that encourage things to
arise in the first place, being aware of the causes and conditions for things to disappear that
have already arisen, being aware of the causes and condition of things that have not yet arisen,
and being aware of the causes and conditions that discourage things to arise in the future.
I would like to emphasize this method of analysing our experience. The Buddha asks us to get
to know who we are in a more detailed manner. That is the Foundations of Mindfulness.
Knowing the Body, knowing breathing, knowing the position of the body, knowing activities of the
body, knowing the composition of the body, being aware of the impermanence of the body.
Same way with feelings - knowing their being pleasant or unpleasant or not - know their
impermanence. Likewise with knowing the mind - knowing the state of the mind - knowing the
many levels of mind such as a mind where there is greed, or hatred or delusion present.
Further refining this understanding of mind - knowing if any of the Hindrances are present. Is
there any sense-desire present, or ill-will present, or sloth & torpor present, or restlessness and
worry present, or if there is doubt present?
Further breaking these aspects down, he has us look at how this is all created and comes to be
in our mind and body -. To look at the group of mental objects that grasp life. In themselves, we
can see that none of these Aggregates are permanent or personal - our experience of life is

subjective. The Aggregate of forms, feelings, perception, mental formations and consciousness
together serve to lay down a process where we suffer as a result of our clinging to them.
He continues to further break things down for us in this teaching of the Sense Spheres or
Senses Bases. A base being the home where something happens and a sphere being an home
planet or area where something happens.
Here, the Buddha provides a complementary approach between the outer world and ourselves
and places the emphasis on three aspects that we will explore. The first is the six internal
bases or spheres, which are namely the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind in themselves.
We have seen these from a few perspectives. The first being the process being activated, as
consciousness. This is in terms of seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, feeling sensations and
mental processes such as thinking. Here we are looking at it in terms of the internal sense
object that is the instrument that does the sensing. We understand the ‘eye’, how it works, how it
functions, and its characteristics that meet our needs. Likewise with the ear, the nose, the
tongue, the body and the mind.
The second is the external sense base or sphere which are objects externally that we are aware
of: visible forms, sounds, odors, tastes, tactile sensations, and mind objects. The external world.
We understand that there are material forms - objects, that are visible, or we can hear, or we
can smell, and so on. We know that these are produced as a result of having physicalness,
consisting of the elements. We know them as having a label, such as a cat or a dog. That it has
it’s own karma or nature, that it comes into our consciousness under certain conditions.
And thirdly, we have this new term, well actually one that I introduced way back. It is this word
fetter. Specifically the Buddha uses the phrase ‘the fetter that arises dependent on both (for
example - for instance eye and forms)”.
Based on this relationship between the internal and external sense spheres the fetters arise
according to 10 distinct functions and characteristics. In the Sutta Pitaka they are known as the
Ten “Fetters of Becoming”
- belief in a self (Pali: sakkāya-diṭṭhi)[7]
- doubt or uncertainty, especially about the Buddha's awakeness and nine supermundane
consciousnesses (vicikicchā)[8]
- attachment to rites and rituals (sīlabbata-parāmāsa)[9]
- sensual desire (kāmacchando)[10]
- ill will (vyāpādo or byāpādo)[11]
- lust for material existence, lust for material rebirth (rūparāgo)[12]
- lust for immaterial existence, lust for rebirth in a formless realm (arūparāgo)[13]
- conceit (māna)[14][15]
- restlessness (uddhacca)[16]
- ignorance (avijjā)[17]
In the Abhidhamma they are put as:
- sensual lust (Pali: kāma-rāga)
- anger (paṭigha)
- conceit (māna)

- views (diṭṭhi)
- doubt (vicikicchā)
- attachment to rites and rituals (sīlabbata-parāmāsa)
- lust for existence (bhava-rāga)
- jealousy (issā)
- greed (macchariya)
- ignorance (avijjā).
We have been introduced to these fetters previously because of my emphasis on looking at the
first of them, sakkaya ditthi, which is belief in a self or personality view, repeatedly throughout
the teachings.
There are considered 4 Planes of Liberation according to the Suttas. These are the
stream-enterer (Sottapana), the once-returner (Saka-da-gami), the non-returner (or Anā-gāmi)
and arahant or fully enlightened one.
A Sottapana or stream enterer has dropped the first three fetters of identity-view, doubt, and
belief in rites and rituals, while the once-returner has further weakened sensual desire and
ill-will. The non-returner has fully freed themselves from sensual desire and ill-will. While the
arahant has fully been released from the 5 higher fetters of craving for material and immaterial
existence, conceit, restlessness and ignorance.
In the Kotthita Sutta (which means “To Kotthita”) the Buddha’s chief disciple, Sariputra explains
to Maha Kotthita that our problem lies not in the senses themselves, nor in the objects, but that
our suffering comes from the passion that arises in dependence on both. It comes from our
interest in them.
Kotthita asks:”"Now tell me, friend Sariputta, is the eye the fetter of forms, or are forms the fetter
of the eye? Is the ear... Is the nose... Is the tongue... Is the body... Is the intellect the fetter of
ideas, or are ideas the fetter of the intellect?"
"No, my friend. The eye is not the fetter of forms, nor are forms the fetter of the eye. Whatever
desire & passion arises in dependence on the two of them: That is the fetter there. The ear is
not the fetter of sounds... The nose is not the fetter of aromas... The tongue is not the fetter of
flavors... The body is not the fetter of tactile sensations... The intellect is not the fetter of ideas,
nor are ideas the fetter of the intellect. Whatever desire & passion arises in dependence on the
two of them: That is the fetter there.
"Suppose that a black ox and a white ox were joined with a single collar or yoke. If someone
were to say, 'The black ox is the fetter of the white ox, the white ox is the fetter of the black' —
speaking this way, would he be speaking rightly?"
"No, my friend. The black ox is not the fetter of the white ox, nor is the white ox the fetter of the
black. The single collar or yoke by which they are joined: That is the fetter there."

Bhikkhu Analyo says that - by exploring this, we gain insight into the impersonal nature of our
own thoughts. When we are in meditation, we can see how difficult is to follow the train of
thoughts, feelings and desires as they pass by. As we continue, it is possible for us to see that it
is impossible for us to have thoughts only when and how we would like them. This is the central
purpose of meditative training, to gradually tame the thinking activity and bring it under more
conscious control.
We see that the six senses and their objects give rise to “ the conditioned arising of
consciousness”. Our experience turns out to be primarily subjective - we experience things
based on how these sense impressions are received and known. Although how one
experiences the world is thought of as being objective is in fact conditioned by the subject as
much as the object. We see that we are in fact biased for the most part in how we see the world.
What comes into our sense doors is often projected onto the object by the perceiver. By our
perceptions.

These fetters arise based on what has been perceived. Being mindful of this process that leads
to the arises of a fetter is the 1st stage of the sense-spheres - seeing the fetters arise with the
internal sense organ and the external sense object. This takes non-reactive observation. The
2nd phase is to see where perception causes suffering, and also in discovery of our habits of
mind so that we can prevent this from continuing to occur.
The Foundations of Mindfulness so far up to this point of the our understanding up to the the
Hindrances has been that of knowing when things arise. With this teaching on the sense
spheres we further refine this so that our attention is directed toward the knowing of the first
stages of the perception process.
The Buddha describes the process of perception as a conditioned sequence from contact via
feeling and cognition to thought. This can further generate conceptual proliferation -what is
referred to as papanca in buddhist practice. This process then leads to further proliferation and
explodes into all kinds of associations concerning the past, present and future. This last stage
of the process, contact, is an event in which one is a passive experiencer who perceives this
conceptual proliferation as their own thoughts and feelings. Once we bite the hook the course is
set. What we believe is going on becomes our understanding along with the ramifications of
this process. I see this as our part of our karma - our underlying habits and tendencies.
'Bhikkhu, as to the source through which perceptions and notions [born of] mental proliferation
beset a man: if nothing is found there to delight in, welcome, and hold to, this is the end of the
underlying tendency to lust...this is the end of resorting to rods and weapons...here these evil
unwholesome states cease without remainder,'
“Dependent on eye & forms, eye-consciousness arises. The meeting of the three is contact.
With contact as a requisite condition, there is feeling. What one feels, one perceives (labels in

the mind). What one perceives, one thinks about. What one thinks about, one objectifies. Based
on what a person objectifies, the perceptions & categories of objectification assail him/her with
regard to past, present, & future forms cognizable via the eye. This is taken from the
Madhu-pindika Sutta MN 18 also known as “The Honeyball Sutta”.
As is pointed to here, the critical stage of this sequence occurs with the initial feeling and
cognition. The initial bias present is then reinforced by additional thinking and panpanca.
The Honeyball Sutta also mentions what are referred to as various “latent tendencies”. The
most common being sensual desire, irritation, view, doubt, craving for existence, and ignorance.
These “latent tendencies” are what are known as anu-saya.
Typically these are seven listed as:
1. Latent tendency of desire for sense pleasures (kamaraganusaya)
2. Latent tendency of aversion (patighanusaya)
3. Latent tendency of conceit (mananusaya)
4. Latent tendency of wrong view (ditthanusaya)
5. Latent tendency of doubt (vicikiccanusaya)
6. Latent tendency of desire for existence (bhava-raganusaya)
7. Latent tendency of ignorance (avijjanusaya)
In addition to papanca or mental proliferation and latent tendencies, there is another process
also that relates to the process of perception. Here we are introduced to another term, the
“asava’s” or flows. These are underlying tendencies which influence our perceptual process
that arise without our conscious intention but due to unwise attention and to ignorance. Unwise
attention being ayoniso manasikara and ignorance or avija. To counteract the arising of the
influxes is our training and to be successful is a synonym to full awakening.
The common asava’s, also known as cankers, taints or fermentations are:
1. Desire for sense-pleasure (kamasava)
2. Desire for continued existence (bhavasava)
3. False views (ditthasava)
4. Ignorance (avijjasava)
I found this wonderful blog that describes the latent tendencies in Theravada Buddhism.
Dr. Ari Ubey-sekara wrote:
Whenever a sense object is received through one of the six sense doors of the eye, ear, nose,
tongue, body and the mind of an unenlightened person, the mental defilements that are present
in the mind will act at three different levels.
1. Subtle level of latent tendency (anusaya)
2. Intermediate conscious level of obsessions (pariyutthana)
3. Gross level of transgression (vithikkama)

At the first level of latent tendency (anusaya), mental defilements that exist in the mind at a
dormant, subtle or preconscious level get aroused by a particular sense object received through
a sense door. The mental defilements that are aroused give rise to emotions and thoughts at the
intermediate conscious level of obsessions (pariyutthana).
Finally, at the gross level of the defilements, these emotions and thoughts manifest themselves
as unwholesome verbal or bodily actions (vitikkama). At the first and second levels, arousal of
the dormant defilements and the arising of emotions and thoughts are confined to one’s own
mind with no involvement with the external world. However, at the gross level when the
unwholesome thoughts and emotions manifest in the form verbal and/or bodily action, it can
affect one’s moral behaviour with negative effects to oneself and others around.
The path that is taught by the Buddha is to a) eradicate the taints, b) uproot our latent
tendencies, and c) abandon the fetters. These terms point to the same basic problem from
slightly different perspectives.
The asavas are thought as the root causes for the arising of dukkha, which flow into our
evaluation of what we are perceiving.
The underlying tendencies are the unwhole inclinations of the unawakened mind that tend to get
triggered during perception. The fetters are factors that arise to bind us into continued suffering,
referred to as samsara.
In order to avoid this, we are called upon to restrain our senses. We do this by being aware to
be mindful. It is suggested that this sense restraint is a cause for the arising of joy and
happiness, which leads as a basis for concentration and insight. To live in full awareness in the
present moment can give rise to a profound sense of delight.
This restraint of our senses in not simply avoiding seeing and hearing. Bhikkhu Analyo points
out that, if this were true, then blind and deaf people would be accomplished practitioners. It
calls on us to not dwell on the general appearance or sign (nimitta) or the secondary
characteristics of sense objects in order to avoid the flow of detrimental influences. We just
don’t linger.
Analayo references something called a penetrative understanding of the nature of cognition
(sanna) being a prominent cause for realization. If we perceive things under the influence of
sensuality or aversion- this can lead to other distortions which are the cause of unwholesome
thoughts and intentions. The suggestion is that we cultivate beneficial ways of perception as a
countermeasure. How we perceive then becomes part of our training -to retrain our habits. We
learn to look at our experience with the knowing the true characteristics of conditioned
experience. As we do so we become more familiar with this way of seeing - thereby creating
new habit patterns. This is done with both reflection and contemplation.
While going thru this research, I came to the conclusion that this discussion on the Sense
Spheres can get way too technical. In reality, it is being aware of what we are aware of and how
this affects us. There is a beautiful teaching that can condense and summarize these teachings.
They were expressed by the Buddha’s instructions to a non-Buddhist ascestic named Bahiya.
These words I refer to again and again. He said:
When in the seen will be only what is seen, in the heard only what is heard, in the sensed
only what is sensed, in the known only what is known, you will not be by that; when you

are not by that, you will not be therein; when you are not therein, you will be neither here,
nor there, nor in between. This is the end of dukkha.
