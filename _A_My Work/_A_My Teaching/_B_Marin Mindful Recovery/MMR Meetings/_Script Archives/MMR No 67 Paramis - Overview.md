MMR#67: Paramis (Overview)
As promised I have been looking for an opportunity to talk about a set of 10 perfections - noble
qualities of character that are associated with awakened beings. Qualities that we can develop
in order to build the character necessary for awakening.
Just as we are aware of and can develop the factors of awakening -- we can also develop these
qualities of awakening.
Theravāda Buddhism
The Theravada teachings on the pāramīs can be found in late period of the canon of books and
post-canon commentaries. One commentator describes them as noble qualities usually
associated with bodhisattvas -- those who delay their own awakening and instead use their
efforts to work for the benefit of others . Thanissaro Bhikkhu describes them as perfections
(paramī) of character necessary to realize awakening enlightenment as one of the three
enlightened beings, a samma sambuddha (a universal buddha - the highest form of
buddhahood)- a pacceka-buddha (a buddha who attains enlightenment by ones own efforts at a
time when the dhamma is not known to the world) or an arahant (one who attains enlightenment
with the aid of a samma sambuddha).
In the Pāli Canon, there is what is known as the Buddha-vaṃsa (the Chronicle of the Buddhas)
which describes the life of Gautama Buddha and the 24 Buddhas who proceeded him. In this,
the 1st Chapter of the 14th book of the Khuddaka Nikāya there is listed perfections (dasa
pāramiyo) as:
The first is Dāna pāramī: which is the perfection of generosity, giving of oneself.
Then there is the Sīla pāramī: which is the perfection of virtue, of morality, and of proper
conduct.
And then the Nekkhamma pāramī: the perfection of renunciation.
The 4th being Paññā pāramī: which is the perfection of wisdom, of discernment.
#5 is Viriya pāramī: the perfection of energy, diligence, vigour, and effort.
#6 is Khanti pāramī: that of patience, tolerance, forbearance, acceptance, and endurance
#7 is Sacca pāramī: that of truthfulness, and honesty.
#8 is Adhi-ṭṭhāna pāramī : that of perfect determination, and resolution.resolve.
Ninth is Mettā pāramī: the parami of goodwill, of friendliness, and of loving-kindness.
And finally there is Upekkhā pāramī: the perfection of equanimity, and serenity.
You may recognize that the last two of these virtues, mettā and upekkhā, also are
brahmavihāras - the sublime abidings.
Historically
The Theravādin teachings on the pāramīs can be found in many canonical books (the Jataka
tales (which are stories about the previous lives of the buddha), the Apadāna - stories of various
Buddhist monks and nuns who lived at the time of the Buddha, the Buddhavaṃsa, and the
Cariyā-piṭaka - which is a book about the former lives of the Buddha where the bodhisattva

exhibited behaviors known as perfections - prerequisites to Buddhahood. These post-canonical
commentaries were written to supplement the Pāli Canon at a later time, and are most likely not
an original part of the Theravādin teachings.
The oldest parts of the Sutta Piṭaka (for example, Majjhima Nikāya, Digha Nikāya, Saṃyutta
Nikāya and the Aṅguttara Nikāya) do not have any mention of the pāramīs as a category
(though they are all mentioned individually).
Some scholars even refer to the teachings of the pāramīs as a semi-Mahāyāna teaching added
to the scriptures at a later time in order to appeal to the interests and needs of the lay
community and to popularize their religion. However, these views rely on the early scholarly
presumption of Mahāyāna originating with religious devotion and appeal to laity. More recently,
scholars have started to open up early Mahāyāna literature, which is very ascetic and expounds
the ideal of the monk's life in the forest. Therefore, the practice of the pāramitās in Mahāyāna
Buddhism may have been close to the ideals of the ascetic tradition of the samana - “one who
exerts themselves for some higher spiritual purpose.
Traditional practice
Bhikkhu Bodhi maintains that, in the earliest Buddhist texts (which he identifies as the first four
nikāyas), those seeking the extinction of suffering (nibbana) pursued the noble eightfold path.
As time went on, a backstory was provided for the multi-life development of the Buddha; as a
result, the ten perfections were identified as part of the path for the bodhisattva. Over time, the
pāramīs have been seen as being significant for those on this pathless path.
Bhikkhu Bodhi summarizes:
In established Theravāda tradition the pāramīs are not regarded as a discipline peculiar to
candidates for Buddhahood alone but as practices which must be fulfilled by all aspirants to
awakening. The only difference being that the most dedicated bodhisattva raise the bar to
cultivate these pāramīs and therefore dedicate more length of time to them. But the qualities
themselves are universal requisites for awakening, which all must fulfill to at least a minimal
degree to merit the fruits of the liberating path.
In the Samyutta Nikaya - which is included in the original teachings but also elsewhere in the
pali canon, the Buddha mentions only the three trainings, morality (sila), concentration
(samadhi), and insight wisdom (panna). You may recognize these as being known to us as the
three divisions of the Noble Eightfold path.
Some mention that these 10 paramis as the perfections that us lay-folk would undertake being
that we are not samanas/monks. I will not go into detail about all of them here but I briefly
discuss some of these that we may not be so familiar with.
The point is that these parami have the effect of purifying and transforming the mind of the giver.
So when speaking about Dana for example - the act of Generosity - the purpose of it being that
it has the quality of making the mind and heart pliable. When someone makes a generous offer
of some gift, the very act of giving serves as a decisive support
[2]
to make the mind more pliable

and ready for observance of precepts, for cultivation of concentration and for development of
insight wisdom through practice of Vipassanā meditation.
It is within the experience of every Buddhist, that a feeling of awkwardness and embarrassment
arises in them whenever they visit, without an offering, a monasteries or temple for the purpose
of keeping precepts, of listening to Dhamma talks or for the practice of meditation. It is
customary for the lay community to bring an offering, such as flowers, incense, cheese or
chocolate whenever visiting a monastery.
Formally at monasteries, the lay community requests that the teachings be given and teachers
give this freely both as an act of dana. There are many ways that dana can be given - we can
give gifts but the act of receiving one’s gift is also considered and act of generosity. It is related
to sympathetic joy (of mudita - one of the four sublime perfections). We can give the gift of
kindness, of compassion, of our time, of listening, of tolerance and patience. Our meditation
practice is a gift to the world. We know of many ways in our hearts to be generous.
It is different than giving with the expectation of receiving. Dana is the act of both giving and
receiving without reward. Gifts given in pursuit of love or out of anger, fear, or foolishness etc.
and even giving punishment, giving a sentence of death are worldly giving. They do not form
part of Perfection of Generosity.
This act of dana it therefore a factor in the other paramis. This is a pattern that is seen in the
others also.
When speaking about sila paramis - this is exhibited by keeping the non harming precepts of
not-killing, taking things that are not given, of using inappropriate speech, speech at is untrue,
rude or spreads rumors or discord within the community, of not using our sexuality
inappropriately, or of using intoxicants which dull the mind. It is an act of sila but also an act of
dana and a way of providing protection for ourselves and others.
Nekkhamma pāramī (the pleasure of renunciation) can be practiced in many ways. It is one of
the practices associated with Right Intention. The most basic definition of this being to give up
worldly pleasures of the world and turning more towards spiritual practice. Generally one
simplifies one's life by reducing or giving away possessions, cutting back on entertainment and
other forms of distraction that take us away from presence. One might rededicate more to the
practice by spending more time alone or periods of silence, practicing more meditation,
simplifying what one wears, not using cosmetics, moderation in food, etc.
Paññā pāramī: is the perfection of wisdom and of discernment. This is the 3rd basket of the
Noble Eightfold Path which is comprised of Right View and Right Intention. It is the
understanding that one knows the realities appearing before us in the moment as they really
are. We have been exploring this from many perspectives, being mindful, deepening our
understanding of who we really are. Discernment of what is skilful and what is unskillful.

Viriya pāramī: the perfection of energy, diligence, vigour, and effort. This means to look at where
we place our efforts and what how. What we think and ponder upon will be the inclination of our
mind. Therefore we make the effort to not let our difficulties be a deterrent to our spiritual
growth.
This is where Khanti pāramī comes in. Practicing that of patience, tolerance, forbearance,
acceptance, and endurance in the face of greed, hatred and delusion that is within ourselves,
our community and our society. We are tolerant of pain, happiness, hardship, praise, both
fortune and misfortune. Our nature is of understanding of the nature of things - and to not hold
onto those things that are ours to own anyway.
It must come from a place of truthfulness, and honesty. This is the meaning of Sacca pāramī.
For if we cannot be honest with ourselves then we cannot open the truth of the way it is, the way
of nature, of dhamma or the teaching. We are straight. We come from a place of not acting or
saying anything other than the truth.
Adhi-ṭṭhāna pāramī : that of perfect determination, and resolution means to have firm intention
even in the face of our defects. The resolve that we will not be shaken “though the winds blow
from all directions, those with firm intentions do not waiver.
All this is done with the parami of goodwill, of friendliness, and of loving-kindness. Metta. This
means to look at how we look at others and ourselves in a kindly way.
And finally there is Upekkhā pāramī: the perfection of equanimity, and serenity. I hope everyone
here has that as an aspiration. It means equanimity both in happiness and unhappiness, it is not
moved because it takes happiness and unhappiness as the same thing: realities that arise because
of conditions and then fall away. No one can own happiness and unhappiness. Everything is
Dhamma.
It is obvious that even though everyone might know the ten parami as good, in everyday life, not
in every occasion can parami occur.
