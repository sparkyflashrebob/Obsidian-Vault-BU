Self-Forgiveness
(modified from Teachings from Tara Brach and Jack Kornfield)

There is this saying “Don’t push anyone, including yourself, out of your heart”.
Isn’t this the essence of true freedom? To have a heart free of the burdens of hate and
disappointment- free from resentment? To have a heart that is able to hold these with wisdom
and compassion?
“Darkness cannot drive out darkness: only light can do that. Hate cannot drive out hate:
only love can do that.”
― Martin Luther King Jr., A Testament of Hope: The Essential Writings and Speeches
In order to love we need to let of hate and in order to let go of hate we have to learn to let go
and in order to let go of our hate and disappointment of ourselves and others we have to learn
to forgive.
One of the very roots of really whatever is going on with us in terms of suffering is that sense of
not being forgivable. That something is just intrinsically not OK. That it is not acceptable. Usually
we think of forgiveness in terms of the need to forgive others. Usually pointing the finger
outward. If you look up forgiveness on the internet, almost all of the results speak to forgiving
others. Rarely do we look inside ourselves.
But forgiving others starts with seeing the suffering in ourselves and also seeing the effects of
our non-forgiveness in our other relationships. If we are lost in our resentment - see that our
resentment is more valuable to us than our freedom from suffering then we cannot begin the
process of healing because we will continue to harm ourselves and others. This process will be
hidden from us.
We may not want to look. For ourselves, it could be because we carry a sense of self-blame. It
can be the feeling that we are not a good parent or partner - somehow being defective -
identifying with our addictions and our defects. The sense of shame of not being who we feel we
should be - to be a disappointment. Whatever it is, many, many people carry at the core of their
suffering the sense of being unforgivable.
In the process of healing - its this pivotal element - that sense of being unforgivable - that over
a period of time - softens. When we start to heal that is when real freedom begins.
Charlotte Joko Beck - a zen teacher put it this way: "Our capacity to know joy is directly related
to our capacity to forgive."

There really is this direct relationship between forgiving and the freedom of the heart. And as
most of us know, when we feel like we've caused harm, it's the last thing we think we can do. It's
so hard.
So this evening - as a part of our exploration of forgiveness over the next couple of weeks - we
will take a look at what makes it so hard to forgive ourselves?
Many people find that the word forgiveness has associations that are difficult to work with. To let
go, to surrender, to admit personal lack, association with the feeling that you are bad - a sinner.
At the very least, if we cannot come to say that we forgive ourselves, we might, instead, say that
we can hold this with compassion or acceptance.
Why do we get stuck with self-blame? One layer is our culture. For thousands of years there
has been this basic message of basic badness - of sin.
There is a story of an Eskimo who asks a missionary priest, “"If I did not know about God and
sin, would I go to hell?" And the priest says, "No. Why do you ask?" So the Eskimos says, "Well,
why did you tell me?"
This basic badness can get exacerbated within families where there is a lot of judgement and
criticism or in some deep way, if our needs aren’t being met.
Research has shown that when we are wounded early on, many children think it’s their fault. We
can think something is wrong with us. This can deepen this kind of core shame and insecurity
we have in our being. And in ways to protect ourselves, to manage, to try to meet our needs, we
can behave in ways that can cause harm.
Tara Brach speaks of a metaphor where a person is walking in the woods, and they see a little
dog under a tree. And their initial response is, "Oh, how nice, a dog." And they go over to pet it,
but the dog lurches at them with its fangs bared-- you know, ferocious. And they go from being
friendly towards the dog to angry and thinking "bad dog."
And then they see that the dog's paw is in a trap. And then there's that shift again to, "Oh my
gosh, this poor thing." And it's not like they're necessarily going to try to get close to the dog and
get bit. But their hearts have changed again.
Despite this - we can be caught in non-forgiving, as if in a trance. Our reality being narrow and
fixated - only seeing the harm that we have done and the sense that it is our fault. Many times
we forget the chain of events that led up to our acting out in ways that caused harm. We may
even fail to even recognize that our deepest longing is to not to cause harm. Our deepest
longing is for connection, for love, and for peace.

Why do we resist forgiving? Lily Tomlin said, “forgiveness means giving up all hope for a better
past.” We know this, rationally - that we cannot change the past. Self blame does not help.
And yet we are unable to let go anyway. How come?
There are a couple of clues we can begin to be aware of to know how we are unable to forgive.
1. When we learn to forgive ourselves many times we go ahead and give ourselves
permission to do what we want to do anyway. To continue the behavior
a. There is this fear that our shadow selves will rule the day. That our defects of
character are who we are. How we identify. Having our habitual way of being be
the way it normally turns out.
b. We forgive ---- but then we find that our unawakened self reveals itself and
wreaks havoc at some point.

2. There is a quote from Yogi Berra who says, “ I never blame myself when I am not hitting
well. I just blame the bat. And if it keeps up, I just change bats. After all, if I know it isn’t
my fault I am not hitting, how can I be mad at myself”
a. Pointing the finger at others or on something else. Our Ego says to not look this
way.
b. But the truth of the matter is If we never look at ourselves and take a fearless
inventory, then we will never improve.

3. There is a subtle belief that if we forgive ourselves then we will never become the person
we want to be. This is counterproductive to me but I guess it is part of human
psychology. Having a sense of who we think we are. Maybe some of you have some
insight into this aspect.
4. There can be the thought - if I’ve really hurt someone, how will forgiving myself help?
Why should I accept or forgive myself? Won’t that be letting me get away with it?
Wouldn’t I be co’ing myself?
5. There is this sense of punishing being right - that it is deserved.
We will not be able to face the consequences of our actions and forgive ourselves until we can
bring these and other deep seated beliefs above the line and into awareness. Only then can we
witness them and decide how much we want to buy into them.
When we contemplate forgiveness it can be helpful to reflect a bit as we did some at the
beginning of our session this evening. So let's do a bit more of that right now - just so we can
get a sense of the direct experience of what we might find difficult.

It might also be that we have caused pain, maybe anger, feelings that we should be punished,
feeling empty or numb, have the fear that we will do it again, having self-doubt, horrified, feel
grief, loneliness, or failure, having vulnerability, or hurt, or that we will not be loved, fear that it
might be true - that we are a bad person, but then again we might feel joy.
If letting go of blame gives you joy, what would stop you? Can you feel joy right now? If you
can, is there anything between you and letting go of blame? This kind of inquiry can be
beneficial.
Joy usually is not felt if we are not able to let go of self blame.
Usually what is beneath the blame - very deep - is grief. This can feel even more difficult to
hold. We can hold on to blaming so that we do not have to open to the fullness of reality
underneath - to how we grieve.
We can get addicted to the 2nd arrow of blame. It can seem to be way easier and safer to avoid
forgiving ourselves so we do not have to face what is beneath. There is this illusion of control
that buffers our vulnerability. We would do anything to avoid it.
Our culture doesn’t like soft words such as love and compassion. We identify with anger and
aggression. It takes courage to forgive ourselves. The courage to be willing to let go of the
strategy of blame.

Chogyam Trungpa once said, "The essence of human bravery is refusing to give up on anyone
or anything, including ourselves." Self-forgiveness is part of the path towards our evolving
consciousness. We can think of this as our evolution - to move from fight, flight, freeze and from
aggression towards ourselves to the more integrated fullness of our being that calls upon
compassion and forgiveness.
Danna Faulds, a poet, says, "Do you value your reasons for staying small more than the light
shining through the open door? Forgive yourself. Perfection is not a prerequisite for anything but
pain. Please, oh please, don't continue to believe in your stories of separation and failure. This
is the day of your awakening."
To understand forgiveness we need to recognize the storyline of blame and to sense if we can
come in touch with the actual feeling underneath with kindness. To get beyond the surface of
how things appear.
Tara Brach tells the story of a woman in maximum security in prison who was kind of a bully but
was part of an eight-week mindfulness meditation course. At the end of the course she spoke
about the effect of the course on her. What moved her was a poem by Thich Nhat Hahn, “Call
Me by my True Names.”
It says, "I'm the frog swimming happily in the clear pond. And I'm also the grass snake, who
approaching in silence, feeds itself on the frog. On the 12-year-old girl refugee on a small boat
who throws herself into the ocean after being raped by a sea pirate. And I'm the pirate, my heart
not yet capable of seeing and loving. "Please call me by my true names, so I can hear all my
cries and laughs at once. So I can see that my joy and pain are one. Please call me by my true
names, so I can wake up, and the door of my heart can be left open-- the door of compassion."
Upon contemplating this the woman in prison shared that all my life “I was the bad one, the
problem one. And now I know I am suffering, too”.
When we can get beyond blame and sense the suffering without putting ourselves down or
letting ourselves off the hook by co’ing ourselves, or saying we do not deserve to be forgiven -
knowing that this hurts is a moment when we can begin to move towards self-forgiveness.
Tara was working with a man who was a recovering alcoholic - drinking had broken up his
family. He went to a 12-Step Program and the process of making amends. As a part of this he
started visiting his daughter because they both felt they really wanted to connect.
When he visited her he was self-conscious and aloof. So as Tara and the man talked about it
she asked him to describe what his beliefs about these encounters were - what was going on in
those moments? He thought that she would never forgive him - that he didn’t deserve to be
forgiven - that he screwed up too badly.
Tara asked him “how familiar is that feeling of being unforgivable and bad?.

This brought him back to when he was 8 years old and his parents were breaking up - it being
his fault - THAT’S THE BELIEF. And the feeling of loneliness. Then at 14 he turned to smoking
pot and drinking with the kids in the neighborhood and so on and so forth. Many of us know this
story ourselves.
Tara asked him to think of that 8-year old boy - he’s lonely - he’s ashamed of himself - to
knowing that it was not his fault they divorced - and it is not his fault he tried to make himself
feel better.
That was the point that he realized that that little boy was suffering. The truth of this realization
brought him to tears. Having someone else say that “it was not his fault” - that he was not bad -
that broke him apart.
This shifted his relationship with himself and his daughter. He became able to share what he
was going through. How ashamed he had felt and how much she mattered to him. This was
the beginning of a true reconciliation.
When we talk about it not being our fault many people feel that this takes away our sense of
responsibility. It actually becomes the beginning of being able to be responsive.
We have to go through the process of recognizing what we are feeling - to get in touch with what
is underneath all of that - to feel the guilt, grieving and sorrow. When we can say it is not our
fault it does not remove us from the responsibility. It is just saying there is not a personal
badness that is the cause. There is a larger process involved.
Having the capacity to be compassionate towards and feeling the grieving we feel is actually
what allows us to begin to open up and establish relationships that are some of the most
important ones in our lives. To be able to let in the reality of the people we are with.
There is a lot of research out now on forgiveness and some of the best research shows how the
process of self-forgiving and self-compassion is what leads to a way of being able to open our
hearts to others.
You can’t will self-forgiveness. There can be a willingness though. It begins with the intention
that comes from the wise place in us that knows that we are not free until we include ourselves
in our own heart.
It is not a one-shot. It is a process that unfolds on its own timing.
It is rooted in a deep woundedness - often with trauma - that requires support. We really need
the support of others - to friends - groups - therapists. Often we have to imagine others
forgiving us as part of forgiving ourselves.
It is also important to have the ability to be still with what is happening to us. To not jump away
from, to avoid, or to push it too quickly. We want to just be able to be with it.

We want to bring into consciousness that which has been unconscious to us. We have been
living it, feeling it. But somehow we haven’t let ourselves actually know it.
Simon Wilde, a Christian mystic poet, writes, "The danger is not that the soul will find no bread,
but that by a lie it should convince itself that it's not hungry." And there is something in that - for
us - that we think we can get along without it. There's something unconscious that-- we actually
don't know that there's something that we need.
Marianne Williamson reminds us that “The practice of forgiveness is our most important
contribution to the healing of the world”. This begins with us - with forgiving ourselves - for if
we cannot forgive ourselves then we can not attend to the deep healing within ourselves that is
required for us to ending suffering and freeing ourselves.
I offer this for our consideration.

ADDITIONAL that will be covered next week:
12 Principles of Forgiveness in Buddhism according to Jack Kornfield.
1. Understanding what forgiveness is and what it is not.
2. Sense the suffering that comes with the inability to forgive.
3. Reflect on the benefits of a loving heart.
4. Discover (in relation to your identity), you don't have to be loyal to your suffering.
5. Understand that forgiveness is a process, not a single action.
6. You have to set your intention for forgiveness.
7. Learn the inner and outer forms of forgiveness.
8. Start with the easiest thing that can open your heart.
9. Be willing to grief and let go.
10. Sometimes trauma is stored in our physical bodies.
11. Shift of identity.
12. Perspective.
