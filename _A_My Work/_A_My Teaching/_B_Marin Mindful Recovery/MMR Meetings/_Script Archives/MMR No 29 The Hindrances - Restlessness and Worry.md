4. Restlessness-worry (uddhacca-kukkucca)
See also: uddhacca and kukkucca
The hindrance of restlessness-worry (ud-dhac-ca-kukkuc-ca) refers to a mind that is agitated and
unable to settle down.
Ud-dhac-ca is a pali Buddhist term that is translated as "excitement", or "restlessness". In the
Theravada tradition, ud-dhac-ca is defined as a mental factor that is characterized by agitation, like
water whipped by the wind.[1]

In the Mahayana tradition, it is called aud-dhat-ya (sanskrit) and is
defined as a mental factor that causes our mind to fly off from an object and recollect something
else.
Ud-dhac-ca means to shake above or to be without rest. Joseph Goldstein describes it in
“Mindfulness: A Practical Guide to Awakening” as a mind not settled into the object but hovering over
it. In the body it is sometimes described as feeling as if we are jumping out of one’s skin. It is
uncomfortable and can be difficult to pay attention to as a result. Restlessness manifests as an
inability to sit peacefully when disturbed. We may have urges to move, either constant small
movements or even to avoid the feelings altogether by changing position.
Some feel that It is caused by the fault-finding state of mind which is not satisfied with things as they
are and so is looking for something better.
It requires patience, discipline, and courage to face it.
● Gil Fronsdal says that: "The discomfort of restlessness creates an outward looking
[tendency] – saying to itself - what can I do to fix this? What can I do to settle this? [...] So the
challenge in restlessness is how to turn towards it and be present for it and engage it”
Mental restlessness can be expressed as thinking that is scattered and ever flowing. We are caught
in distraction.
kuk-kuc-ca is a Buddhist term that is translated as "regret"or "worry". In the Theravada tradition,
kuk-kucca is defined as worry or remorse after having done wrong; it has the characteristic of regret.
In the Mahayana tradition, it is defined as sadness because of mental displeasure with a former
action we have taken.
I also believe that this regret or worry doesn’t even have to be something that we ourselves
generate. When I am in fear, particularly with something at work that involves a superior,

restlessness and worry are present in me as my mind becomes restless and anxious.

Analogy
Restlessness (uddhacca) is compared to being a slave, continually having to jump to the orders of a
tyrannical boss who always demands perfection and so never lets one stop.
In the Samyutta Nikaya (35.247) there is a sutta called the Simile of the Six Animals. In it the
Buddha compares the state of mind of an undisciplined meditator in relation to different aspects of
mind. Each of the senses being represented by different animals. There is a snake, a crocodile, a
bird, a dog, a jackal and a monkey - each being connected together by a rope (which is the mind
itself). Under these circumstances each of the animals would seek to go in different directions as is
with their nature. For instance the snake would want to take shelter in the grass, while the crocodile
would want to be in the water. Each of the animals would want to go in different directions.
When the six animals would be worn out and fatigued, they would be dominated but the one among
them that is the strongest at the time. They would submit to it and come under its control.
The solution he offers is to have the meditator place an anchor in the mind similar to a post or a
stake and have all the animals tied down to that. The animals would still be pulling in different
directions but, by having the anchor, the meditator would be able to just sit there watching, observing
their behavior.
In this analogy, the animals would eventually tire and just lay down next to the post. The stake in
this case is mindfulness of the body. It is this mindfulness that assists us with attaining freedom.
Joseph Goldstein describes a few additional ways that restlessness can manifest with meditators.

This recognition means to look at how we are holding it in the body and mind. If there is a

When we are lost in the restlessness and worry without being aware it is like we cannot see the

Getting grounded can be difficult in itself in this case. We cannot force this. But there are some

Mindfulness and compassion can come in.

I am reminded of the Lojong Tibetian Practices - which is a mind training for refining and
purifying one’s motivations and attitudes. There are seven points with slogans used at a training
Point One is to train in the preliminaries, which are the basis for dharma practice.
1. Maintain an awareness of the preciousness of human life.
2. Be aware of the reality that life ends; death comes for everyone; Impermanence.
3. Recall that whatever you do, whether virtuous or not, has a result; Karma.
4. Contemplate that as long as you are too focused on self-importance and too caught up in

In “From When Things Fall Apart: Heart Advice for Difficult Times”, Pema Chodron talks about
another one of these slogans that says, “Drive all blames into oneself.” She goes on to say, “The
essence of this slogan is, “When it hurts so bad, it’s because I am hanging on so tight.” It’s not
saying that we should beat ourselves up. It’s not advocating martyrdom. What it implies is that
pain comes from holding so tightly to having it our own way and that one of the main exits we
take when we find ourselves uncomfortable, when we find ourselves in an unwanted situation or
an unwanted place, is to blame.
This slogan is a helpful and interesting suggestion that we could begin to shift that deep-seated,
ancient, habitual tendency to hang on to having everything on our own terms. The way to start
would be, first, when we feel the tendency to blame, to try to get in touch with what it feels like to
be holding on to ourselves so tightly.”
