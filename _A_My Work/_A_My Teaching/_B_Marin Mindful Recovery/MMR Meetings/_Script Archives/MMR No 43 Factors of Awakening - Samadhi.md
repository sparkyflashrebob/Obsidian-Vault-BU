Samadhi (Concentration) as “One-pointedness”(Ekaggatā):
● Buddhist Meditation in general has two approaches. To Unify the factors of mind either
on a single object or on moment-to-moment changing objects
● Moment to moment awareness is called (Vipassana or Insight Meditation). And is also
a critical part of the path.
● Changing objects - sensations experienced during breathing or the arising and passing
away of the breath, or other sensations within the body or mind.
● Samadhi is an example of the second method which uses a Single object - such as the
breath
● Either way, when concentration is strong - we are undistracted
● Non-distraction is peaceful - cultivation of this path in meditation brings both this peace
of mind and wakefulness this to both our meditation and to daily life
● Concentration is considered a common factor of awareness - it arises to some degree in
every moment of consciousness. Little bits of knowing at all times.
● If this were not so then we would not be able to connect with any object
● However it is usually weak or unstable - mind is flighty
● Typical to have Trains of associated thought, papancha
● Mind can be trained thru skillful practice
● Word for meditation in Pali is “bhavana” - “causing to be developed”
● Concentration gets stronger thru mental development
● As a Fixed-object concentration focuses on a single object
● 40 traditional objects of meditation - body/breath, brahmaviharas, kasinas (colored
disks), body parts, Triple Gem, and the paramis which are a list of qualities that
Buddhism advocates for bringing the path of awakening into everyday life.
● These fixed objects of meditation are said to lead to the jhanas
● Meditation on changing objects (moment-to-moment) does not apparently lead to
absorption but can reveal various vipassana insights (impermanence).
Jhana, States of Concentration:
● Includes the other factors for awakening
● Jhana - means “to meditate”
● Contemplation of a single object & contemplation of the characteristics of phenomena.
(Vipassana Jhanas). Anicca, Anatta, Dukkha
● Also known as absorptions.

● According to Joseph Goldstein various teachers have different views on what these are
and how to develop them.
● Questions over whether we loose all awareness of the body or is there still awareness of
subtle body energies.
● Some emphasis development of Nimita (signs, colored disks)
● Some say insight can be developed in jhana and some say you can’t. I am of the belief
that one can be in jhana and develop insight. Like tuning to a slightly different frequency
or difference focus as if the mind was a microscope
● Anicca, anatta, dukkha
● “For one who is concentrated, one knows and sees things as they really are.”
● There are some descriptions of the development of jhana separate from its

● 1st Development of Concentration - “A Pleasant abiding” - Blameless kind of happiness -

● 2nd Development - known as having Knowledge & Vision - Divine Eye - 4th Jhana.

The Theravada Pali texts mention four kinds of samadhi:
● Momentary, Preliminary, Access and Absorption:
● Momentary concentration (khanikasamadhi): The stabilization of the mind which arises
during vipassana - otherwise known as insight. The object of vipassana meditation is the
present moment and what is being perceived in the present moment. This can be seen
as an entry point in Vipassana practice itslef. Also, once the other forms of concentration
are well established we come back to vipassana as method of gaining insight into reality.
● Preliminary or preparatory concentration (parikammasamadhi): Arises out of the
meditator's initial attempts to focus on a meditation object and proceeds “Access
Concentration”.
● Access concentration (upacarasamadhi): Is a state of concentration where access to
jhana is available. It arises when the five hindrances are dispelled, and with the
appearance of the 'counterpart sign' (patibhaganimitta). The counterpart sign, or nimitas,
are certain mental images, perceptions, or sensations which indicate that jhana is

present, that you are experiencing a refined state of mental awareness. I have been
aware of it in a few different ways, as a feeling, seeing colors, other sensations that are
hard to describe but you know they are unique.
● Absorption concentration (appanasamadhi): The total immersion of the mind on its
meditation of object and stabilization of all four jhanas. The mind is totally immersed in
the object.

Meditators can eventually attain access concentration using either type of momentary concentration
practice—samatha or vipassanā.
However, samatha practices are more likely to lead to access concentration because of their more
stable nature.
As I mentioned, access concentration is characterized by the significant reduction or complete
dropping of the five hindrances and the arising and strengthening of the jhāna factors.

A few more comments about Momentary Concentration and Absorption Concentration:
● Although 4 kinds mentioned in texts two are more emphasized as their ability to provide
insight
● Momentary concentration (vipassana) or Absorbtion (Jhana)
● Momentary - like swimming -
● Absorption - like carried in a boat
● Noted it is quicker and more enjoyable for those in a boat. But not always so.
● Dry insight (also known as bare insight) - insight without jhana - wisdom alone - insight into

● Wet insight - insight with jhana
● Momentary or Absorbtion not exclusive - you can experience either or both at various times.

● It will wax and wane at different times
Qualities of the rupa jhānas[edit]
Jhanas are deep meditative states where one, beginning with Access Concentration, refines the
meditation to that the mind is absorbed by its object and comes to a state of balance in the
practice.
There are four stages of rupa jhāna. Rupa referring to jhana based on being in a form. As well as
four stages of the arupa- jhanas based on the non-material or formless realm).
Each jhāna is characterised by a set of qualities which are present in that jhana.
● First jhana: can be entered when one is secluded from sensuality and unskillful qualities
(the hindrances). There is pīti ("rapture") and non-sensual sukha ("pleasure") as the
result of seclusion, with vitarka-vicara ("discursive thought") continues;
● Second jhana: there is pīti ("rapture") and non-sensual sukha ("pleasure") as the result of
concentration (samadhi-ji, "born of samadhi"[23]

); ekaggata (unification of awareness) is

free from vitarka-vicara ("discursive thought"); inner tranquility;[21][note 4]

● Third dhyana: upekkhā[note 5]

(equanimous; "affective detachment"[21]

), mindful, and

alert, and senses pleasure with the body;
● Fourth dhyana: upekkhāsatipārisuddhi[note 5]

(purity of equanimity and mindfulness);
neither-pleasure-nor-pain. Traditionally, the fourth jhāna is seen as the beginning of
attaining psychic powers (abhijñā).

Each jhāna factor neutralizes a specific hindrance, as follows:
1. Applied attention (vitakka) calms sense desire.
2. Sustained attention (vicāra) pacifies ill will / aversion.
3. Joy (pīti) vanquishes sloth and torpor.
4. Bliss (sukha) eliminates restlessness and remorse.
5. One-pointedness (ekaggatā) overcomes doubt.

The four arupa-jhaas are:
● fifth jhāna: infinite space (Pali ākāsānañcāyatana, Skt. ākāśānantyāyatana),
● sixth jhāna: infinite consciousness (Pali viññāṇañcāyatana, Skt. vijñānānantyāyatana),
● seventh jhāna: infinite nothingness (Pali ākiñcaññāyatana, Skt. ākiṃcanyāyatana),
● eighth jhāna: neither perception nor non-perception (Pali nevasaññānāsaññāyatana, Skt.
naivasaṃjñānāsaṃjñāyatana).
