As we have progressed through the [[4 Foundations of Mindfulness]], I have gotten this feeling that
we are being taken on a journey - a journey deeper and deeper into our world of experience --
as seen through the eyes of the buddha. This journey is like “The Fantastic Voyage” but in this
case being a journey which takes us from the basics of inhabiting a body to opening to the world
of being and yet not limited by it.
In a talk by [[Gil Fronsdhal]] last year, he provided three examples of how meditation practice can
work on us. In the first he describes our minds as being like a lamp covered with tape. At first,
if you turn on the light, and you flip on the switch, you cannot see anything because it is
obscured by the tape. But through meditation, we start to unwind the tape so that it shows more
and more light until it is very bright. This is similar to what our minds are like when we are freed
from the Hindrances of sense-desire, ill-will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry and doubt.
In the second example, and one we have talked about here a few times, our minds are like a
still forest pool that is naturally clear and peaceful but has been trampled on by the disturbances
of the world so that it looks muddied and disheveled. As we meditate, the soot and mud settle
down to the bottom revealing this pristine clarity of our minds.
And in the third, and one that I have experienced on Vision Quest, is the example of
backpacking in the wilderness and then being able to take off your pack and to hike without it.
Meditation is like this, a practice where we can drop our burdens. As a result we feel lighter and
vibrant and free from the things that weigh us down.
The Hindrances are our preoccupations, the mind-states that obscure this innate clarity of mind,
that blinds us. Chasing our desires, or pushing away the things we dislike, being lethargic, not
motivated or disconnected, filled with resistance or doubt.
In the next section in the 4th Foundation of Mindfulness, Mindfulness of Mind-Objects, we
explore that that describes to us what happens as the awakening factors come into play. These
are the mind states that arise when these hindrances are abated. And, as has been pointed out
previously, our task is simply to know when they are present or not.
So here is how it reads:
(4. The Seven Enlightenment Factors)
42. “Again, bhikkhus, a bhikkhu abides contemplating mind-objects as mind-objects in terms of
the seven enlightenment factors. And how does a bhikkhu abide contemplating mind-objects as
mind-objects in terms of the seven enlightenment factors? Here, there being the mindfulness
enlightenment factor in him, a bhikkhu understands: ‘There is the mindfulness enlightenment
factor in me’; or there being no mindfulness enlightenment factor in him, he understands: ‘There
is no mindfulness enlightenment factor in me’; and he also understands how there comes to be
the arising of the unarisen mindfulness enlightenment factor, and how the arisen mindfulness
enlightenment factor comes to fulfilment, and how the arisen mindfulness enlightenment factor
comes to fulfilment by development. “

There being the investigation-of-states enlightenment factor in him... There being the energy
enlightenment factor in him... There being the rapture enlightenment factor in him... There
being the tranquillity enlightenment factor in him... There being the concentration enlightenment
factor in him... There being the equanimity enlightenment factor in him, a bhikkhu understands:
‘There is the equanimity enlightenment factor in me’; or there being no equanimity
enlightenment factor in him, he understands: ‘There is no equanimity enlightenment factor in
me’; and he also understands how there comes to be the arising of the unarisen equanimity
enlightenment factor, and how the arisen equanimity enlightenment factor comes to fulfilment by
development.
43. “In this way he abides contemplating mind-objects as mind-objects internally, externally, and
both internally and externally... And he abides independent, not clinging to anything in the
world. That is how a bhikkhu abides contemplating mind-objects as mind-objects in terms of the
seven enlightenment factors.
So here we have these Seven Factors of Enlightenment. which is itself called satta bojjhaṅgā.
There is:
1. Mindfulness - the pali word sati, which means to maintain awareness of reality
2. Investigation of Mental Objects - the pali phrase dhamma vicaya
3. Energy - known as viriya - which also evokes determination and effort
4. Joy or rapture - the pali word pīti
5. Calm or relaxation or tranquillity - the pali word passaddhi
6. Concentration (samādhi)- a one-pointed state of mind or clear awareness
7. Equanimity (upekkha) - accepting reality as-it-is without craving or aversion.
So what is it that we are doing here? So much of what we have reviewed has been to
understand the foundations of mindfulness. It can appear that this mindfulness is solely a
practice, a doing, but this is not necessarily true. After we get past the mechanics of the
practice, as we settle into the practice, we can begin to understand that mindfulness is not a
practice but a state of mind. It is not so much as what to do but what we allow. There are
things we do and things we allow. It is a balancing act. Our job is to listen and to let go, to
know and to let be. And, as we do this, what we fall into is the present moment.
We tune to the rhythm of the breathing. If we follow too hard we exhaust ourselves or cause
ourselves pain. If we do not follow enough then we fall into sleepyness and dullness. If we can
let go of our preoccupations and tune into the moment, then what rises are these awakening
factors.
Our task is simply to recognize when they arise. All we can do is work on the causes and
conditions that allow them to arise. You cannot do them better. All we can do is give them
space to manifest.
Mindfulness practice is recognition. It is a state of awareness. When it is present, our task is to
get out of the way, to allow. This field of awareness can feel delicious. As we get a taste of its
clarity, its clear knowing - our mind can become preoccupied with it. Anything we become
mindful of can seem delightful. We become curious to see the nature of things - things we

usually would prefer to avoid or become attach too. Pain, for example, can be investigated. As
we look closely at pain we can then come to see it as it is -not as a single object but the arising
and passing away of numerous objects with no central point. When we see thoughts, we begin
to see them as thoughts on a train that pass us by or as bubbles that arise and float away.
What happens is the anarchy of the mind that we began with starts to become something
simple. We begin to see things with clarity. The mind gets more interested. We fall into the
stream of engagement.
It’s like being really interested in what you are doing, we become absorbed. We become
engergized.
When we are working in harmony with our being - joy arises. It can be a lighter type of joy that is
uplifting or it can be very intense like feeling high. When I have experienced this, it was more
like an embodied joy, not just some mental feeling. This Joy then can shift into one of great
calm. This too being a physical feeling.
Gil says that it doesn’t feel that it is something he is doing but something that is being done to
him. With this calmness there arises this feeling of not being interested in anything else. We
become concentrated, we become even more absorbed - one-pointed mindfulness. The is
neither pleasure nor pain. Our minds become clear and empty. After this can come the most
sublime state of all, one of equanimity. It has been described as the best emotion that you can
have. It is a mind that is not plain or dull but also one that is not reactive. We would be
surprised how well the mind operates when it is not in the mode of reactivity. It is a mind that
does not create waves.
This state of mind then gives rise to something quite interesting, actually two things. The first is
that the mind softens - instead of being clenched. It is not tense or tight. The mind is then able
to let go further. It has no filters and does not conceptualize what is going on. It is simply
present. It is a mind that is primed for deep insight, for release, for liberation.
This is a perfect setup for the 4 Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path that we will present
here in the coming weeks.
A couple of notes from the Majjhima Nikaya which are the Middle Length Discourses that the
Satipatthana Sutta is taken from.
The description I have previously given above is how I might describe how the Seven Factors of
Awakening appear in terms of our meditation practice. There is another description of how these
Seven Enlightenment Factors unfold in a progressive sequence through a related teaching
called the Anapanasati Sutta. You will notice that the sequence will begin with contemplation of
the body, the first foundation of mindfulness, and then repeat using the sequence with the other
three foundations of mindfulness - feeling, mindfulness of mind and mindfulness of mind-states.
(FULFILMENT OF THE SEVEN ENLIGHTENMENT FACTORS)
29. “And how, bhikkhus, do the four foundations of mindfulness, developed and cultivated, fulfil
the seven enlightenment factors?

30. “Bhikkhus, on whatever occasion a bhikkhu abides contemplating the body as a body,
ardent, fully aware, and mindful, having put away covetousness and grief for the world (this
being the base mindfulness instructions) — on that occasion unremitting mindfulness is
established in him. On whatever occasion unremitting mindfulness is established in a bhikkhu—
on that occasion the mindfulness enlightenment factor is aroused in him, and he develops it,
and by development, it comes to fulfilment in him.
So we start with basic mindfulness as a practice and as we develop this mindfulness it
then becomes a mental factor in itself.
31. “Abiding thus mindful, he investigates and examines that state with wisdom and embarks
upon a full inquiry into it. On whatever occasion, abiding thus mindful, a bhikkhu investigates
and examines that state with wisdom and embarks upon a full inquiry into it— on that occasion
the investigation-of-states enlightenment factor is aroused in him, and he develops it, and by
development it comes to fulfilment in him.
So this mindfulness factor elicits curiosity in us. As we develop this curiosity of
investigation and examination, it too becomes a mental factor in of itself.
32. “In one who investigates and examines that state with wisdom and embarks upon a full
inquiry into it, tireless energy is aroused. On whatever occasion tireless energy is aroused in a
bhikkhu who investigates and examines that state with wisdom and embarks upon a full inquiry
into it— on that occasion the energy enlightenment factor is aroused in him, and he develops it,
and by development it comes to fulfilment in him.
So as we investigate and examine what we experience this arouses energy in us. This
energy too develops into a mental factor in itself.
33. “In one who has aroused energy, unworldly rapture arises. On whatever occasion unworldly
rapture arises in a bhikkhu who has aroused energy—[ 86] on that occasion the rapture
enlightenment factor is aroused in him, and he develops it, and by development it comes to
fulfilment in him.
Unworldly here means not the joy of experiencing the things we like per say, but spiritual
joy. A pervasive joy that bubbles up and uplifts us as a result of our being connected to
our practice - to being connected to our being - to nature.
34. “In one who is rapturous, the body and the mind become tranquil. On whatever occasion the
body and the mind become tranquil in a bhikkhu who is rapturous— on that occasion the
tranquillity enlightenment factor is aroused in him, and he develops it, and by development it
comes to fulfilment in him.
So this joyful presence leads us to a prevailing tranquillity as a factor.

35. “In one whose body is tranquil and who feels pleasure, the mind becomes concentrated. On
whatever occasion the mind becomes concentrated in a bhikkhu whose body is tranquil and
who feels pleasure— on that occasion the concentration enlightenment factor is aroused in him,
and he develops it, and by development it comes to fulfilment in him.
So this tranquillity leads us to concentration as a factor - being one-pointed in our
awareness.
36. “He closely looks on with equanimity at the mind thus concentrated. On whatever occasion a
bhikkhu closely looks on with equanimity at the mind thus concentrated— on that occasion the
equanimity enlightenment factor is aroused in him, and he develops it, and by development it
comes to fulfilment in him.
So this concentration lead us to a pleasant abiding here and now - one that does not
make waves.
This pattern is then repeated with contemplation of feelings as feelings, contemplating mind as
mind and contemplating mind-objects as mind objects with each of seven enlightenment factors.
Finally the Buddha states that:
40. “Bhikkhus, that is how the four foundations of mindfulness, developed and cultivated, fulfil
the seven enlightenment factors. 1126 [88]
The second note in the Sutta refers to what is meant as “Investigation of states”
(dhammavicaya), the 2nd of the Factors of Awakening. This means to scrutinize the mental and
physical phenomena presented to a meditator’s mind by mindfulness. It implies applying
discernment to body-mind phenomena in order to apply right effort, giving way to entry
into the first jhana. This is the training practice of being with our experience. It is not simply
mental qualities arising and passing away. Although, this, too, is a stage of the path.
The important part in developing this factor is a matter of paying appropriate attention to what is
skillful and abandoning what is not. We look carefully what we are doing in body, speech and
mind, what we have referred to as body fabrication, speech fabrication and mental fabrication.
It implies questioning. It takes into consideration mindfulness of the teachings, your own
experience and your own standards, and if needed also by talking to someone more advanced
on the path. But ultimately, it comes to our asking ourselves, “where is there suffering”.

As with this investigation of states, the Seven Factors of Enlightenment overall are typically
taught as a balancing act between themselves and between them and the Hindrances.
In meditation most of us experience at least two of the five hindrances, those being sloth and
torpor and restlessness and worry. When we are experiencing sloth & torpor, when our
mindfulness appears lacking, it is suggested that we develop joy or rapture, investigation and
energy in order to bring those factors more into play. As was noted by Gil and suggested by the
Visuddhimagga, which is a great treatise on buddhist practice written by Buddhagosa in the 5th
Century, the factors should be developed first by investigation of states, then by energy itself
and then developing joy or rapture.
When we are experiencing restlessness or worry then the suggestion is that we develop more
relaxation, concentration and equanimity - in that order.
The balancing factor is Mindfulness - it is required to remain aware of physical changes as well
as any change of mental state to determine whether they lead us in a skillful or unskillful
direction.
Strong Mindfulness is needed in all instances.
Care should be given to the effort of any of these factors as effort itself is a balance of effort and
no effort. Too much effort can drive away the grace the presents these factors while not enough
or no effort will result in the factors not developing at all. When the causes and conditions are
met, then the factors will eventually present themselves.
When all of these factors are in balance we can then enter into a state of deep concentration
called jhana. Keeping the factor balanced also plays into our ability to maintain the jhanas.
So these Seven are Factors for Awakening. These are factors you develop in order to become
awakened. You can’t take awakening and develop it. You can develop the factors of mind that
will eventually lead to awakening.
This path is build upon very ordinary, day-to-day things, like mindfulness and alertness.
