MMR#54 Right Samadhi/Concentration
So we come to the last topic in the last section that is covered in the Satipatthana Sutta, one of
the two major Suttas directly describing the Foundations of Mindfulness - one of the core
teachings of Buddhism.
The Noble Eightfold Path is the path of practice that the Buddha prescribes as the path that
leads to the cessation of suffering, the 3rd of the 4 Noble Truths. It is comprised of eight
divisions that are further divided into three groups or areas of emphasis. This includes the
practice of sila (morality), practice of samadhi (meditation) and the practice of panna (insight or
wisdom).
We began our exploration of the Noble Eightfold Path by taking a look at the Wisdom Basket
which includes Right View & Right Intention. It is because Wisdom lies at both the beginning
and the ending of the path and is predicated by Right View.
It is with this perspective, that of the conventional understanding of karma & of the ultimate
understanding of the 4 Noble Truths - that we set our intentions, which in turn leads us to right
speech, right action and right livelihood - the moral aspects of the path.
Doing this simplifies our life and minimized the possibilities of remorse, embarrassment and
conflicts with others, which normally would keep the mind churned up.
With these moral aspects of action in the proper perspective we come to place right effort and
right mindfulness leading to right concentration or right meditation.
Ultimately, with a clear mind, and with clear seeing we turn this concentrated mind towards
insight & understanding of the nature of reality from our direct experience - leading to Nibanna.
Today we focus on the last top of the Noble Eightfold Path - that of Right Samadhi or
Concentration.

Samadhi
Samadhi is a state of meditative consciousness - of absorption that is attained by the practice of
jhana. It refers to the development of a luminous mind which is both equanimous and mindful.
● Equanimity - Upekkha - is one of the sublime states (Brahma vihara) that manifests as
an unwavering presence in the face of worldly conditions as represented by the
metaphor of the mind as a still pond that is clear, pure, pristine, and still.
● Mindful - Sati - being characterized as receptive attention and consciousness of what
one is doing in the moment while keeping in mind the dhamma, the teachings, the
elements of the path - a path whose whole purpose is for the surmounting of suffering.
In terms of the Noble Eightfold path, it is said that the first seven practices are requisites for right
samadhi. By living our lives in harmony with ourselves and others we create an environment
conducive to the pursuit of wholesome meditative attainments.
Lee Brazington, in his book Right Concentration: A Practical Guide to the Jhanas, condenses
the Buddhist path in this way. He says "these four preliminary practices of: keeping the
precepts, guarding the senses, maintaining mindfulness, and being content with little are

“off-the-cushion” practices that you need to make the four cornerstones of your basic way of
life."
The goal of meditation is to develop clarity and insight in the nature of reality - to seeing into
dukkha, anicca and anatta, to discard negative states and to dispel avidya (ignorance).

Right Samadhi
● Right samadhi (Passaddhi; Ekaggata; sampasadana): is practicing the four stages of jhana.
● In the Theravada tradition and the Vipassana movement, this is interpreted as ekaggata,
concentration or one-pointedness of the mind,
● This is supplemented with Vipassana-meditation, where we turn our minds towards the
nature of reality - to insight.
● The term jhāna (Skt. dhyāna) is derived two verbs - the first jhayati, which means "to think or
meditate,"
● It is also derived from the verb jhapeti, which means "to burn up," which explains it fun its
function, namely of burning up opposing states, burning up or destroying "the mental
defilements that prevent the development of serenity and insight.
● It is commonly translated as meditation, is more often equated with "concentration,"
● Meditation, though, refers to a wider range of exercises for bhāvanā, or spiritual
development, which can include reflection and contemplation.
● Samadhi, as concentration, means ‘to collect’ or ‘bring together’.
● Often translated as ‘unification of mind’
● It also associated with the term Samatha - which is a calm abiding due to its close
association with equanimity.
● In the suttas is defined as ‘one-pointedness of mind’ (cittass’ekaggata)
● This is the centering of consciousness and aspects of consciousness evenly and rightly on a
single object - undistracted and unscattered.
● What makes this concentration unique is it is not a type of concentration directed to an
object, such as a goal or a task, but is a state of awareness that ends of without any object
or subject, and ultimately onto nothingness and emptiness.
● Attention is fixed on an object for a sustained period of time and we come to get to know the
object deeply - to a point of absorption with sustained interest.
● This type of concentration, otherwise known as Jhana, is series of meditative states of
profound stillness and concentration in which the mind becomes fully immersed and
absorbed in the chosen object of attention.
● This is the cornerstone of Right Concentration.

Practice
Here I will provide a definition of Jhana Practice thru the use of similes from the Sama-dhange Sutta
- The Factors of Concentration.
[FIRST JHANA]
"There is the case where one — quite withdrawn from sensuality, withdrawn from unskillful qualities
— enters and remains in the first jhana: rapture and pleasure born from withdrawal, accompanied by
directed thought and evaluation. One permeates and pervades, suffuses and fills one's very body
with the rapture and pleasure born from withdrawal. There is nothing of one’s entire body
unpervaded by rapture and pleasure born from withdrawal.

"Just as if a skilled bathperson or bathperson's apprentice would pour bath powder into a brass
basin and knead it together, sprinkling it again and again with water, so that one’s ball of bath
powder — saturated, moisture-laden, permeated within and without — would nevertheless not drip;
even so, the monk permeates, suffuses and fills this very body with the rapture and pleasure born of
withdrawal. There is nothing of one's entire body unpervaded by rapture and pleasure born from
withdrawal...
● Withdrawing from sensuality means having solitude, getting away from daily worries, putting
spiritual practice to the fore of our interests.
● Withdrawn from unskillful qualities means having subdued the hindrances. It is a deepening
of solitude. Facing our habitual patterns. Practice of skillful means.
● How might we remove the hindrances...? Dismissal, Replacement which can be samadhi or
some sort of spiritual thoughts such as the bramivihara’s (compassion, loving-kindness,
sympathetic joy and equanimity) - countering anger and resentment).
● There is also bringing into awareness of compassion for your future self. Hiri-ottapa - which
is the wholesome fear and concern for who you are going to become - It is the process of
letting go of the present comfort for the person who you are going to become.
● This can provide us with the impetus to place effort to being with and letting go of factors that
are hindering our growth. We think of our long-term happiness instead of holding onto the
present processes that have been preoccupying our being. Letting go of our story, letting go
of our habitual tendencies and venturing into the unknown..
● Directed thought and evaluation is part of the investigation aspect of the 7 Factors of
awakening, which takes into account our present experience and our intention to awakening.
It is taking an interest in our experience. A Curiosity. Placing balanced effort into our practice.
● Rapture and pleasure born of withdrawal --- Withdrawal here means withdrawal from
external stimulation. It is almost as if, under the circumstances, seclusion and abeyance of
greed, and hatred can bring about a joyous presence in itself.
● Actively desiring to experience a jhāna is not being secluded from the unwholesome mind
state of craving.
● The type of efforting that I find so endearing in crossing the floods is applicable here.

● Effort born of natural curiosity, interest, patience, required.
● This involves initially bringing about what is called access concentration, enough
concentration that keeps the hindrances at bay but allows us to access single-pointed
absorption as described in the simile.
● we can define access concentration as being fully with the meditation object, and if there are
thoughts, they are wispy and in the background and don’t pull you away into distraction.
● The general method for generating access concentration is to put your attention on a suitable
meditation object, and when your attention wanders off, gently bring it back. Keep doing this
until the distractions fade away and your attention on the object is unwavering.
● This is the formula we used as instructions for our base meditation practice.
● We don’t focus on, or even think about, the destination but remain in the present moment,
allowing the mystery to unfold.
[SECOND JHANA]
"Furthermore, with the stilling of directed thoughts & evaluations, one enters and remains in the
second jhana: rapture and pleasure born of composure, unification of awareness free from directed
thought and evaluation — internal assurance. One permeates and pervades, suffuses and fills this
very body with the rapture and pleasure born of composure. There is nothing of one's entire body
unpervaded by rapture and pleasure born of composure.

"Just like a lake with spring-water welling up from within, having no inflow from east, west, north, or
south, and with the skies periodically supplying abundant showers, so that the cool fount of water
welling up from within the lake would permeate and pervade, suffuse and fill it with cool waters, there
being no part of the lake unpervaded by the cool waters; even so, the monk permeates and
pervades, suffuses and fills this very body with the rapture and pleasure born of composure. There is
nothing of one's entire body unpervaded by rapture and pleasure born of composure...
● The stilling is not something that you force upon you. We relax. Our mindfulness deepens.
● This is Rapture and pleasure born of composure.
[THIRD JHANA]
"And furthermore, with the fading of rapture, one remains equanimous, mindful, & alert, and senses
pleasure with the body. He enters & remains in the third jhana, of which the Noble Ones declare,
'Equanimous & mindful, one has a pleasant abiding.' One permeates and pervades, suffuses and
fills this very body with the pleasure divested of rapture, so that there is nothing of one's entire body
unpervaded with pleasure divested of rapture.

"Just as in a blue-, white-, or red-lotus pond, there may be some of the blue, white, or red lotuses
which, born and growing in the water, stay immersed in the water and flourish without standing up
out of the water, so that they are permeated and pervaded, suffused and filled with cool water from
their roots to their tips, and nothing of those blue, white, or red lotuses would be unpervaded with
cool water; even so, the monk permeates and pervades, suffuses and fills this very body with the

pleasure divested of rapture. There is nothing of one's entire body unpervaded with pleasure
divested of rapture...

[FOURTH JHANA]
"And furthermore, with the abandoning of pleasure and stress — as with the earlier disappearance of
elation and distress — one enters and remains in the fourth jhana: purity of equanimity and
mindfulness, neither-pleasure-nor-pain. One sits, permeating the body with a pure, bright
awareness, so that there is nothing of one's entire body unpervaded by pure, bright awareness.

"Just as if a man were sitting wrapped from head to foot with a white cloth so that there would be no
part of one's body to which the white cloth did not extend; even so, the monk sits, permeating one's
body with a pure, bright awareness. There is nothing of one's entire body unpervaded by pure, bright
awareness."

— AN 5.28
