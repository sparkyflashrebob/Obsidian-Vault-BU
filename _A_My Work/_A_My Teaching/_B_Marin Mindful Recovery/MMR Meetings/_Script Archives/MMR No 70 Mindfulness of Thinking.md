Adapted from a talk by Jack Kornfield

In covering this topic I will meld some of the teachings that have been presented in the MMTCP

The one great meditation master of the forest tradition, Ajahn Buddhadasa, was asked how the
modern world appeared to him from his remote forest monastery, and he said, "Lost in thought."
That was the description.
The intention here at Marin Mindful Recovery is to create a safe space that brings joy or ease or
well-being. It allows us to quiet the mind and open our hearts - if not here in the class itself but
to carry this outside of our group gathering and into the world.
The point being that when we are not thinking that other dimensions become available - things
such as love, connection, presence, resilience for example - things that do not have anything to
do with the dimension of thought. Also, to strengthen our relationship to the one who knows -
our innate alertness of being.
We know this when we are in nature - when we walk among the trees or work in our gardens,
listen and watch the birds, smell the fresh air - connect with the aliveness of life itself. None of
this has to world of thought. Yet, at the same time, it is critical that we are aware of the
dimension of thought, that “lost in thought” capacity and to understand this ability that human
beings have to think.
The basic instruction of the Satipatthana Sutta is the phrase “with mind to observe the mind”.
With mindfulness or loving awareness, we can become the one who knows the witness that
observes the workings of the mind. For many of us this is revolutionary.
When we start to pay attention in this way, we can start to see and become familiar with the
inner landscape of the thoughts and beliefs that run our world. It's pretty much constant. The
mind secretes thoughts the way the salivary gland secretes saliva. It just does, and then they
keep coming through-- words and images and ideas and visions and memories and plans.
And they create much of our world. All the roads and bridges and cities and language and

medicine and clothing and timepieces and chairs and governments, and all these things that we
live within, were ideas in someone's mind and in someone else's mind. And so mind is both
creative in enormous ways, and as we can see in the world, mind also can be destructive.
Understanding our minds is critical to our happiness and suffering.
"Who is your enemy?" said the Buddha. "Mind is your enemy. No one can harm you more than a
mind untrained. Who is your friend? Mind is your friend. No one can assist you and care for you
better than your mind well-trained. Not even the most loving mother or father."
Khyentse Rinpoche, an extraordinary Tibetan teacher, writes, "Mind creates both samsara and
nirvana. Yet there's nothing much to it. It's just thoughts."
One of the first insights that come to people, this being sometimes called "insight meditation," is
called "seeing the waterfall." When we sit quietly and sense their breath and body and notice
what's happening. Many of us complain, "I couldn't stop thinking. I was planning, I was
remembering, I was judging, I was imagining, I was analyzing, and there was this huge stream
of thoughts." For some people, that is an enormous moment of clarity, just to realize how much
is going on there because they're so identified with it.
We can begin to realize that the thought of our mother is not their mother. You might remember
back to when we were talked about this when discuss sankhara’s. This is a really profound thing
to say, but a lot of people don't know the difference. And we can take years of our life following
loyalty to misguided thoughts. Nations do it, societies do it in child-rearing. Carrying them and
believing them.
Jack Kornfield tells the story in his Power of Awareness course that speaks to the kind of
conditioning that we have. "'Am I gorgeous?' my child asks, drawing the word out like pulled
taffy. 'Yes,' I say, 'you are. The pink and teal dress is probably made of some highly flammable
material, some chemist's approximation of satin. Pudgy fingers decorated with pink polish trace
the sequins on the bodice. 'I love this.' A giant pair of bubblegum pink wings flap slowly. Little
feet dance and sparkly red slippers. 'I'm just like a real princess!'
'Yes,' I say, 'you are.' Curly hair, joyful smile, flawless skin. This child is the epitome of beauty.
This child, my son. He's four years old and prefers to wear dresses. Maybe it's a phase, maybe
not. Even as I wonder how I produced such an angelic-looking creature, I wish he would put on
some pants and go back to playing with toy tractors. Not because it matters to me-- it doesn't,
but because I'm already hearing in my head the name-calling he will face in kindergarten.
Many adults already seem disturbed by the dresses and utter awkward apologies when they
realize he's not female. This culture wants little boys to dream only of baseball, trucks, and
trains. This culture has no room for little boys who want to be gorgeous.
He picks up a parasol a neighbor gave him and opens it jauntily over his shoulders. 'Am I
beautiful?' he asks. And I sweep him into my arms and plant a kiss on his cheeks. 'Always,' I
say. 'Always.'"

And each time I read it and hear it, there's a kind of tenderness to think of every child that's born
with this beautiful, innocent spirit, and the different ways that our conditioning and thoughts start
to occur. How prejudice and misguided understanding can shrink and diminish and judge and
limit a human being. But it's not who we are.
When we begin to be aware of the mind, we begin to have the doorway of liberation opened --
to see that the beliefs and the ways they structure the world are not necessarily the way that it
is.
One time Ajahn Chah and a group of monks were walking in the countryside. Maybe it was out
on alms round. At one point he said, "See that big boulder over there in the field? Is it heavy?"
We said, “yes”.
He said, "Not if you don't pick it up."
And there was so much in that little moment of how we hold it and imagine it in whether we pick
it up or not. And thoughts can serve us in amazing and beautiful ways, but they can also
constrict us and imprison us.
Carlos Castaneda, he says, "You talk to yourself too much. You're not unique in that-- every one
of us does. We maintain our world with our inner dialogue, and yet a man or woman of
knowledge is aware that the world will change completely as soon as they stop talking to
Themselves."
What do we say in this inner world? What's liberating is the gateway to liberation-- is to see and
know the inner thought landscape.
So let's do that little 30-second or one-minute practice. You don't have to change your posture
particularly, but when you're ready, just let your eyes close, and what we're going to do is count
thoughts, all right?
And so you're going to sit for at least half a minute or a minute, and notice picture thoughts,
word thoughts, whatever kind of thoughts they are, and simply number them. Now the numbers
are a thought, but if you count the numbers, that's called the cat chasing its tail, all right? You're
going to sit and wait like the cat at the mouse hole, and the little thoughts will come out. But
they're sneaky. They might come up from behind and say, "There haven't been many thoughts
yet, have there?"
Just let yourself sit and see what happens. On your mark, get set, go.
How many thoughts did you count?

It is also my understanding that some people think through their bodies. There is some research

What becomes liberating is to begin to notice the thought process itself. As we walk down the
street or we engage with people - noticing this inner dialog going on - the way we talk to
ourselves - this on-going commentary and planning. Most of this is below the line of
consciousness - referring to The Hero’s Journey from Joseph Campbell - there being a small
portion of what we are aware of and a much greater area of our being that is unknown - in our
subconscious.
We can become aware of our style of thinking - our patterns of thinking. If they are stories about
the future then those stories have a kind of anxiety in them - trying to manage and predict the
outcome - many continuing on with these fortellings and creating worlds out of them that we can
live from. Thoughts of the past? Many of these have themes of grief or love or guilt or
nostalgia.
Many of these are reruns - old news - scenarios that we keep running through our minds.
Our thoughts of judgement - how we think the world should be. This inner judge and jury. An
inner border wall. An area of inquisition. Idi Amin.
It is better to not think that we are falling short, for if we are at war with our thoughts, we will be
at war for the rest of our life. It is better to have an attitude towards our thoughts of interest,
curiousity and humor.
When we are in monkey mind, we never sit still. When we are having misguided thooughts -
how we relate to them can make the difference.
And at the same time, we have within us this enormous capacity to love. Those judging
thoughts have tried to protect us, they were learned in there-- you know whose voice that is, we
won't talk about them, right?
They can be parents, teachers, or whatever. They were learned, in some way, as beliefs to try to
make us safe. But they also keep us from the redwoods and the clouds in the sky.
So we learn to turn away from the ruthless inner critic. And as begin to look with the awareness,
with loving awareness into the mind itself with this there comes a shift. Nisarga-datta, an Indian
Guru said, "The mind creates the abyss and the heart crosses it."
So how do we go about this?

One thing we can do is to name the thoughts - these whispers in the mind. When we are aware
of them in meditation we can say “thank you, I am okay now”. We shouldn’t get into a struggle
with thoughts.
We notice repetitive thoughts and the top 10 tunes. Thoughts that seem to repeat every so
often, either when thinking about a particular topic - like politics - or our families or our work
environments.
In meditation frequently when we turn to being mindful and aware of thought - those thoughts
will dissolve like a cloud in sunlight.
Tara Brach reminds us to see when we are in the trance; when we are experiencing obsessive
thinking, when we are judging, when we are looking for ways to distract ourselves, and ways in
which we are speeding from one thing to the next - where we are not seeing how we are acting
and thinking.
We can learn to normalize our thoughts. It is in our nature to think. That is what the mind does.
We are not here to make thinking itself a problem. The mind has no pride.
But there are practical ways to do it. We might add “this is an interesting story” and not be
identified with it. I frequently laugh at some of the thoughts that come up in me.
So we can learn to hold our thoughts lightly - to see them for what they are. They are just
thoughts
One of the great Taosit sages said “ "If a man is crossing a river--" or a woman crossing a river--
"and an empty boat collides with their skiff, even though they'd be a bad-tempered person, they
will not be shouting and not be angry. But if there's a man in the other boat, they will shout at
them to steer clear and shout again angrily, and all because there is someone in the boat. Yet if
you can empty your own boat crossing the river of the world, no one will oppose you and no one
will seek to harm you."
The Buddha once said “ Those who are wise are free from clinging to opinions.
For them, the heart and mind is liberated. There are no ties, no entanglement, no folly. But those
who grasp at views and opinions, they wander about the world annoying people.
For me this is one of the greatest teachings - not believing the thoughts.
Jack tells the story of a 6th Grader who wrote him,
"Dear Jack, I came to your meditation center with my school. When I went, I did not take any of
the meditating seriously--" spelled C-E-R-I-S-L-Y-- "till I started to get in big fights with my
parents and myself. I recalled the time we spent meditating and how good it felt, because I was
quiet in my own little world. So I took the little knowledge I got about meditating one night after a

I tried to do what you told us to do, and when I opened my eyes and went back into my house, I

me."
In being mindful of thought there are three (3) levels of awareness we can bring to the knowing:
a. We can notice the content - planning mind , worried mind , judging mind, creative mind
or the like - this is the landscape
b. As we settle back into loving awareness we can shift the attention from content to
process - As we name the thoughts we can see them as they dissolve like clouds under
sunlight - We can being to notice that thoughts are unsubstantial - - like a star at dawn -
a flash of lightening in a summer cloud - an echo - a rainbow - a dream - they are not
real - they come and go - and with seeing this process of thoughts arising and passing
there comes liberation from thought.
c. We then can begin to shift the attention to the knowing - loving awareness - who you are
is loving awareness. Who you are is consciousness itself that was born into this body.
And you shift from the content to seeing the process to becoming the clear space of
awareness that's the witness of it all.
We can begin to live in the heart - fulfilling our longing to connect in a deep way. To develop a
presence that helps others.
It's then possible to play with the thoughts. Not in a way that's struggling against them, but to
replace them in different ways. The phrase from the Dalai Lama when he teaches this, he says,
"Some of your thoughts do not have your best interest in mind. So why would you continue to

think them?" And you can instead put in thoughts of metta and lovingkindness or self-
compassion or gratitude or joy or peace.

We can develop a mind that is open and spacious - like looking at space - the stars and galaxies
are there. We can open to the mystery. It is your home.
