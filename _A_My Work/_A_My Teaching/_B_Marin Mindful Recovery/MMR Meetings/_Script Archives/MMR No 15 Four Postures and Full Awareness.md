Continuing our discussion on the Satipatthan Sutta on the Foundations of Mindfulness, ... the
capacity for attending to the content of our experience as it revealed in the immediate present.
This sutta is the single most important lesson on the practice conducive to insight. Its purpose is
to train the mind to see with microscopic precision the true nature of the body, feelings, states of
mind, and mental objects.
This practice is designed to arouse wisdom or insight but the techniques can produce deep
states of concentration and states of bliss.
It is frequently described as the Gradual Training. This is a practice. It is not something that is
exclusive to the wealthy, or the powerful - or those most fortunate. In fact, many of us are drawn
to this path as a result of great suffering, either mentally or physically. There is no need to
believe in anything, nor nothing to gain. The truth of the nature of reality is unveiled to us - we
awaken to our true nature.

This week I bring in the next couple of paragraphs in the Sattipatthana, which expands on the
definition of Mindfulness to include applying mindfulness to the four postures.
(2. The Four Postures)
6. “Again, bhikkhus, when walking, a bhikkhu understands: ‘I am walking’; when standing, he
understands: ‘I am standing’; when sitting, he understands: ‘I am sitting’; when lying down, he
understands: ‘I am lying down’; or he understands accordingly however his body is disposed.
Ajaan Lee Dhammadharo who is considered one of the greatest teachers and meditation
masters, and was a teacher of one of my primary teachers, Thanissaro Bhikkhu, said that
meditating in four postures is simply to provide rest and relief for the body. The meditation
practice is essentially the same no matter what posture, being aware of your object of
meditation at all times.
Beginners should devote most of their time to two postures: sitting and walking as the two other
postures are not very conductive to collecting the mind. By meditating on the two postures of
sitting and walking concentration will come easily.
Standing and lying meditation aren't very conductive to collecting the mind. When lying down
concentration can easily turn into sleep. When standing the mind has trouble settling snugly
down.
But once you are skilled and find that the posture is no obstacle in reaching concentration, there
is nothing against you dividing your time among the four postures. If you can meditate with
every breath, so much the better.
There are different names for different lying down postures but I will not mention them here
because they are mostly negative. The point is that you can shift around as you like to relieve
feelings of weariness.
But when you decide to meditate in earnest, you should return to the correct posture, establish
mindfulness and then watch over the mind to keep it firm and uncompromising until it reaches
concentration.

In the 3rd Section of the Satipatthana the Buddha expands of this awareness of the body to
include full awareness in our actions.
(3. Full Awareness) 8. “Again, bhikkhus, a bhikkhu is one who acts in full awareness when going
forward and returning; who acts in full awareness when looking ahead and looking away; who
acts in full awareness when flexing and extending his limbs; who acts in full awareness when
wearing his robes and carrying his outer robe and bowl; who acts in full awareness when eating,
drinking, consuming food, and tasting; who acts in full awareness when defecating and
urinating; who acts in full awareness when walking, standing, sitting, falling asleep, waking up,
talking, and keeping silent.
I find this to be a very powerful and direct statement on what it is meant to be aware. It is
awareness of ALL what we are doing - ALL our activities, and not awareness of the content of
our actions.
Again, the Buddha is referring to Sampajañña, know as “clear comprehension” which is
categorized in the commentaries into four types: The 1st is full awareness of the purpose of
one’s action; 2) full awareness of the suitability of one’s means or methods, how are we being
aware; 3rd - full awareness of the domain, that is, not abandoning the subject of meditation
during one’s daily routine; and 4th - full awareness of reality, the knowledge that behind one’s
activities there is no abiding self.

Closing Summary
Just as with mindfulness of the breath, paying attention to the other parts and movements of the
body can benefit our practice and lead to peace and freedom.
Joseph Goldstein, a contemporary of Jack Kornfield, who co-created the Insight Meditation
Society on the East Coast, and who wrote a book on the Satipatthana that we used as a text in
this group, cites several benefits:
It strengthens continuity of awareness.
We can maintain our awareness longer by just noticing as we move from posture to the next
throughout the day.

It reveals our states of mind.
When rushed, our walking posture can reveal this state of mind by our leaning forward as if we
were toppling forward
When we are aware, we are just walking: “When walking, just walk.”
It supports our understanding of the three characteristics.
Impermanence: each movement arises and falls away.
Inability to have lasting satisfaction: we constantly shift positions to be more comfortable as
noted in the saying, “Movement masks dukkha (suffering). Note that the suffering is more
discomfort, a finer form of dissatisfaction or wanting things to be other than they are.
Selfness nature: we see that movement just happens (arises from causes and conditions). As
with shifting the body to avoid discomfort, this just happens. No one is directing the movement.
“As we walk, we can hold in our minds the unspoken question, “Who is walking, and who is
standing?”
Taking refuge in our awareness is a key component to staying spiritually fit and not being swept
away by the world. These are practical practices to use in our daily lives. It can be used as a
protection again addictive behavior.
There is no greater inventory than this I believe. Being aware of our activities in total is to
become fully aware. Each aspect of our awareness can lead to insight into the totality of our
being.
Mindfulness/Awareness is the gateway to the deathless.
