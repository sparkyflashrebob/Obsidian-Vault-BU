The Noble Eightfold path consists of three areas: Sila or Ethics, Samadhi or
Meditation/Concentration and Panna or Wisdom. Last week we talked about Panna - the

What is Sila?

Sila, though, is the cornerstone upon which the entire Noble Eightfold Path is built. Sila means
(virtue, moral conduct).
The practice of sila is defined by the middle three factors of the Eightfold Path which includes:
Right Speech, Right Action, and Right Livelihood.
Buddhism, with its non-theistic framework, grounds its ethics, not on the notion of obedience,
but on that of harmony.
This is from Bhikkhu Bodhi in his book on the Noble Eightfold Path.
‘The observance of Sīla leads to harmony at several levels—social, psychological, kammic, and
contemplative.
At the social level the principles of Sīla help to establish harmonious interpersonal relations,
welding the mass of differently constituted members of society, with their own private interests
and goals, into a cohesive social order in which conflict is reduced.
On a social level we generally follow agreed upon rules of engagement: For example we obey
traffic lights, and get in line at the shopping center. We are generally civil. Whereas if we are in
a war zone or oppressed in some manner then we not safe at all.
At the psychological level Sīla brings harmony to the mind, protection from the inner split caused
by guilt and remorse over moral transgressions.
At the kammic level the observance of Sīla ensures harmony with the cosmic law of kamma, the
understanding that our actions have consequences that will experience either in the here and
now or in the course of future.
And at the fourth level, the contemplative, Sīla helps establish the preliminary purification of
mind to be completed, in a deeper and more thorough way, by the methodical development of
serenity and insight - as we work in the other areas of the path to develop our meditation and
wisdom.

This from the Dhammapada:
“To abstain from all evil, to cultivate the good, and to purify one’s mind—this is the teaching of
the Buddhas” (v. 183).
Precepts
The primary practice of Sila with practicing Buddhists is to voluntarily undertake a particular set
of training rules appropriate to their life-situation:
For instance, for Lay men and women, like us, we would observe the Five Precepts (pañca-sila)
Lay men and women doing intensive meditation practice (as on Uposatha days or on retreat)
would observe the Eight Precepts (attha-sila).
Novice monks (samanera) and nuns (samaneri) observe the Ten Precepts (dasa-sila)
A fully-ordained monk (bhikkhu) follows the 227 rules of the Bhikkhu Patimokkha; a nun
(bhikkhuni) would follow the 311 rules of the Bhikkhuni Patimokkha.
Before we go on I thought I would add some clarity here as it can appear that 311 rules might be
considered unfair to women versus men only having 227 rules:
1. more than one third of these extra rules were formulated to protect bhikkhunīs from
being the direct recipients of the abusive or careless behavior of other bhikkhunīs;
2. two of the extra rules (Pācittiyas 6 and 44) prevent bhikkhunīs from putting themselves in
a position of servitude to bhikkhus or lay people;
3. according to the rules' origin stories, all but three of the extra rules (Pācittiyas 59, 94,
and 95) were formulated only after bhikkhunīs complained to the bhikkhus about an
errant bhikkhunī's behavior.
The Five Precepts are:
1. Not taking life;
2. Not taking what is not given;
3. Not committing sexual misconduct (interpreted as anything less than chastity for the
monk and as sexual conduct contrary to proper social norms, such as adultery, for the
layman);
4. Not engaging in false speech;
5. Not using intoxicants;
The three additional precepts for those involved in intensive meditation practice includes:
6. Not eating after midday;
7. Not participating in worldly amusements;
8. Not adorning the body with ornaments and using perfume;
The two additional precepts for novice monks and nuns includes
9. Not sleeping on high and luxurious beds; and

10. Not accepting gold and silver.
This is the standard way that the precepts are taught.
The Unwholesome Ways to Act
Being concerned that we have to ensure that our actions do not bring harm to ourselves and
others. The way we would do this is by refraining from the ten unwholesome ways to act. They
are:
1. Refraining from taking the life of any being
2. Refraining from taking what is not freely given
3. Refraining from inappropriate sexual conduct
4. Refraining from lying
5. Refraining from divisive speech
6. Refraining from using harsh words
7. Refraining from idle talk (gossip)
8. Refraining from coveting other’s possessions and positions
9. Refraining from resenting the good fortune of others
10. Refraining from holding a closed mind about things one doesn’t fully understand

Right Action
These 10 Unwholesome Ways to Act are parts of all three of the Noble Path that we would
follow in order to realize cessation of suffering . Some of the items on this list refer specifically to
Right Speech, others Right Livelihood.
In terms of Right Action, the focus is primarily with refraining from taking life, from stealing, and
from sexual misconduct. The emphasis here is on our Intentions. So, just as when we are
working the Steps of AA and we slip up, here we look to the step preceding it for guidance. This
is how our awareness of Sila can inform us of how well our Views and Understanding and ways
of thinking might be in proper balance. The proof is in our actions. We know when it is just right
or somehow off a little.
It is unintentional that we take life when we walk around, drive our cars, fly planes, need to eat
and such, but we do. However that is not our intention.
But it is also true that we can deepen our intentions, practice renunciation in a more refined
manner. This would require us to be more present, more mindful in each moment, more
conscious of every step and action we take, so that we can reduce or eliminate the harm that
we cause. Every step of the Noble Eightfold path is a mindfulness practice. Part of this path of
practice that I love to refer to as the knife.

Right Speech
The other steps here follow in this same manner. With Right Speech we can recognize what we
are about to say before we say it. This offers us the freedom to choose when to speak, what to
say, and how to say it. With mindfulness, we see that the heart is the ground from which our
speech grows. We learn to restrain our speech in moments of anger, hostility, or confusion, if
this is our true intention. Over time we learn to train the heart to more frequently incline towards
wholesome states such as love, kindness and empathy. From these heart states Right Speech
naturally arises.

Right Listening
What also compliments this is Right Listening. With Mindfulness, listening is attending to more
that just the content of what others are saying. It also means that we are aware of our physical
sensations, thoughts, and emotions. With others we are aware of their voice, facial
expressions, gestures, pauses, underlying meanings, and other nuances that accompany the
spoken word of others. Mindfulness is a practice of deep listening. Mindful of our inner
environments, our external environment, and both, how they influence each other.
It has been called deep listening by TNH, others “generous listening”, “listening from the heart”.
This listening is both a skill and a way of being. Does this sound familiar to those of us who
follow the ways of Council of the Good Red Road.
When we listen we are providing a gift to others.
With these two together, Right Speech and Right Listening, many people find to be the most
difficult of the precepts.
Right Livelihood
As with all of the Buddhist Path, Right Livelihood encourages us to engage in compassionate
activity, to make our living in a way that does not cause harm and that is ethically positive.
Almost everyone’s life involves an economic dimension. Work and career need to be integrated
into this path. Most of us spend the majority of our waking lives at work, so it is important to
understand how our work affects our mind and heart. How can we approach our work so that it
is meaningful? How can it be a support and not a hindrance to spiritual practice - a place to
deepen our awareness and kindness.
Just as I mentioned with Right Action, we are mindful of our work life, it is helpful for us to follow
an honest occupation which fully respects other people and the natural world. It means being
responsible for the consequences of our action and taking only a fair share of the earth’s
resources.
Here, too, we find that our understanding of how this plays out in our lives can further deepen
our understanding of the practice. The antidote to unskillful livelihood as a way of gaining one’s

living is by doing not harm, by benefitting others, legally, peacefully, honestly, openly, and in
other ways of gaining merit so as to avoid the pitfalls of greed and delusion.

The 5 Gifts (Gift to Ourselves and Others)
In a teaching called the 5 Gifts the Buddha describes the Five faultless gifts benefit us.
"There are these five gifts, five great gifts ..... that are not open to suspicion, will never be open
to suspicion, and are unfaulted by knowledgeable wise ones. Which five?
"There is the case one, abandoning the taking of life, abstains from taking life. In doing so, he
gives freedom from danger, freedom from animosity, freedom from oppression to limitless
numbers of beings.
So this is a gift to limitless beings by abandoning the taking of life.
He goes on.
In giving freedom from danger, freedom from animosity, freedom from oppression to limitless
numbers of beings, he gains a share in limitless freedom from danger, freedom from animosity,
and freedom from oppression.
So this is a shared gift for ourselves by abandoning the taking of life.
This is the first gift.
The other gifts are similarly included when we:
● abandoning taking what is not given (stealing)
● abandoning illicit sex
● abandoning lying
● abandoning the use of intoxicants
— AN 8.39

Four Types of Individuals
Our understanding of the precepts can be seen from many levels that are not usually
considered in this society. How we might support others. As I just mentioned, we can see these
as gifts. Here is another teaching on four types of individuals found to be existing in the world
that further explores how we might understand this in a deeper manner.
Practice the precepts — and support others' practice, too
"Monks, these four types of individuals are to be found existing in the world. Which four?
1. The one who practices for his own benefit but not for that of others.
2. The one who practices for the benefit of others but not for his own.
3. The one who practices neither for his own benefit nor for that of others.
4. The one who practices for his own benefit and for that of others.
"And how is one an individual who practices for his own benefit but not for that of others?
A. There is the case where a certain individual himself abstains from the taking of life but
doesn't encourage others in undertaking abstinence from the taking of life.
B. He himself abstains from stealing but doesn't encourage others in undertaking
abstinence from stealing.
C. He himself abstains from sexual misconduct but doesn't encourage others in undertaking
abstinence from sexual misconduct.
D. He himself abstains from lying but doesn't encourage others in undertaking abstinence
from lying.
E. He himself abstains from intoxicants that cause heedlessness but doesn't encourage
others in undertaking abstinence from intoxicants that cause heedlessness.
This is first type of person. The individual who practices for his own benefit but not for that of
others.
"And how is one an individual who practices for the benefit of others but not for his own?
1. There is the case where a certain individual himself doesn't abstain from the taking of life
but encourages others in undertaking abstinence from the taking of life... (similarly with
the precepts on stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, and intoxicants).
Such is the individual who practices for the benefit of others but not for his own.
"And how is one an individual who practices neither for his own benefit nor for that of others?
1. There is the case where a certain individual himself doesn't abstain from the taking of life
and doesn't encourage others in undertaking abstinence from the taking of life...
(similarly with the precepts on stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, and intoxicants).
Such is the individual who practices neither for his own benefit nor for that of others.

"And how is one an individual who practices for his own benefit and for that of others?
1. There is the case where a certain individual himself abstains from the taking of life and
encourages others in undertaking abstinence from the taking of life... (similarly with the
precepts on stealing, sexual misconduct, lying, and intoxicants).
Such is the individual who practices for his own benefit and for that of others.
"These are the four types of individuals to be found existing in the world."
— AN 4.99
All of the above individuals would experience different levels of karma and the ending of
suffering.

The rewards of virtue (Personal Reward)
Here is another one of those sequences that we may be able to recognize. This is on the
rewards of virtue.
[Ananda:] "What is the reward and blessing of wholesome morality?"
[The Buddha:] "Freedom from remorse, Ananda."
"And of freedom from remorse?"
"Joy, Ananda"
"And of joy?"
"Rapture, Ananda"
"And of rapture?"
"Tranquillity, Ananda."
"And of tranquillity?"
"Happiness, Ananda."
"And of happiness?"
"Concentration, Ananda."
You may notice this as the middle of 7 Factors of Awakening (specifically joy, rapture, tranquility,
& concentration).
The passage continues on from here.
"And of concentration?"
"Vision and knowledge according to reality."
"And of the vision and knowledge according to reality?"
"Turning away and detachment, Ananda."
"And of turning away and detachment?"
"The vision and knowledge with regard to Deliverance, Ananda."

— AN 10.1 (Nyanatiloka, trans.; from Path to Deliverance, pp. 65-66)
Simile of the Saw
One of my favorite stories is called the Simile of the Saw. It is the story, that the Buddha told, of
a slave/maid named Kali who worked for a woman named Vedehika, who was thought of very
highly in the community. Hearing these stories about her owner, as the story goes, Kali asked
herself if there was any anger present in “her lady” without showing. And then thought to test
her.
So Kali started getting up late, after daybreak, and Vedehika questioned her. Kali said there
was no reason. To which Vedehika said, “No reason, you wicked slave” and angered &
displeased she scowled.
Then Kali started to not be as neat with her work in addition to continuing to get up late. This led
to Vedehika hitting Kali over the head with a rolling pin and cutting it open.
Kali then went into the neighborhood, blood streaming down her face and denounced Vedehika
in public.
The Buddha went on to give a talk on Right Speech that is very powerful.
He said: “"Monks, there are these five aspects of speech by which others may address you:
timely or untimely, true or false, affectionate or harsh, beneficial or unbeneficial, with a mind of
good-will or with inner hate. Others may address you in a timely way or an untimely way. They
may address you with what is true or what is false. They may address you in an affectionate
way or a harsh way. They may address you in a beneficial way or an unbeneficial way. They
may address you with a mind of good-will or with inner hate.
In any event, you should train yourselves: 'Our minds will be unaffected and we will say no evil
words. We will remain sympathetic to that person's welfare, with a mind of good will, and with no
inner hate. We will keep pervading him with an awareness imbued with good will and, beginning
with him, we will keep pervading the all-encompassing world with an awareness imbued with
good will — abundant, expansive, immeasurable, free from hostility, free from ill will.' That's how
you should train yourselves.
This last statement is extremely important I believe. The story is about right speech from others
towards us but it also speaks to our attitude and our commitment to freedom, from the ending of
suffering in the face of hostility from others. Keeping this idea of freedom, non-harming in our
hearts and minds NO MATTER WHAT.
The ending of that Sutta ends with a very famous simile where the buddha speaks to what is in
our hearts and minds even to the extremes of human suffering.
"Monks, even if bandits were to carve you up savagely, limb by limb, with a
two-handled saw, he among you who let his heart get angered even at that would
not be doing my bidding. Even then you should train yourselves: 'Our minds will be
unaffected and we will say no evil words. We will remain sympathetic, with a mind

of good will, and with no inner hate. We will keep pervading these people with an
awareness imbued with good will and, beginning with them, we will keep pervading
the all-encompassing world with an awareness imbued with good will — abundant,
expansive, immeasurable, free from hostility, free from ill will.' That's how you
should train yourselves.
