Summary and Review of Satipatthana Sutta:
This evening we conclude our extended look at the Satipatthana Sutta. But in many ways, this is
just a beginning. Mindfulness is central to the path, the practice and the fruits of our awakening,
to revealing, thru our own direct experience, the true nature of reality, and to freeing ourselves
from needless suffering.
In the weeks to follow, it is my intention for us to further deepen our understanding of
mindfulness away from any specific teachings- to explore how the addictive and allergic
tendencies of being human manifest in our ways of being, to our thinking and in our relations to
ourselves and others.
This tour of the four foundations of mindfulness began with this statement attributed to the
Buddha:
“Bhikkhus, this is the direct path for the purification of beings, for the surmounting of sorrow and
lamentation, for the disappearance of pain and grief, for the attainment of the true way, for the
realization of Nibbāna— namely, the four foundations of mindfulness.
The word satipaṭṭhāna is a compound term.
The first part, sati, originally meant “memory,” but in Pali Buddhist usage it far more frequently
points to of attentiveness directed to the present— hence the makeshift rendering
“mindfulness.”
The second part is explained in two ways: either as a shortened form of upaṭ-ṭhāna, meaning
“setting up” or “establishing”— here, of mindfulness; or as paṭ-ṭhāna, meaning “domain” or
“foundation”— again, of mindfulness.
Thus the four satipaṭṭhānas may be understood as either the four ways of setting up
mindfulness or as the four objective domains of mindfulness, to is further clarified in the rest of
the sutta, and as we have come to know, throughout the Buddha’s teachings.
The sutta describes in detail the meaning and path of practice - the ways to consider and use
the Four Foundations of Mindfulness, which are:
1. Being Mindful of the Body
2. Being Mindful of Feeling
3. Being Mindful of our state of Mind
4. And being Mindful of the objects of mind which in Buddhism includes objects of
Dhammas - aspects of nature and reality.

Culmination
The buddha concludes the Satipatthana Sutta by making this statement:
46. “Bhikkhus, if anyone should develop these four foundations of mindfulness in such a way for
seven years, one of two fruits could be expected for one: either final knowledge here and now,
or if there is a trace of clinging left, non-return.
He goes on to repeat this statement reducing the time from six to five and on to one year, then
down to months, to weeks, and to days, each time stating that these two fruits could be
expected: either final knowledge here and now, or if there is a trace of clinging left, non-return.
This refers to what is referred to the Four Stages of Enlightenment otherwise knows as the path
and the fruit. Four levels of attainment or insight.

The Four Stages of Enlightenment

WHAT DOES ALL THIS MEAN?
To me the most important stage, one I have talked about many times here, is this first level,
which is referred to as stream-entry, where one has insight into the belief that there is an
unchanging self - insight into the 5 aggregates, lost any attachment to rites and ritual (that by
taking any particular action that you can be freed, and has lost any doubt in the teachings.
It comes from the simple ability to be in the present moment and to witness our direct
experience without judgment.
