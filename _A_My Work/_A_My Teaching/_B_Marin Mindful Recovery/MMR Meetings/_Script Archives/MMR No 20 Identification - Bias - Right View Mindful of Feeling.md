As stated in the sutta, there are three basic ways to categorized types of worldly feelings that
arise:
We can say they are Pleasant
We can say they are Painful
We can say they are neither pleasant or unpleasant, otherwise Neutral
All three types of worldly feelings can cause suffering.
Pleasant feelings (either physical or mental) are associated with greed (wanting more
enjoyment and desire).
Unpleasant feelings are associated with aversion, pain, or anything that you want to resist or get
rid of.
Neutral feelings are neither pleasant nor painful. Neutral feelings have the tendency to allow
delusion (confusion) to emerge. This is because the mind may either be ignoring or not aware of
feelings.
The basic point introduced here is awareness of whether a particular feeling is related to
progress or regress on the path.
Householder (no motivation to change)
Spiritual Seeker (motivation to seek deeper spiritual understanding)
Accomplished One (realized a higher path: Equanimity)
The Buddha did not categorically reject all pleasant feelings, nor did he categorically
recommend unpleasant experiences for their supposedly purifying effect. Instead, he placed
emphasis on the mental and ethical consequences of all types of feeling.
Just as it would be unnecessary to contend weather changes, it is suggested one need not
contend with our changing feelings.
Contemplating in this way, one becomes able to establish a growing degree of inner detachment
with regard to feelings.
A mindful observer of feelings, by the very fact of observation, no longer fully identifies with
them and thereby begins to move beyond the conditioning and controlling power of the
pleasure–pain dichotomy. This creation of two parts which is a whole is the central theme of
non-dualism.
The task of undermining identification with feelings is also reflected in the commentaries, which
point out that to inquire “who feels?” is what leads from merely experiencing feeling to
contemplating them as a satipaṭṭhāna.
This merely experience feeling as feeling is seeing things as the way they are.

Brahmajāla Sutta.
the Buddha instructed his us to be neither be elated by praise nor displeased by blame, since
either reaction would only upset their our composure.
In a Sutta called "the Traits" he elaborated on this.
“There is the case where a person, suffering loss of relatives, loss of wealth, or loss through
disease, doesn’t reflect: ‘That’s how it is when living together in the world. That’s how it is when
gaining a personal identity [atta-bhāva, literally “self-state”]. When there is living in the world,
when there is the gaining of a personal identity, these eight worldly conditions spin after the
world, and the world spins after these eight worldly conditions: gain, loss, status, disgrace,
censure, praise, pleasure, & pain.’ Suffering loss of relatives, loss of wealth, or loss through
disease, he sorrows, grieves, & laments, beats his breast, becomes distraught.
And then there is the case where a person, suffering loss of relatives, loss of wealth, or loss
through disease, reflects: ‘That’s how it is when living together in the world. That’s how it is
when gaining a personal identity. When there is living in the world, when there is the gaining of a
personal identity, these eight worldly conditions spin after the world, and the world spins after
these eight worldly conditions: gain, loss, status, disgrace, censure, praise, pleasure, & pain.’
Suffering loss of relatives, loss of wealth, or loss through disease, he doesn’t sorrow, grieve, or
lament, doesn’t beat his breast or become distraught.
This references what is known or commonly referred to as the 8 worldly winds:
praise and blame
gain and lost
fame and ill repute
pleasure and pain
The idea is that we begin to look at how we experience the world in terms of the views that are
illuminated, rather than on their content.
Look for a trace the arising of views to craving (taṇhā),
which in turn arises dependent on feeling.
We begin to gain insight to pleasure has a tug on us, while with pain there is a pushing away.
With neither pleasure nor pain there is a tendancy to feel that things are unclear.
It is by fully understanding the role of feeling as a link between contact and craving, the
view-forming process itself can be transcended. We can be mindful of this process.
It is suggested transcendence of views that is a principle aim of the satipaṭṭhāna
contemplations.
The second satipaṭṭhāna, contemplation of feelings, has an intriguing potential to generate
insight into the origin of our views and opinions.

Ditthi (Feelings and Views)

In fact, sustained contemplation of feelings can reveal the fact that feelings decisively influence
and colour subsequent thoughts and reactions.
A good example of this is when someone holds the view that "black men" are dangerous that
they act on this fear without any real evidence that they are in danger.
This runs against the supposed supremacy of rational thought over feelings and emotions. This
is actually an illusion.

Biases
Logic and thought actually often only serve to rationalize already existing likes and dislikes,
which in turn are conditioned by the arising of either pleasant or unpleasant feelings. Thoughts
creating feelings reflecting back and solidifying these pre-existing views.
The initial stages of the perceptual process, when the first traces of liking and disliking appear,
are usually not fully conscious, and their decisive influence on subsequent evaluations often
passes undetected.
Feeling can provide quick feedback during information processing, as a basis for motivation and
action. Is it pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
I'd like us to explore that as a topic.
Contemplation of feelings offers an opportunity to bring these evaluative and conditioning
functions back into conscious awareness.
We become informed of our deepest beliefs and views. These ruts in our memory banks.
If a feeling triggers a thought that is identified with and not discerned then this could cause a
subsequent dogmatic adherence and clinging to that view, thought or belief. Not seeing cause
and effect.
In contrast, a realistic appraisal of the conditional dependence of views and opinions on the
initial evaluative input provided by feeling uncovers the affective attachment underlying personal
views and opinions.
Clear awareness of the conditioning impact of feeling can lead to a restructuring of habitual
reaction patterns that have become meaningless or even detrimental.

Back to Feelings-----
By being Mindful of our Feelings, our emotions can be deconditioned at their point of origin.
Without such deconditioning, any affective bias, being identified with by the initial evaluation
triggered by feeling, can find its expression in apparently well-reasoned “objective” opinions and
views.
As I stated earlier, the Buddha's crucial issue was to uncover the psychological attitude towards
our experiences including feelings--but it also includes underlying the holding of any view, since
he clearly saw that holding a particular view is often a manifestation of desire and attachment.
So, an important aspect of the early Buddhist conception of right view is therefore to have the
“right” attitude towards one’s beliefs and views.
The crucial question here is whether one has developed attachment and clinging to one’s own
views, which often manifests in heated arguments and disputation.
The standard formulation of Right view in the discourses is directly concerned with attachment
and clinging, formulated by way of the 4 Noble Truths. It is with understanding how suffering
comes about and to reduce or eliminate it. This scheme of the 4 Noble Truths is then applied to
view themselves so it can be transcended.
The four Noble truths being understanding suffering, the origin of suffering and the cessation of
suffering, and how to remove it (which is the Noble Eightfold Path).
We will be going into the 4 Noble Truths at length in the future but this discussion introduces us
to the far reaching depth of this teachings.
The Buddha,, on being challenged to proclaim his view, answered that his view was such that is
to the absence of quarreling with anyone. This related to the understanding of impermanent
nature of the three types of feeling to freedom from disputation. "I do not dispute with the world,
it is the world that disputes with me."
I'd like us to have a discussion on how our feelings affect us and how they manifest in our

How might this help us to be more compassionate, loving, and peaceful?
