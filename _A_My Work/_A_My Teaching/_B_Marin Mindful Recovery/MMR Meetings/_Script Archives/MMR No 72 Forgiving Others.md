Forgiving Others
Last week we talked about forgiveness of self. Before moving on to our contemplation of the
forgiveness of others I’d like to summarize what we covered a bit. And as a FYI, next week I am
thinking of continuing this series of talks summarizing the teachings of forgiveness and
reviewing the 12 Principles of Forgiveness as described by Jack Kornfield.
Self-forgiveness is usually needed because we judge ourselves to measure up to our own
standards or because of something we have done that has harmed others. We can get caught
in the social conditioning from our childhood, our family dynamics. We can get lost in our
narratives and stories and think that we are not forgivable because we think that we are a bad
person, or by forgiving that we would be getting away with our transgressions, in some way we
would give permission to act in unskillful ways in the future.
Society considers love, compassion & forgiveness as soft, weak.
But our deeper longing is to not cause harm, to having connection with others, and for a feeling
of being at peace.
In this practice we contemplate: What's wrong with forgiving ourselves? What will I have to face
if I forgive myself?
Would this be because then we would have to face the multitude of feelings and beliefs - our
regret, that we are condoning, our anger, that should be punished, the emptyness inside us, our
fear, self-doubt, horror, grief, loneliness, failure, vulnerability, or hurt- that we won't be loved.
If letting go of blame gives you joy, what would stop you? Do we see only the pitfalls?
In many ways this self-blame is a way of buffering deeper feelings below. We can get addicted
to the angst of the 2nd arrow. It can somehow make us feel alive. We need to have the courage
to forgive ourselves - to have the willingness to let go of our strategy to blame.
Do we value our reasons for staying small more than being the light shining thru the door? We
want to recognize this story and gain contact with the feelings underneath with kindness.
We can start to see our own suffering. We can come to understand the grieving and sorrow in
other cultures. We can begin to see that it is not personal badness
We are part of a larger process where there is huge suffering. This Self-forgiving and
self-compassion - Opens our hearts.
We can't will self-forgiveness or forgiving others but we can set an intention. We can connect
with the wise place in us that knows we will not be free until we include ourselves in our own
heart.
It is a process. Seeing our woundedness requires support. We need to develop the skill to sit
still with what is happening, to bring into consciousness that which has been unconsciousness.

Albert Camus writes it this way. He says, "We all carry within us our places of exile, our crimes,

In the Bhagavad Gita it says, "If you want to see the brave, look to see those who can
return love for hatred. If you want to see the heroic, look to those who can forgive."

There is the story of Sujatha Baliga, who had been terribly abused and sexually abused as a
young woman. This woman went to Harvard and decided she was going to become a lawyer
and get them-- all the people who had perpetrated.
She had this encounter with the Dalai Lama who noticed that she was making herself sick with
the hatred that she carried and the bitterness. Sujatha and her associates started doing
restorative justice - working not just with the victims, but also the perpetrators. And as she
began to lay out what it meant to really recognize and understand the suffering that they had
caused, she also started to hear their stories. And one day in that-- not so long in that process,
she said, "It all shifted to me because I heard their stories. And I looked around, and I saw not
just these men who had done this, but I also saw 14 boys who'd all been abused themselves,
many of them sexually abused. And all of a sudden, I realized, it wasn't just personal. It was a
chain of suffering that was passed on from one generation to another."

There are a few ways of explaining how we might understand forgiveness
One way would be to understand that forgiveness:
● Does NOT condone what happened. It doesn't mean forgive and forget.
● In fact, it's very helpful to say, you may find it necessary to stand up and prevent what
happened, or you may make the inner determination, I will do all in my power to prevent
this suffering from continuing for me or for anyone else.
● We can have the misconception of forgiveness, that it's a weakness or that it's ignoring
what happened. This is not accurate.
● We are not condoning it. It's saying, "I will not let this happen again. I'll do what's
necessary."
Why is this valuable?

● First of all it releases us, and it releases the world around us from the grip of anger, and
hatred, and hurt, and fear.
● It shifts us, as human beings, from the reptilian brain, from that most primitive fear brain,
and allows us to see from or rest in a place of wisdom, with the wise heart, to see with
kindly eyes.
● It allows us to move from being in exile to somehow, again, feeling a connection or a
trust in this world.
We can ask ourselves: when we don't forgive, how is that for us? How is it to live with that? How
is it in our bodies, in our nervous systems?
We can understand who is this for?
● And as I often say, the people who hurt you-- they could well be on vacation in Maui right
now having a good old time.
● And you're, "I hate them, I hate them, I hate them." Who is suffering?
● When you say who it's for, you understand that it's primarily for yourself.
● And what's true is that you do not necessarily have to talk to those other people again.
● You don't have to continue that relationship. It has to be done in a way that protects
yourself and protects others.
● And don’t do it for their sake when you forgive someone, because it may or may not
change them.
● But you do it for the sake of your own heart, and your own well-being, and your own
freedom.
There is a story of a man whose father died, his six siblings fought, two of them with lies and
greed, to gain control of the family construction company. In two years, they nearly robbed this
man of his inheritance. But they were his family-- parents of beloved nieces and nephews. He
had been with them for every family gathering-- weddings, holidays. Family was his life.
The man, Alvaro, went through shock, and loss, and anger, and grief, and tears. And after a
year, he realized he could trust them, but he could only trust them to be themselves. And he
didn't want them to colonize his heart or his life. He told his lawyer to use every legal means to
protect his inheritance, but he would not give up loving them. By seeing them clearly, he
regained trust in himself. And though he went through some painful legal battles, he also
learned that he could care for himself and still love them or trust them in a new way.
This is one of the complexities of forgiveness:
● One has to protect oneself.
● But at the same time it does not mean you can’t continue to love.
Jack Kornfield tells a story of a conversation he had with I think of this dialogue with Sri
Nisargadat - a famous Indian guru of non-dualism, who was with a fellow who was sitting there
and complaining about his mother. She wa not a good mother, wasn't caring, who'd been
abusive at times, and still was not open to him. And Nisargadat listened to all that and looked
back and said simply, "Yes, and still love your mother." And the man replied, "She wouldn't let

me." And Nisargadat looked him in the eyes and said, "She couldn't stop you." This is
really the freedom that we are given no matter what happens outside ourselves.

there is something delicate in forgiveness.
It is not sentimental or weak. Hatred never ends by hatred, but by love alone is healed. This is a

And sometimes, as you start to do it, you feel the anger, you feel the grief, you feel the hurt. You

The work of forgiveness is not for the faint-hearted. And it can be a long process. But in the end,
as Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn describes, "If only it were so simple. If only there were evil people
somewhere committing evil deeds, and it were simply necessary to separate them from the rest
of us and destroy them. But the line dividing good and evil cuts through the heart of every
human being. And who among us is willing to destroy a piece of their own heart?"
As we discussed with Self-Forgiveness the reason that we have been hard is complex and not
entirely the fault of those who have harmed us . When we forgive we are not condoning it or
say it is okay to continue the behavior in the future.

There are a few ways to Practice forgiveness but we usually start with asking forgiveness from
those that we've harmed, even before you go to self-forgiveness or forgiving others, because it
opens us to realize that we, too, have betrayed others.
And every one of us has done this in some way or another. And when we can recognize that,
then it becomes possible to open not only to ask their forgiveness, but to open to extend
forgiveness to those that have harmed us.
