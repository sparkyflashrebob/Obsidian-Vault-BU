Last week we ventured further into the 4th Satipatthana, being Mindful of Mind-Objects,
otherwise called Dhammas, -- turning our attention to what is called “The Sense Spheres”.
Bhikkhu Analayo translates dhammas as “mental factors and categories”, “classificatory
schemes”, and “frameworks or points of reference applied during contemplation. The point
being that contemplating these dhammas is a way for us to learn to see the world through
“Buddhist spectacles”.
In the first three satipatthana’s we looked at the body, sensations, and the mind. These, too,
can, and usually do, become mental objects themselves. Additionally, the 4th Satipatthana
specifically places it focus on a list of 52 mental factors and categories. These include the 5
Hindrances of sense-desire, ill-will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry and doubt: the 5
Aggregates (form, feelings, perceptions,mental formations (also known as sankaras), and
consciousness). To these we add the Sense-Spheres, which are the eye and visible objects,
ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and touch, and mind and mental objects.
“Again, bhikkhus, a bhikkhu abides contemplating mind-objects as mind-objects in terms of the
six internal and external bases. And how does a bhikkhu abide contemplating mind-objects as
mind-objects in terms of the six internal and external bases? Here a bhikkhu understands the
eye, he understands forms, and he understands the fetter that arises dependent on both;
(pause)..... and he also understands how there comes to be the arising of the unarisen fetter
(unwise attention), and how there comes to be the abandoning of the arisen fetter, and how
there comes to be the future non-arising of the abandoned fetter.
It goes on..... “He understands the ear, he understands sounds... He understands the nose, he
understands odours... He understands the tongue, he understands flavours... He understands
the body, he understands tangibles... He understands the mind, he understands mind-objects,
and he understands the fetter that arises dependent on both; and he also understands how
there comes to be the arising of the unarisen fetter, and how there comes to be the abandoning
of the arisen fetter, and how there comes to be the future non-arising of the abandoned fetter.
The word for understanding in buddhism is paññā, which also means, wisdom or intelligence -
with a focus on a greater version of understanding and wisdom - not just a passing or a run of
the mill understanding. It is the understanding of the true nature of phenomena. Typically, this
understanding is of the three characteristics of all things: anicca (their impermanence), dukkha
(the dissatisfaction or suffering that comes with things not being stable), and anattā (non-self or
ours to control).. We will again look at how this understanding illuminates this idea of not self
some this evening.
I find it helpful to review how the Buddha saw how that things are dependently originated, which
is simply stated as "if this exists, that exists; if this ceases to exist, that also ceases to exist".
In the Honeyball Sutta, which is also taken from the Majjhima Nikaya (the same set of suttas
from which the Satipatthana Sutta is taken -- one of the Buddha’s disciples, Venerable Maha
Kaccana, described the Buddhas’ teachings on dependant origination in this way:

“Dependent on eye & forms, eye-consciousness arises. The meeting of the three is contact.
With contact as a requisite condition, there is feeling. What one feels, one perceives. What one
perceives, one thinks about. What one thinks about, one objectifies. Based on what a person
objectifies, the perceptions & categories of objectification assail him/her with regard to past,
present, & future forms cognizable via the eye.
This last part, where the perceptions & categories of objectification assail him or her with regard
to past, present & future is what is called papañca. Papanca is where what one perceives sets
off a chain reaction of associated thinking. Notice that, in this statement, the wording is “What
one feels, one perceives. What one perceives, one thinks about. What one thinks about, one
objectifies. Based on what a person objectifies, the perceptions & categories of objectification
assail him/her with regard to past, present, & future forms cognizable via the eye.” The same
can be said with regards to hearing, tasting, touching, and mental objects.
There is where this notion of the “agent” that is introduced. That agent is us. Assume ourselves
as the one perceiving,the one who is cognizing, the one who is forming mental object, the one
who is this consciousness that arises.
Referring back to the Aggregates, it is the experiencing of form, feeling, perceptions, mental
formations and consciousness that we identify with as being this agent. In Buddhism this is
actually thought more as processes that is taking place.
The six internal and external sense spheres are the basis for everything we know. The Buddha
expounded on this in many ways but in one case very directly. In what is called “The All: The
Sabba Sutta “The Blessed One said, "What is the All? Simply the eye & forms, ear & sounds,
nose & aromas, tongue & flavors, body & tactile sensations, intellect & ideas. This, monks, is
called “The All”.
Anyone who would say, 'Repudiating this All, I will describe another,' if questioned on what
exactly might be the grounds for his statement, would be unable to explain, and furthermore,
would be put to grief. Why? Because it lies beyond range."
In this last section he says that if someone contents that they can describe another way this
could be and were asked to explain this, they would not be able to do so. This All is everything
we can know. It refers to everything we can sense. In order to explain another would be to try to
explain nibbana.
Nibbana is considered to lie beyond the range of the six senses and their objects. Nibbana is
non-conditioned and unbound. It is said that a person who has attained nibbana has gone
beyond all phenomena and therefore cannot be described. In another way it is described as
“consciousness without feature”.
With discernment, we understand that “The All”, like everything else, is to be abandoned
because it is conditioned. The mind and mind objects are conditioned - they are treated as the
sixth sense. Thoughts, reasoning, memory, reflection, moods and emotions are experiences of
the mind and are seen impersonally, just as all the other senses. These teachings are raised for
our contemplation and investigation.
Why are the sense bases considered conditioned? It is because “What one feels, one
perceives. What one perceives, one thinks about. What one thinks about, one objectifies. Based

on what a person objectifies, the perceptions & categories of objectification assail him/her with
regard to past, present, & future. They are dependant on other conditions.
Joseph Goldstein describes in Mindfulness: The Direct Path to Realization, how we experience
the tongue, how it moves around the mouth, turns things over, and seeks out the touch
sensations of the food and the various tastes and flavors that arise. It does seem like it has a
mind of its own. Likewise with the seeing, and the hearing, etc.. It is like we cannot turn of this
seeking of the senses. The movements, this seeking, is as a result of intentions in the mind.
The sankharas - mental formations. These include the assumptions you have about yourself -
from childhood, parents, teachers, friends, relatives, society - all of that - whether you perceive
yourself as good and positive or bad or negatively or a confused way - these are sankharas.
Ajahn Sumedho calls this “coming from a fixed perspective” instead of understanding one that is
actually always changing.
As a result of investigation we become less identified with the play of phenomena that
continually plays out. We see that each experience is simply what it is and the experience of
the “I” is extra.
In another sutta there is this discussion:
“If the cause and condition for the arising of eye-consciousness [eyes and forms] would cease
completely and totally without remainder, could eye-consciousness be discerned?”
“No, friend.”
“In this way, friend, this has been declared . . . by the Blessed One thus: ‘For such a reason this
consciousness is nonself.’”
Likewise we can see that this works similarly to that of nose-consciousness, ear-consciousness
and the rest, including consciousness of mental objects.
Joseph asks us “What keeps us, then, from opening the clenched fist of our minds and falling
into the midst of everything? This is where our understanding of the eye and form, ears and
sound, etc can help us. We can directly watch the arising of these fetters at the sense doors.
There is a very famous sutta called the Fire Sermon where the Buddha spells out clearly and
dramatically.
“Monks, all is burning. And what, monks, is the all that is burning? The eye is burning, forms are
burning, eye-consciousness is burning, eye-contact is burning, and whatever feeling arises with
eye-contact as condition—whether pleasant or painful or neither-painful-nor-pleasant—that too
is burning. Burning with what? Burning with the fire of lust, with the fire of hatred, with the fire of
delusion; burning with birth, ageing, and death; with sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection and
despair. . . . “The ear is burning. . . . The mind is burning. . . . “Seeing thus, monks, the
instructed noble disciple becomes disenchanted with the eye, with forms, with
eye-consciousness, with eye-contact, with whatever feeling arises with eye-contact as
condition—whether pleasant or painful or neither-painful-nor-pleasant; becomes disenchanted
with the ear . . . with the mind. . . . Becoming disenchanted, one becomes dispassionate.
Through dispassion, [one’s mind] is liberated.”

So we practice looking at the defilements at the sense doors. We can notice how they feel
energetically. Like a hot coal - once we are aware of it's effect on us - our innate wisdom and
instincts come into play.
Revisiting Dependent Co-Arising also called Dependent Origination. It is a detailed breakdown
of the arising and passing away of the world and justifies why I prefer to look at rebirth as a
moment to moment process. It is suggested that there are ten billion mind moments in a
second. And we have opportunities in each moment to break the rotating of the wheel of our
suffering. Typically we look at contact and feeling. This is the place where we can break the
Chain. Yet, if we give unwise attention to contact and feeling, then our conditioned response is
trigger. Desire arises - the desire of wanting to continue or get more or the want to push away
and get rid of- or the desire for a neutral experience such as dullness or delusion - to disconnect
from experience.
If we are mindful of contact and feeling, we can cut the chain of dependence right there. We can
rest in the knowing of experience. There is less effort in this and therefore there can be more of
a sense of ease in our practice and in our lives. If we can remember that there are only six
things happening, -- the All that we have discussed here today, we can make things more
simple for ourselves. The power of Mindfulness is a receptive process that helps us to
accomplish this.
We have other tools too. If need be, we can be more active in our knowing by noting the feelings
of our experience. Another aspect of Mindfulness we can look at is being Mindful of Desire
itself. As we have a feeling based on contact with the senses, the result is desire - craving -
thirst. Again, it is when we are unaware of this process, when we do not give wise attention to
what is going on that we mis-interprete what is actually happening. With wise attention we can
become aware of this process unfolding and place our attention on more skillful ways of being.
When the Buddha talks about the defilements and the fetters it would be wise for us to keep in
mind that these teachings are speaking about suffering and the way out of that. It can be a key
to a life of tolerance, of contentment and of joy.
We can be Mindful of Perception also. Perception is not absolute. Most of us are believe that
what we experience of ourselves and the world is what actually is. Perception is also
conditioned. It is like we are tuned to see things in certain way. We are receptive to
experiencing events in a certain way but do not see how we are influencing these perceptions.
Karma plays into this on one level. Generally, how we see things in the Bay Area are seen
completely differently than most of the rest of the country. Racism is where one sees someone
as threatening or less than because of the color of their skin. It is obvious that perception is in
the eye of the beholder. I have a poster on my wall at home here, showing a whole slew of our
various biases. Two people seeing the same thing can see things in completely different terms.
Understanding this, it is suggested that we try to learn what Joseph quotes a 16th century Zen
Master as saying “Don’t side with yourself.” It doesn’t mean we let go of all of our viewpoints. It
means that we have the understanding of this conditioned nature of our experience.
In Buddhism there are certain teachings that bear this out further. The first is taking what is
impermanent to be permanent. Intellectually we can be aware of this, but when we are in the
grips of our experience this understanding usually is forgotten.

The second is taking what is unattractive to be attractive. We enjoy having our sense pleasures
fulfilled - looking at what are perceive to be beautiful. We are continually caught in this desire for
the beautiful. We casually overlook the aspects of these sense experiences that are not
beautiful but need to be prop’d up.
The third is taking what is unsatisfactory to be happiness. Enjoyment of the sense pleasures is
frequently how we define happiness. This ends up being a continual seeking for fulfillment. We
want another hit and then another and then another. Perpetually seeking happiness outside of
ourselves. We even can be deluded into thinking that wanting itself is happiness. I can easily
get caught in the excitement of getting something I want. It can make me feel happy,
temporarily. We can get in thinking that we have to always be doing something to be happy.
And finally, taking what is Non-Self to Be Self. Notice how we identify with sensations in the
body, or our thoughts and our moods. As we have been discussing, having the understanding
and seeing that our perceptions are conditioned, that they are habits, means that we learn to let
go of this habit of selfing.
It is through this practice of the Sense Spheres that we can cultivate other ways of perceiving.
We can see them thru the three characteristics - anicca, anatta and dukkha.
We can see practice with the defilements of greed, hatred and delusion by countering them with
the practices of giving of oneself, compassion and wakefulness.
We can become attuned into how sense perceptions give rise to the fetters.
We can reflect on the aggregates - seeing their impersonal nature.
We can summarize this with the direction give by the Buddha at the end the section on the
sense spheres with this refrain:
“Mindfulness that ‘there are dhammas’ is established in one to the extent necessary for bare
knowledge and continuous mindfulness. And one abides independent, not clinging to anything
in the world. “That is how in regard to dhammas one abides contemplating dhammas in terms of
the six internal and external sense-spheres.”
I offer this for your consideration.
