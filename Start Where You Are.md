There was a young man in the military who had a problem with his  temper. He got angry very easily, flew off the handle quickly. So his superior officer remanded  him to take an eight-week training in mindfulness, which they have in the military now in some  places. I'm very grateful for that and hope it goes up the chain of command. 

[LAUGHTER] 

And after about six weeks of this training, he was on his way home in the evening in the  supermarket. It was crowded, there were long lines, and the woman in front of him in the long  line was in the wrong line. She had only one item, she should have been in the express line, and  he had a kind of temperament, exaggerated perhaps by his military training, that things should be  done properly. So he got a little annoyed that she was in the wrong line. 

She was carrying a little baby. She got up to the checkout clerk, and they started to look at the  baby and coo over this little kid, as one does sometimes. And then she handed the baby to the  checkout clerk to hold, and he became really upset. How unthoughtful. How thoughtless. There's  a long line. Doesn't she know people are in a hurry? And he could feel that anger rise in his  body. 

But because he had also been training himself in mindfulness and awareness, he began to notice  the pain of that anger and realized that there was nothing that it would help with. So he took a  few deeper breaths after recognizing it had settled down, and he looked up again and he realized  it was kind of a cute kid. 

[LAUGHTER] 

So when it was his turn to check out, he looked at the checkout clerk and he said, "That was a  cute little boy that you were holding back there." And she said, "Oh, did you like him? He's my  boy. You see, my husband was in the military like you, but he was killed in Afghanistan last  year. So now I have to work full-time. And my mom, she takes care of my boy, and she tries to  bring him in once a day so I can see him."
