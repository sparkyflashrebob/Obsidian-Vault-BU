## Dharma Talk: Easing Our Inner Torment Without Gaslighting Ourselves

[Pause / settling breath down into the belly]

Before I jump into this talk, I want to offer my usual disclaimer.

These reflections are offered strictly for your contemplation and spiritual practice, and never as a judgment or reaction to anyone in particular. When I talk about human clumsiness, or getting tangled up in fear, I am almost always talking about myself and the things I catch in my own heart. These words are never meant to harm, but only to shine a warm light on our shared human practice.

[pause / small smile]

I want to start tonight with that famous observation from Viktor Frankl:

> *"Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and our freedom."*

[pause]

Now, that is a profound insight. Most of us have heard it, and intellectually we nod our heads, thankful for that reminder. 

Frankl speaks about this *space* between the stimulus and the response. 
On one side, we have the **stimulus**—something coming into our senses. Seeing a grocery total that shocked you, opening a high electric bill, hearing someone make a rude or dismissive comment, reading alarming news on your screen, or feeling an unexpected pain in your lower back. That is the stimulus coming through the senses.

On the other side, we have the **response**—the emotion that arises: a jolt of anxiety, anger, dread, or frustration, accompanied by our body tightening up.

Frankl says there is a space between the two. But what happens to that space? 
When we aren't mindful, our conditioning jumps right across it. The sensory contact happens, and before we even know what hit us, the emotion has already taken over. Stimulus and response seem fused together into a single, instantaneous reaction. 

And because we don't feel that space, we immediately fall into one of two traps.

The first trap is **The Downward Spiral**. 
Something happens that we don't want to experience—a rude encounter, a financial shock, bad news—and we plunge straight into anger, panic, and despair. Our mind starts spinning disaster movies, and we stew in that dread until our sleep is wrecked.

The second trap—and we see a lot of this in spiritual culture—is what I call **The Gaslighting Trap**, or the Spiritual Bypass. 
We try to talk ourselves out of the pain. We tell ourselves, *"Just stay positive! High vibes only! Everything happens for a reason!"* 

So we put a smile on our face, but underneath, **our stomach is tight or knotted up**. 

And then we tell ourselves that **we shouldn't be this way!** 
We judge ourselves: *"I've been meditating for years, I should be calmer than this. What is wrong with me? I shouldn't be feeling stressed or upset."* 

Pretending that things aren't difficult, or pretending that you don't feel shaken when unwanted things happen, isn't spiritual awakening. It's just gaslighting your own nervous system.

So the real inquiry for our practice—and really the heart of our theme tonight—is: **How do we ease our inner torment without gaslighting ourselves about the difficult facts of our lives?**

[pause / slow breath]

To understand this, we can turn to one of the most practical teachings the Buddha offered: the teaching of **The Two Arrows** (*the Sallatha Sutta*).

The Buddha asked: When an untrained person experiences pain or difficulty, what happens? They sorrow, grieve, and lament. It is as if they were shot by an arrow, and then immediately shot by a second arrow. They feel two pains: the physical impact, and the mental torment.

Let's unpack what these two arrows actually are in our ordinary, daily lives.

**The First Arrow** is the raw, unavoidable impact of living as an embodied human being in an uncertain world. 

The Buddha spoke directly about the things in this life that are stressful, what he called *Dukkha*. He pointed out the unavoidable realities we all face:
- **Old age, sickness, and physical decline**: waking up to physical aches, injuries, or an aging body.
- **Separation from what we love and cherish**: losing people, our roles, or the routines we care about.
- **Being associated with what we dislike or hate**: having to deal with rude people, workplace friction, rising prices, disturbing news headlines, or circumstances completely beyond our control.
- **Not getting what we want**: having our plans unravel and our expectations disappointed.

And how do we encounter all of these things? **We experience them when they come into our senses.**
Through the eye, the ear, the nose, the tongue, the body, and, in particular, the mind. 

When you see a grocery bill that is eighty dollars higher, or hear someone talk down to you at work, or see conflict on the news, or feel your knee aching—that is a real First Arrow entering your senses. 

It is an actual, factual impact. It hurts. It is not in your head. It is simply part of being alive.

And the first step of honest practice is honoring that First Arrow without shame: *This is hard. This came into my senses, and it makes complete sense that it feels unpleasant.*

[pause]

**The Second Arrow**, on the other hand, is what our mind adds on top of that experience. This is what causes us to suffer. 

It’s the story we spin about the impact: the blame, the catastrophic predictions, and the attack on our character: *"They don't respect me. I'm failing. The world is out to get me. I'm never going to make it."*

[pause]

Now, why does unwanted sensory contact hit us so intensely in the body? Why does the stomach get tight or knotted up right below the ribcage?

In early Buddhism, there is that fundamental law of conditionality—what the texts call *Idappaccayatā*:  
> *"When this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises."*

Things don't happen because you are broken; they happen because of causes and conditions.

Your conscious intellect understands modern concepts like checking accounts, corporate politics, or international news. But the deeper, older parts of your nervous system—the ancient circuits that control your diaphragm and gut—are millions of years older than modern culture.

Those systems have only two basic settings: **Safe and Connected**, or **Threatened and Endangered**.

When unwanted, threatening information enters your senses—whether it’s a high bill, an angry voice, or chaotic news—your body treats that sensory contact as a threat to survival:
1. **The breath freezes**: The diaphragm locks up so you stay still and scan for predators.
2. **The gut shuts down**: Blood is pulled away from digestion to fuel your muscles to fight or run.
3. **The core muscles tighten**: The belly armors up to protect your soft organs from physical blow.

That tight, knotted feeling in your stomach isn't a character defect. It isn't a failure of your mindfulness. It is your **inner creature reacting to conditions of threat**. 

When we understand conditionality, we don't have to get mad at our bodies. We can understand: of course the body tightens up when conditions feel threatening! That's just nature doing what it was built to do.

[pause]

Think about what happens in nature with animals. 

Take a deer out in a meadow. A deer is grazing peacefully, and suddenly it spots a tiger or a predator stalking in the tall grass. The survival alarm sounds instantly: its heart races, its muscles fire, and it bolts at full speed across the field to get to safety. 

But notice what happens once the danger has passed. 
The deer reaches safety and stops. It literally trembles for a moment—it shakes the adrenaline and the shock right out of its physical body. And within minutes, it goes right back to peacefully grazing on the grass. 
The deer doesn't stand by the stream for the rest of the afternoon stewing: *"What if that tiger comes back tomorrow? Why did that tiger target me? What if I'm losing my speed?"* The animal doesn't carry the predator around in its mind. Once the threat is gone, it shakes it off and returns to baseline.

[pause / small smile]

With human beings, this is completely not the case!

We don't just shake it off. Instead, our minds take over. **We think about the tiger when there is no tiger.**

The paper bill is just sitting quietly on the kitchen counter, but in our heads, the tiger is mauling our future. Someone made a sharp comment to us at work two days ago, and we are still replaying the confrontation in our heads while we're trying to fall asleep in our bed. There is no physical predator in the room—we are sitting in a safe room, breathing air, with a roof over our heads—but the mind keeps ringing the five-alarm bell, keeping our stomachs tight and knotted up for days on end.

[pause / settling breath]

And this leads us to look at the Five Aggregates (*the Khandhas*)—the components that make up our moment-to-moment human experience:
We have the **physical body and form**, the **feeling tone** (whether something feels pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral), our **perceptions**, our **consciousness**, and that crucial aggregate: **the mind**—our mental formations, our thoughts and reactions.

The senses bring in the raw data of life: sight, sound, physical sensation. Feeling tone registers it as unpleasant. 
And right there, the mind aggregate jumps in and starts running wild.

The Buddha gave us a brilliant observation about how the mind works. He said:
> *"Whatever we frequently think and ponder about, that becomes the inclination of our mind."*

[pause — let that land in the room]

Whatever we frequently think and ponder about, that becomes the inclination of our mind.

If we spend our days taking unpleasant sensory experiences and constantly thinking and pondering about catastrophizing stories, resentments, and self-judgment... our mind naturally inclines toward bitterness, dread, and torment. We carve that groove deeper and deeper.

This is why we must learn to distinguish **The Facts of Experience** from **What We Add On**:

| The Raw Experience (The Facts / The Senses) | What We Add On (The Mind's Story / Second Arrow) |
| :--- | :--- |
| **A high bill or grocery total** enters the eye | *"I am a failure. I can't take care of my family. I am worthless."* |
| **Someone speaks sharply or rudely** at work | *"They have zero respect for me. Nobody appreciates me. People are horrible."* |
| **An alarming news headline** flashes on screen | *"Everything is doomed. The world is hopeless, and nothing I do matters."* |
| **A stiff back or chronic ache** in the body | *"My body is ruined. I'm falling apart, and life is over."* |

Look at that difference.
The column on the left is **the direct experience**—the First Arrow entering through the senses. It is a fact of this human life. It requires practical navigation: budgeting, resting, having a calm boundary with a coworker, or turning off the news.

The column on the right is **what the mind adds on**. That is the story, the myth, the second arrow. It attacks our dignity and creates endless inner suffering.

The sensory event happens outside of our direct control. But what we think and ponder about it—what we add on—is something we *can* catch.

[pause]

And that brings us right back to Viktor Frankl's space. 
Where is that space?

**The space is right between the sensory experience and what the mind adds on.**

When you see the bill, when you hear the rude remark, when you feel the ache—you pause. You notice the sensation. You notice the tight, knotted stomach. 

And you realize: **You are not the knot.**

You are the open awareness that *knows* the knot. 
The tight stomach is like a sudden squall blowing across the desert. It is windy, it is turbulent. But the open sky doesn't panic. The sky has plenty of room for the squall to pass through without being harmed.

[pause / look around warmly]

### The Two Wings of Spiritual Freedom: Mindfulness (Clear Seeing) and Compassion (The Open Heart)

Now, how do we actually rest in that open sky? How do we live this out when we leave our cushions tonight?

Teacher Tara Brach often speaks about the **Two Wings of a Bird** that allow us to fly and be free. 
If a bird only has one wing, or if either wing is injured, it cannot soar—it just flutters in circles on the ground.

The first wing is **Mindfulness**, which Tara often calls **Clear Seeing**—the foundation of Wisdom (*Paññā*). 
Mindfulness is the clear seeing of things as they actually are, in the present moment, without distortion, without denial, and without gaslighting ourselves. It is the clear eye that looks directly at what’s happening and discerns: *“This is the raw sensory experience—the First Arrow. And that over there is the story my mind is adding on.”* Clear seeing sees conditionality. It understands that we are the spacious awareness, not the knot in the belly.

The second wing is **Compassion** (*Karuṇā*)—**an open heart, lovingkindness, and caring action**.
As Tara teaches, compassion is the wing that meets whatever is clearly seen with tenderness rather than condemnation. It’s what keeps our mindfulness from turning into a cold, clinical, or overly intellectual exercise. Compassion looks down at that tight, knotted stomach and says: *“I see you hurting. You’re not broken. I care about this suffering.”* And compassion doesn't just sit there—it moves into action. It takes wise, caring, practical steps in the world to protect our health, set boundaries, and support those we love.

Notice what happens if we try to navigate life with only one wing:
* If we have **only clear seeing without compassion**, we can become cynical, dry, or hyper-critical. We diagnose our stress, we see all our flaws, but our heart is shut down and cold.
* If we have **only compassion without clear seeing**, we slide right into sentimentality, wishful thinking, and spiritual bypassing. We try to "love" our way through by denying reality, putting on a fake smile, and gaslighting our own nervous system.

It is only when **Head and Heart fly together**—Mindfulness and Compassion, Clear Seeing and an Open Heart—that we find genuine freedom and resilience.

[pause / gentle breath]

And this brings us directly to **The Practice of Double Holding**:

> **"With the Wing of Clear Seeing: Recognizing experience as experience;**  
> **With the Wing of Compassion: Holding ourselves with an open heart and caring action."**

* With one hand, rooted in **Mindfulness and Clear Seeing**, we hold the unvarnished facts outside—refusing to hide, refusing to gaslight ourselves, and meeting reality as it comes into our senses.
* With the other hand, rooted in **Compassion and an Open Heart**, we hold our own body and mind with tenderness—using kindness as an active medicine to soothe the knot, and taking grounded, caring action in the world.

Here is the four-step practice you can keep in your pocket:

### 1. Acknowledge the Experience (Mindfulness / Clear Seeing)
Don't gaslight yourself. When something unpleasant hits your senses, see it clearly and say it plainly:  
*"This is difficult. This entered my senses, it hurts, and it makes complete sense that my stomach is tight or knotted up."*  
Clear seeing stops the exhausting game of pretending.

### 2. Notice What You're Adding On (Insight / Discernment)
Catch the mind aggregate in the act. Ask yourself:  
*"What story am I building on top of this? Am I spinning disaster movies? Am I beating myself up?"*  
Remember what the Buddha said: *What we think and ponder upon becomes the inclination of the mind.* Use clear seeing to put down the second arrow. Let the story drop.

### 3. Comfort the Body with Kindness (Compassion / Open Heart)
You cannot talk your stomach out of a knot with intellectual arguments. Speak to your body with the warmth of an open heart:
* Place your palm flat on your upper belly or over your heart. Let the physical warmth sink in.
* Take a slow, resetting breath: two gentle inhales through the nose, and a long, relaxing sigh out through the mouth.
* Use compassion as an active aid, speaking to your body like a dear friend:  
  *"I know this feels threatening. I know you're unsettled. But right now, in this ten seconds, we are breathing. We are safe in this room. You can soften. Let me handle the practical situation outside."*

### 4. Take Grounded, Caring Action (Clear Seeing & Compassion in Motion)
Soothing your nervous system isn't about becoming passive. It is about clearing your mind and opening your heart so you can take wise action:
* You address the practical facts—the budget, the difficult conversation, the body's need for medical care or rest—without the panic.
* You set healthy boundaries with rude people or negative news.
* And if you need support, you reach out and ask for help without shame. In our culture, asking for help is often treated as a flaw, but reaching out is an act of deep wisdom and open-hearted human courage.

[pause / settling breath down into the belly]

### Breaking the Chronic Habit: Immediate Relief and Long-Term Freedom

Now, when we carry around this kind of stress day after day, it doesn't just stay an occasional reaction. It becomes **chronic**—it turns into a deep, automatic habit of the mind, so we find ourselves living in a state of continuous stress and low-grade alarm. 

And that constant tension takes an enormous toll. It drains our life force, wears down our physical health, and keeps us feeling perpetually exhausted.

This is why this practice is so vital: it works on two distinct levels.

In the immediate moment, it gives us practical, real-time relief. Whenever you experience something unwanted—whether you're looking at a bill, dealing with a difficult interaction at work, or feeling an ache in your body—Double Holding gives you a way to pause, soften the tight knot in your stomach, and stop shooting that second arrow into yourself. You don't have to get dragged into the downward spiral.

But the long-term benefit is where real freedom lives. 
Over time, as we repeatedly practice clear seeing and open-hearted kindness, we actually rewire the habit of the mind. We stop running that mental engine that keeps conjuring the tiger all day long. Our nervous system finally gets the message that it can stand down. We become more peaceful, more resilient, and far less stressed overall.

And think about what that gives us: **it gives us our energy back.** 
Instead of spending nearly all of our daily energy feeding the stress—fighting our own feelings, catastrophizing, and wrestling with our inner knots—we reclaim that energy. We now have the spaciousness, the vitality, and the clear head to deal with our lives in a far more skillful, creative, and grounded manner.

[pause / slow breath]

As we close tonight, remember: 
Life will bring us things we don't want to experience. That is simply the reality of having a human body in this world. 

But you do not have to add torment to pain. You do not have to turn against yourself. 

Spread both wings of your practice: look at your life with the **Clear Seeing of Mindfulness**, and hold your human vulnerability with the **Open Heart and Caring Action of Compassion**. When both wings are strong, you can navigate any storm without losing your footing or your dignity.

Thank you for your practice, and thank you for your kind attention tonight.

## Summary (Pocket Takeaways)

[Pause / settling breath down into the belly]

As we get ready to close our evening, here are a few simple signposts to keep in your pocket this week:

* **Honor the Sensory Impact (First Arrow)**: Facing things we don't want to experience—bills, rude behavior, tough news, physical aches—is part of being human. Acknowledging that it hurts isn't negativity; it's honest presence. Stop gaslighting yourself.
* **Catch What You Add On (Second Arrow)**: Notice the difference between the facts of experience and the stories the mind spins. Remember: *Whatever we frequently think and ponder about becomes the inclination of our mind.*

* **We Think About the Tiger When There Is No Tiger**: In the wild, a deer runs from danger, shakes off the shock when safe, and goes back to grazing. We keep the predator alive in our thoughts. Catch yourself when you are conjuring a tiger in a room where you are completely safe right now.
* **Inhabit the Space**: Viktor Frankl's space lives right between the sensory contact and what the mind adds on. You are not the knot; you are the spacious awareness observing it.
* **Fly with Both Wings (Tara Brach's Teaching)**: 
  - **The Wing of Mindfulness (Clear Seeing)**: Seeing things as they actually are, with discernment that separates sensory facts from mental stories.
  - **The Wing of Compassion (The Open Heart)**: Meeting ourselves with active kindness, comforting the tight knot in the body, and moving into caring action in the world.
* **Practice Double Holding**: Hold the truth of reality outside with clear seeing, and hold your inner being with an open, compassionate heart.
* **Immediate Relief, Long-Term Freedom**: This practice relieves stress in the heat of the moment, but its lasting fruit is breaking the chronic habit of stress. Instead of burning your energy fueling worry, you reclaim that energy to deal with your life in a skillful, peaceful manner.
