## Theme Introduction

[Look around the room, take an easy breath, and speak plainly]

Before we settle into our meditation tonight, I wanted to introduce a theme that touches something almost all of us experience, often on a daily basis: **how we carry difficulty directly within our physical bodies.**

Many of us wake up in the morning with physical aches and pains, or that familiar sensation where our stomach is tight or knotted up. Like we are going to enter into a battle. 

And naturally, we try to cope. Many of us throw ourselves into our workday because we don't have any other choice.  Sit down at our desks, dive into projects, solve problems, or attend to others. And for many of us, while we are deeply absorbed in that external work, something remarkable happens: the pain seems to fade into the background. We barely notice it. Yet we experience the current source of our stress, the stress of needing to work. 

Yet the moment we stop—the moment we take a break, walk away from the computer, or try to sit quietly—the ache rushes right back in. 

Modern neuroscience shows us that when we focus on an outside task or are thinking of others, our brain quiets down to what is known as the **Default Mode Network**—the resting-state circuitry that runs when we aren't focused on a specific outside goal. But as soon as the task ends and we turn back inward, the default mode comes right back online, and our internal sensations and mental chatter take center stage.

When that happens, we often swing between two extremes:
Either we try to run away into constant distraction, numbing ourselves and pretending nothing is wrong...
Or we collapse into frustration and self-pity, wondering why this keeps happening to us and believing we can somehow think our way out of bodily distress.

Tonight, we are going to explore what lies between those two extremes: **The Practice of Double Holding: Finding the Middle Way.** 

How do we stand right in the middle of our conditions—holding the unvarnished facts of our experience with clear seeing, while holding our vulnerable bodies with active compassion and sensible care?

## Dharma Talk: The Practice of Double Holding: Finding the Middle Way

[Pause / settling breath down into the belly]

Before I jump into our reflection tonight, I want to offer my usual disclaimer.

These reflections are offered strictly for your contemplation and spiritual practice, and never as a judgment or reaction to anyone in particular. When I talk about human clumsiness, getting caught up in distraction, or getting tangled in mental stories, I am speaking directly from my own life and the things I catch in my own heart. These words are never meant to lecture, but only to shine a warm light on our shared human practice.

[pause / small smile]

### Carrying Difficulty in the Body

For a long time now, I have noticed that I tend to carry a great deal of difficulty directly within my physical body. 

It is something I have explored from many angles over the years. I know that being even a little bit more physically active alleviates quite a bit of that collected tension. And I try to look closely at what I eat, and pay attention to my immediate environment that might cause pain. 

All of these things help, but I always have the lingering sense that lifestyle tweaks alone are not the entire story.

For years, I used to wake up with daily morning headaches—many of which classified as migraines. They had a strange, sharp predictability. For instance, whenever I walked outside into the cold air or stepped back indoors—whether from seasonal allergies or smoke from wood-burning fireplaces that lingers in the winter air—the headache would suddenly flare or intensify. That sensory contact would trigger it immediately.

[pause]

And then my workday would begin. 

As soon as I sat down at my desk and poured my attention into the tasks ahead, something remarkable happened: I would barely notice the pain at all. It seemed to disappear. 

I realized that I could take my mind, apply it to an outside task, and almost completely shut off how I was feeling in the body. 

But the moment I stopped working—the moment I took a break during the day, walked away from the screen, or paused to check in with how I was feeling—those painful sensations would return. 

So I came to see that so much of what we experience depends on what we are paying attention to. Whatever we shine the light of our attention on comes more into focus, and what we don't fades into the background somewhat. 

[pause]

### The Default Mode of the Mind

This reflects the way our brains function. 

When we are deeply engaged in an external task, or when we are thinking about and serving others, the brain quiets what neuroscientists call the **Default Mode Network**—the resting-state circuitry that runs when we aren't focused on a specific outside goal. 

When the default mode is quieted by an engaging task, our internal aches recede into the background. 

But as soon as the task ends and we turn inward—or when we sit down on a meditation cushion—the default mode comes right back online. Our internal sensations and thoughts take center stage again. 

And that is why so many people find it challenging to sit in silence. The moment the distraction stops, the collected tension of the body and the chatter of the mind are right there waiting for us.

Yet engaging directly with this default mode—rather than running away from it—is one of the most beneficial fruits of our meditation and spiritual practice. That is where real transformation takes place.

[pause / slow breath]

### The Habit of Distraction

Working brought its own stress, of course. Yet in a strange way, I got quite comfortable with that stress. 

We humans actually have an immense ability to carry a lot of tension. The trouble is that we often fluff it off and attribute it entirely to something outside of ourselves. We place the blame elsewhere, rarely looking at our own habits and actions. A lot of this has to do with the innate fear that inhabits us. It is really easy to to look to external matters and to be able to point out the faults in it, but it's very difficult for do to to turn that around and look inside of ourselves. 

When we do look within, many of us choose to distract ourselves. This is why many of us become workaholics. But work is hardly the only distraction we use. We use our phones, media, entertainment, or general busyness,. We often bang our heads against the wall trying to escape stress (I also call this "punting"), yet in many ways we feel we have no choice.  We just want relief. 

[pause]

Yet, we all have to work. The stress of earning a living is not something we can just easily get around, short of constantly switching jobs or being lucky enough to find work we truly enjoy. For many of us, even when we do have enough money, we continue on even though it is stressful. 

Distraction has its limits. Numbing ourselves or pretending difficulty doesn't exist only creates other problems down the road. It's a great way to avoid looking inside ourselves. 

Navigating this physical and mental distress requires something far more honest than distraction. It requires learning how to stand in the middle of our conditions—what I call **The Practice of Double Holding**.

[pause]

---

### 1. The First Arrow: Experiencing What Arrives

When difficulty enters through our senses, we discover an inescapable reality: **we do not get to choose whether we experience it or not.**

The experience has arrived. 

The truth of the matter is that our senses are aware of what we experience before we can cognize it. 

Through the eye, the ear, the nose, the tongue, the body, and the mind:
* Cold air or wood smoke entering through the senses.
* An aching temple, a tight neck, or an unexpected lower back spasm.
* A high electric bill or a grocery total that gives us a shock at the checkout counter.
* Hearing a sharp or dismissive comment from someone at work.

Our brain has evolved as a predicting machine. It is here to keep us alive. As experience comes through the senses, the body is immediately takes action. The brain then senses these changes, and a flood of feelings and emotions comes through. 

Sometimes the intensity is mild, and we can manage our way around it. But when pain is severe—like a full migraine—choice vanishes. We cannot simply meditate it away or talk ourselves out of it. We have no choice but to be right there with it.

In contemplative practice, this is what the Buddha described as the **First Arrow** (*the Sallatha Sutta*): the immediate, raw hit of life. There is really nothing we can do to stop the immediate arising of that hit. It is what it is.

When a migraine strikes, really the only sensible thing we can do is do our best to alleviate it through practical, grounded means. 

Depending on the intensity, that might mean taking a pain reliever like ibuprofen, using something topical on the temples, stepping into a dark and quiet room, or drinking a tall glass of water. When I have a migraine, I frequently have to do all of these things. 

During these moments, our mental outlook is essential. Severe pain can make any of us short-tempered occastionally. But rather than going off on a tangent and compounding things, if need be, we can simply explain to those around us that we are having a migraine and need to pay attention to that. We don't have to snap or blame. We can simply hunker down and focus on our well-being - on taking care of ourselves. And I find that a lot of this is comes quite naturally, if we allow it. 

Whether we are *holding* that pain or fighting it, however, this is where our spiritual practice begins. 

Learning to let go does not mean getting rid of the pain or trying to force it away. It means releasing our tight mental grip around it. 
We can see this ourselves.  If we close our fists tightly, like we're holding a rock, and then open our hands.  We drop the rock. 
It's just that simple.  But it's so difficult for us to do. 

[pause / gentle breath]

---

### 2. The Second Arrow: Catching the Story and "The One Who Knows"

Once we do what we can to alleviate that initial difficulty and things start to settle down, a much subtler challenge arises: **The Second Arrow**.

Normally, the first thing we do is look outside of ourselves to find a cause, trying to figure it out and point the finger at external circumstances: *"Who did this? Why is the weather like this? Why are people so difficult?"*

And when we realize we can't point the finger outside, our minds often turn inward into self-depreciation. 

We start feeling sorry for ourselves. The mind starts creating stories and explanations: 
*"Why does this always happen to me? What is wrong with me? - Feeling that we are broken, not good people. 

I believe this is the central theme for most of us, that we want to be able to look at ourselves in the mirror and see that we are good people, that we're lovable, and that what we see in that mirror is what we expect ourselves to be 
There is an old saying, we want to be able to see ourselves as our dogs do.

[pause]

What cuts this off is simply bringing mindfulness to our experience. 

We don't necessarily have to talk to ourselves or recite long internal scripts. Often a simple phrase - a positive aspiration helps, but ultimately it is a quiet awareness—an immediate, wordless knowing:
* Being aware of the head aching and the stomach being tight or knotted up.
* Being aware that an unexpected bill or difficult news has arrived.
* Being aware that the mind is trying to find out why this is happening, generating stories and self-pity around the ache.

This is the vital function of what contemplative teachers call **the one who knows**—the observing quality of awareness itself.

When we rest as the observer, we stop taking the thoughts personally. We recognize that the mind is creating stories around the pain, but we do not have to claim those stories as who we are. 

This especially becomes clear when we realize that there is nothing outside that we can point the finger at, and that this storytelling actually is a sign that we are stressed.  

None of us like to suffer, and none of us want to suffer. Bringing this knowing quality to both the first arrow and the second arrow usually makes the second arrow very short-lived, or prevents it from happening at all. 

By not adding the second arrow, we save ourselves from an enormous amount of unnecessary torment. 

In fact, when we really look at it closely, we begin to see that these stories and thoughts appear to happen on their own, divorced from us. 

[pause / settling breath]

---

### 3. Causes and Conditions: Beyond "Thinking Our Way Out"

As we observe life with this knowing quality, we begin to see the subtle web of causes and conditions—what the Dhamma calls *Idappaccayatā*:  
> *"When this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises."*

Things don't happen because we are fundamentally broken; they happen because of causes and conditions. 

Yet notice how many things we repeat over and over, even when we know they aren't necessarily good for us. 

Take working at a computer: I work a great deal on my computer, which means I sit for long stretches. I used to speak often about headaches and tension, yet how often did I simply forget to get up and move around a bit, or get up to drink a tall glass of water? 

Dehydration is a very common contributor to headaches, as is prolonged sitting. 

This looking to causes where we can, and knowing the results that follow, is a continuous practice. 

With reflection, we can see these causes more clearly. We may still have a lot of work to do, but it becomes our general way of moving through life. 
While I still don't get enough formal exercise, for instance, I am far more active overall today than I used to be.

Causes and conditions are compounded by many factors—some within our reach, and many completely outside of our control. 

But recognizing conditionality protects us from a major illusion: **believing we can think our way out of our problems.**

We cannot solve bodily and life difficulties purely in our heads. Believing that if we just analyze our thoughts enough, or arrange external conditions to be completely perfect, is unreasonable. It doesn't work, yet that is how most of us live our lives. 

When we get caught up in our heads, we end up keeping the threat response alive. It is easy for us to traumatize ourselves. 

As we often remind ourselves in this circle: **We think about the tiger when there is no tiger.**

In the wild, when an animal like a deer faces a predator, it runs, shakes off the shock once it reaches safety, and goes right back to grazing. 

We humans, on the other hand, sit in a safe room with a roof over our heads, and food on the table, yet our minds keep conjuring the tiger for hours. We fixate on making problems out of things due to causes. So in some cases we are actually co-creating our stress.  
We think about the tiger when there is no tiger present. 

[pause / slow breath]

---

### 4. The Practice of Double Holding: Walking the Middle Way

So what is the alternative?

We cannot take the "easier, softer way" of constant avoidance—numbing ourselves in work, grasping onto distractions, ignoring the messages the body is sending us, or pretending difficulty doesn't exist. Avoidance definitely has its limits.

Nor can we live in the fantasy of perfect control, pretending we can eliminate every ache or unpleasant event.
 But we can see what we are adding to experience. 

Standing in the middle and being as skillful as possible is the key.

What does it actually look like to stand in the middle? 

It means we don't take the road of constant avoidance and distraction, and we don't collapse into despair or self-pity. Instead, we bring our practice right into the center of whatever is happening. We can hold both the truth of reality outside and our vulnerable bodies inside with a four-part movement of mind and heart:

[pause]

Here is how this unfolds as a living, spoken practice in our day-to-day lives:

First, there is **clear seeing**. This sense of mindfulness in *the one who knows*. This is being aware of the raw sensory experience just as it arrives through the senses, while we're not denying it or minimizing it. We don't have to pretend everything is fine when it isn't; there is no need to gaslight ourselves. We can simply acknowledge: *Experiencing difficulty is present. Given conditions, this arises.* 

[pause]

Following that, we can **disarm the story**. We bring an observing presence to the mind, noticing when the mind searches outside of itself to place blame, or turns inward into self-pity and harsh self-judgment: *"Why does this always happen? What is wrong with me?"* When we rest as the observer, we notice that this storytelling is simply a natural symptom of being stressed. We can see the story for what it is, and we gently let it drop without taking it personally or compounding our distress.
 Actually, when we're mindful, we are also being this observing presence. 

[pause]

And thirdly, **bringing compassion to our experience**. Having an open heart, being kind to ourselves, recognizing that none of us want to suffer, and bringing genuine warmth to our experience—a calmness, a peace, a soothingness. 

We can literally comfort the body: placing a warm hand over the belly, on the heart, or lightly against the temples. We invite a slow, resetting breath: a gentle inhale through the nose, and a long, relaxing release through the mouth. So we're meeting our vulnerability with care rather than abandonment—not compounding the pain, but holding it with tenderness.
 This is another act aspect of being mindful - remembering what can do when situations arise in a skillful manner. 

[pause]

And lastly, **grounding in action that considers both short-term and long-term relief**. 

In the immediate moment, we apply practical relief right now: taking a tall glass of water, resting our eyes, taking an ibuprofen, stepping away from the screen for ten minutes, or communicating our needs cleanly to those around us without snapping. 

And then, looking at the bigger picture, we adjust conditions over time so that we don't perpetuate unskillful behavior. We look honestly at causes and conditions, cultivating daily rhythms of regular movement, staying hydrated, setting healthy boundaries with work and news, and engaging with the default mode in our meditation practice. By tending to both the moment and the underlying causes, we protect our well-being and actually are able to have more peace, ease, and contentment in our lives.
 Might I say another aspect of mindfulness - of this remembering? 

[pause / settling breath down into the belly]

### Breaking the Chronic Habit: Immediate Relief and Long-Term Freedom

When we carry around this kind of stress day after day, it doesn't just stay an occasional reaction. It becomes **chronic**—it turns into a deep, automatic habit of the mind, so we find ourselves living in a state of continuous low-grade tension. 

And that constant tension takes an enormous toll. It drains our life force, wears down our physical health, and keeps us feeling perpetually depleted.

This is why this practice is so vital: it works on two distinct levels.

In the immediate moment, it gives us practical, real-time relief. Whenever you experience something unwanted—whether you wake up with a headache, encounter an unexpected bill, or feel your stomach knotting up—Double Holding gives you a way to pause, soften the grip, and stop shooting that second arrow into yourself. You don't have to plunge into the downward spiral.

But the long-term benefit is where real freedom lives. 

Over time, as we repeatedly practice clear seeing and open-hearted kindness, we actually rewire the habit of the mind. We stop running that mental engine that keeps conjuring the tiger all day long. Our nervous system finally gets the message that it can stand down. We become more peaceful, more resilient, and far less stressed overall.

And think about what that gives us: **it gives us our energy back.** 

Instead of spending nearly all of our daily energy feeding the stress—fighting our own feelings, catastrophizing, and wrestling with our inner knots—we reclaim that vital energy. 

We now have the spaciousness, the vitality, and the clear head to deal with our lives in a far more skillful, creative, and grounded manner.

[pause / slow breath]

---

### Standing in the Middle

We will never have completely perfect conditions. 

We will have days where our heads ache, where our bodies feel tight, where the bills are due, or where unexpected demands arrive on our doorstep.

We have no choice but to **experience** these moments, because life brings them to our door. 

Yet **we do have a choice in how we hold them.**

We don't have to flee into distraction or drown in self-pity. We can stand right in the middle:

* **In one hand**, rooted in **Mindfulness and Clear Seeing**, we hold the difficult reality of the world and our bodies as they are—seeing the facts clearly, without denial, and letting go of the need to fight what is already here.
* **In the other hand**, rooted in **Compassion and the Open Heart**, we hold our own heart and body with genuine kindness—meeting our pain with care, and taking sensible short-term and long-term actions to alleviate suffering.

In that quiet balance between clear seeing and active care, the storm may still be passing through, but the ground we stand upon remains steady, open, and at peace.

## Summary (Pocket Takeaways)

[Pause / settling breath down into the belly]

As we get ready to close our evening, here are a few simple signposts to keep in your pocket this week:

* **Experiencing Difficulty Arrives (First Arrow)**: Raw sensory impacts—headaches, tight bellies, unexpected bills, cold weather, difficult news—arrive through our senses without our asking. Acknowledging that difficulty is present is honest mindfulness, not negativity.
* **Catching the Story (Second Arrow)**: When pain arises, the mind often searches for outside blame or collapses into self-pity (*"Why me? What's wrong with me?"*). This storytelling is a symptom of stress. Resting as *the one who knows* allows the story to drop.
* **The Default Mode Network**: External work can temporarily quiet our awareness of pain, but the moment we pause, the resting-state mind brings sensations back online. Our meditation practice teaches us to meet the default mode with presence rather than running away into distraction.
* **Causes and Conditions (*Idappaccayatā*)**: Physical distress often stems from understandable conditions—prolonged sitting, dehydration, cold air, stress. Looking to causes helps us adjust our habits, while releasing the fantasy that we can "think our way out" of bodily problems.
* **Not Thinking About the Tiger**: In nature, animals shake off stress once danger has passed. Humans tend to keep the threat alive in thought. We can notice when we are conjuring a tiger in a room where we are currently safe.
* **The Practice of Double Holding**:
  1. *Clear Seeing*: Acknowledging raw experience as it is.
  2. *Disarming the Story*: Letting go of mental blame and self-pity.
  3. *Bringing Compassion*: Meeting bodily vulnerability with warmth, a hand on the heart or belly, and a resetting breath.
  4. *Taking Grounded Action*: Applying immediate relief (water, rest, ibuprofen) and tending to long-term conditions (movement, boundaries).
* **Immediate Relief, Long-Term Freedom**: Double Holding offers real-time comfort when distress strikes, but its lasting fruit is breaking the chronic habit of stress—freeing up our vital energy so we can navigate life with clarity and ease.
