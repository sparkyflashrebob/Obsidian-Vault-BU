# Living Mindfully Meditation Group Meeting - Aug, 16

### Outline
### Living Mindfully Meditation Group Opening and Housekeeping

- Acknowledged as taking place on ancestral Coast Miwok territory, with a formal recognition of the indigenous communities who originally stewarded the land where the virtual meeting was held.

- Group agreements were outlined, including the expectation that what is shared in the meeting stays within the meeting to preserve privacy and trust.

- Emphasized that the space is intended to be safe for everyone regardless of background or identity, and that participants should speak from their own experience and refrain from giving unsolicited advice.

- Participation, check-ins, and open discussion were noted as entirely voluntary, with passing always being an acceptable option.

- Providing space and allowing for stillness and pause was highlighted as an important element of the group's practice.

### Participant Check-ins and Internal Weather Reports

- Andrea shared that she noticed a tendency toward negativity and resistance during her workday, and used mindfulness tools to repeatedly return to the present moment throughout the day.

- Tiredness was identified by Andrea as a factor that increases vulnerability to default mode thinking, and sugar consumption was noted as having had a noticeable physical effect.

- Andy reflected on his own morning routine, noting completion of his glass of water, a morning walk, and approximately thirty minutes of meditation, expressing satisfaction with his progress.

- A shift in Andy's meditation practice toward concentration and mindfulness of breathing was described as a natural and positive progression.

- Gratitude was expressed by Andy toward Andrea for her engagement and for demonstrating how the teachings apply in daily life.

### Introduction to the Theme of Right Speech and Inner Dialog

- Introduced was the idea that most exhaustion comes not from the external world but from the constant internal narrative that evaluates, critiques, and worries without conscious permission.

- Right speech was reframed from being solely an outward practice directed at others to being fundamentally rooted in how one speaks to oneself internally.

- Posed was the central question of whether one is speaking to oneself with a harsh inner critic or with genuine patience and understanding when old habits and discomfort arise.

- The session's theme was established as exploring the art of inner dialog, examining why the mind feels like a crowded committee of competing voices rather than a single peaceful entity.

- Bringing radical honesty and kindness to internal dialog was presented as the very ground for personal freedom and spiritual practice.

### Guided Meditation Period

- Participants were invited to find a comfortable upright posture, with lying down offered as an option while maintaining the intention of remaining mindful, alert, and awake.

- A body scan was guided from the top of the head to the feet, with attention directed toward noticing and releasing areas of tension before settling into the breath.

- Awareness was directed to the rhythm of breathing, with participants encouraged to notice whether the breath was deep or shallow, fast or slow, without attempting to control it.

- Arising thoughts were described as natural occurrences to be gently noted as thinking or planning before returning attention to the breath.

- Participants were invited to ask themselves what kind of breathing would feel good in that moment and to work with that quality for the remainder of the meditation.

### The Root of Suffering and the Nature of Inner Commentary

- Thich Nhat Hanh was cited as teaching that suffering arises not from sights, sounds, smells, tastes, or tactile sensations but from the commentary applied to those experiences.

- Courage was described as necessary for looking directly at the noise of one's own mind, especially when that noise feels uninvited or untamed.

- The way one speaks to oneself internally was identified as setting the entire tone for how one speaks to the world externally.

- Practicing right speech as an internal discipline was described as transforming it from a personal exercise in self-monitoring into a living path of healing.

- Fundamental honesty was described as beginning with seeing oneself exactly as one is, without pretense, hiding, or shame, and acknowledging one's habits and fears without judgment.

- Honesty without kindness was cautioned against, as it can degenerate into self-flagellation, and extending grace to one's own reactive mind was presented as the basis for extending tolerance to others.

- Listening inwardly without entitlement or self-interest was described as allowing one to hear what discomfort and unease are actually trying to teach rather than simply getting in the way.

### Indigenous and Earth-Based Perspectives on Speech and Intent

- In indigenous and Earth-based traditions, speech and intent were described as living forces that shape the web of relations around a person.

- Words were characterized not as abstract symbols but as carriers of either medicine or harm depending on the intention behind them.

- Native elders were referenced as teaching that walking in balance begins with speaking truth gently first to oneself, so that one's presence becomes a refuge for the community rather than a source of friction.

- Stopping the use of speech as a weapon to judge others or defend fragile positions was presented as clearing the space for true connection and for asking what truly matters in terms of love, care, tolerance, and helpfulness.

### The Committee of the Mind in Buddhist Psychology

- Buddhist psychology was described as experiencing the mind not as a single unified entity but as a diverse and dynamic committee of different voices, selves, and power centers.

- Each member of the internal committee was explained as representing a different sense of identity created at some point in the past as a strategy for attaining happiness or satisfying a particular need.

- Thich Nhat Hanh was quoted as describing the untrained mind as less like a communion of saints and more like a group of corrupt politicians where different factions constantly vie for power and make backroom deals.

- Different knowing centers were described as sending messages across internal compartments and walls built to block out what one does not want to see.

- When sitting down to meditate, the conflict within the committee becomes obvious, as one part of the mind votes to focus on the breath while other factions actively attempt to sabotage that effort.

- Short-sighted committee members were described as hiding their motives out of embarrassment, speaking in subliminal hints and whispers, and sometimes arguing that one does not need to give in to their unskillful demands.

### Verbal Fabrication and the Mechanics of Inner Speech

- Sister Dhammadinna was cited from scriptural texts as defining verbal fabrication as the active process that constructs thoughts and speech, stating that directed thought and evaluation are called verbal fabrications.

- Directed thought, referred to as vitarka, was described as the act of choosing a topic and lifting the mind to place it there, likened to a hand aiming at a target.

- Evaluation, referred to as vichara, was described as the act of mulling over a topic and examining its details, likened to a hand polishing a metal dish.

- Thich Nhat Hanh was quoted directly as writing that thinking is the speech of the mind, explaining that thoughts are inner conversations expressing themselves silently.

- Speaking aloud was described as essentially giving others access to the internal audiotape running in one's head.

- During meditation, these same verbal functions are used skillfully by directing thought to the breath and evaluating how the breath feels in order to maximize comfort and ease.

### Papancha and the Dangers of Unanchored Thinking

- When directed thought and evaluation are not anchored in wisdom, they were described as degenerating into a destructive mental state known as papancha, or the chattering mind running completely out of control.

- Papancha was identified as being fueled by three toxic drivers, namely craving, conceit, and dogmatic views or biases.

- Andy shared that papancha frequently hijacks his own practice, particularly when thoughts arise about teaching others instead of remaining present with his own experience, identifying this as an expression of conceit.

- The entire avalanche of papancha was described as being born from the single reflex assumption of "I am the thinker," which objectifies the self and splits reality into "me" and the world.

- This splitting was described as transforming a person from the agent of thought into a helpless victim of their own projections, leading to cycles of anxiety, worry, and conflict.

### Practices for Skillful Inner Dialog

- Ajahn Sujato's practice of deliberate thinking was described as using a neutral intentional statement such as "I am a human being" spoken slowly inside the head while listening closely to the words as they arise.

- Noticing the empty pause before the first pronoun arises was presented as a way to help the mind rest in the silent space around thoughts and recognize itself as awareness rather than chatter.

- Ajahn Chah's recommendation to step into a dual parental role with oneself was described as speaking to the ego-driven, demanding part of the mind with maternal tenderness and authority, affirming that one is looking out for its welfare and will take the most skillful path.

- Thich Nhat Hanh's guidance on non-violence toward emotions was described as cradling emotion in the arms of mindfulness, breathing in with the acknowledgment that anger is present and breathing out with the intention to take good care of it.

- A two-cushion dialog practice attributed to Thich Nhat Hanh was described as sitting on one cushion as the adult self, speaking to a visualized younger self on the opposite cushion, then physically moving to inhabit the child's perspective and allowing raw expression before returning to the adult cushion to offer reassurance.

- A physical intervention recommended by Ajahn Fuang was described as locating where in the body the talking part feels situated, often in the throat, chest, or forehead, and directing the breath into that exact spot to dissolve the physical tension of the inner words.

- Mindfulness in any form was summarized as functioning like a brake, creating awareness of what is being experienced and interrupting the automatic momentum of inner commentary.

### Reflection and Open Discussion on the Session's Themes

- Andrea shared that following the meditation she was aware of having very few thoughts, describing the experience as lovely.

- Resonance was expressed by Andrea with the practice of being one's own parent, noting that she has been applying this approach when catching herself having negative or resistant thoughts that feel like a very young part of herself.

- Calmly redirecting herself toward responsible action using a parental tone was described by Andrea as something she has gotten much better at doing in recent practice.

- The two-cushion exercise was noted by Andrea as something she had forgotten about and found valuable as a way to actually speak to one's younger self and see what arises.

- Andy reflected on the experience of noble silence, acknowledging a personal compulsion to fill space as a teacher and noting that Gemini had reminded him that not needing to create content to fill silence is itself a noble quality.

- Discomfort with stillness was identified as a cultural conditioning, with Andy observing that many people in recovery are not accustomed to things being still and peaceful and have a tendency to want to stir things up.

- Andy acknowledged personal difficulty with the two-cushion role-play practice, noting uncertainty about whether he could genuinely inhabit his child self, while suggesting that imagining how the child felt might be a way to make the practice more accessible.

- The importance of not believing or acting on every thought was emphasized, with Andy distinguishing between not being responsible for the first thought that arises and being responsible for where one takes it.

- Tension in the head experienced during the session was noted by Andy as an example of the committee being present even while otherwise feeling well, reinforcing the teaching that one lives in the world and cannot simply reject what is happening physically.

### Closing Summary and Loving-Kindness Dedication

- The session's core teaching was summarized as the recognition that suffering comes not from external sights and sounds but from the endless commentary piled on top of experience.

- Right speech was restated as beginning in the silence of one's own mind, with the central question being how one meets fear and old habits, whether with a harsh inner critic or with the warmth of a good mother holding a crying child.

- The practice was described as not fighting the voices of the committee or driving them out, but learning to pause, feel where tension grips the body, and remember that one does not have to sign one's name to every thought that crosses the floor.

- Awakening was described as not about collecting clever theories, spiritual status, or locking down a permanent state of calm, but as a moment-to-moment commitment to drop defenses, tell the rigorous truth about what one is feeling, and share one's human messiness with kindness and unconditional presence.

- The true sanctuary was identified as being found not on a distant mountain peak but in the middle of ordinary everyday life.

- A loving-kindness dedication was offered, beginning with intentions of goodwill toward oneself and expanding outward to loved ones, neighbors, each other, and all living beings everywhere, wishing safety, health, ease, awakening, and peace.
