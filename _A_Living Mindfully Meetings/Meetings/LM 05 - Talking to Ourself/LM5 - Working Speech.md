Dharma Talk Guide: "The Art of Talking to Yourself"

Elements of Right Speech

**Thanissaro Bhikkhu, one of my primary teachers, once wrote that:**

**"We suffer largely because of the way we talk to ourselves. That’s what the message of the four noble truths is all about.**

**It’s not that we suffer from sights, sounds, smells, tastes, or tactile sensations. We suffer because of the commentary we apply to these things."**

Meditations 10: Dhamma Talks, "Comfortable as an Outsider, December 28, 2019

**It takes a tremendous amount of courage to look directly at the noise in our own minds, especially when that noise feels uninvited or untamed.** 

**When Thanissaro Bhikkhu points out that we suffer not from what hits our senses,** 

**but from the relentless commentary we add to it,** 

**he touches on the very root of our daily struggle.** 

**We spend so much energy commenting and judging,**

 **or worrying about how our unvarnished defects might spill out if our control slips away,** 

**these endless thoughts we have.** 

**The moment we turn our awareness inward,** 

**we realize that the way we speak to ourselves inside sets the entire tone for how we speak to the world outside.**

**Practicing [Right Speech] (**_**Sammā Vācā**_**) into this conversation transforms it from a personal exercise in self-monitoring into a living path of healing. Right Speech is often taught through the lens of refraining from lies, harsh language, or gossip,** 

**but at its heart, it is about alignment.** 

**It begins with the fundamental honesty of seeing ourselves exactly as we are,** 

**without putting on a front or hiding behind defenses.** 

**When we observe our own habits, our quickness to criticize, or our deep fear of being judged,** 

**being mindful of them,**

**honesty requires us to simply acknowledge those feelings without pretense or shame.**

Honesty with the Inner Voice: 

**Right Speech starts in the silence of our own thoughts. If we are constantly tearing ourselves down or nursing subtle resentments toward others,** 

**our outer speech will eventually reflect that poison.** 

**Being honest means recognizing when our internal commentary is driven by fear** 

**or the desire to win,** 

**rather than truth.**  
 

Kindness as Forgiveness: 

**Honesty without kindness can quickly degenerate into self-flagellation.** 

**When we catch ourselves slipping into judgment** 

**or running up against our defects,** 

**the practice calls for immediate forgiveness.** 

**True kindness is realizing that we are all carrying hte heavy, unconditioned human messy truth,** 

**and extending grace to our own reactive minds is what allows us to extend that same tolerance to others.**

Unpoisoned Listening: 

**We cannot practice Right Speech if we are caught in the defects of a biased listener.** 

**Listening inwardly without entitlement or self-interest allows us to hear what our discomfort** 

**and the "diss-ease" we feel are actually trying to teach us,** 

**rather than just trying to get our own way.**

**In Indigenous and earth-based traditions, speech and intent are viewed as living forces that shape the web of relations around us.** 

**Words are not just abstract symbols; they carry medicine or harm.** 

**When the Native elders speak of walking in balance, it begins with speaking truth gently—first to oneself, so that one’s presence becomes a refuge for the community rather than another source of friction.**

**When we stop using our speech as a weapon to judge others or defend our own fragile positions,** 

**we clear the space for true connection.** 

**We stop focusing on the minutiae of who is right or wrong,** 

**and instead begin asking the only questions that truly matter:** 

**Are we loving, caring, tolerant, and helpful?** 

**Are we willing to drop our rigid conditioning and simply listen with an open heart?**

1. The Nature of the Inner Dialogue: The "Internal Committee"

**It is helpful to challenge the common assumption that the mind is a single, unified entity.** 

**In Buddhist psychology, the mind is experienced more like a diverse group of voices.**

The "Committee of the Mind": **Rather than a single voice, the mind functions like a "committee" of different power centers or opinions.** 

**Often, one part of the mind knows something** 

**while another part is kept in the dark** 

**due to internal walls we build to compartmentalize our lives.**

The Invisible Director: **This subtle internal dialogue is the default mode of how we function;** 

**we are constantly taking orders from it even without realizing.** 

**In fact, if your mind quietens down and you think,** _**"There is no thinking happening now,"**_ 

**that itself is a subtle thought of the committee reporting on its own status.**

The Speech of the Mind: **Ultimately, "thinking is the speech of our mind".** 

**How we talk to ourselves internally is deeply shaped by how we have heard others talk,** 

**and these external habits of communication gradually colonize our internal dialogue.** 

**This exact phrasing "THE SPEECH OF THE MIND: is a direct quote from Thich Nhat Hanh, who writes in** _**The Heart of the Buddha's Teaching**_**:**   
**"Thinking is the speech of our mind."** 

**He explains that our thoughts are our inner conversation expressing itself silently,** 

**and when we speak aloud, we are essentially giving "earphones" to others to listen to the audiotape running in our heads.**

**This modern insight rests on a foundational scriptural framework.** 

**In the** _**Cūḷavedalla (CHOO-luh-veh-duh-luh) Sutta**_ (Majjhima Nikāya 44)**, an enlightened bhikkhunī Sister Dhammadinnā defines "verbal fabrication" (**_**vacī-saṅkhāra**_**)—as the active process that constructs our thoughts and speech.** 

**She states:**

**"Having first directed one's thoughts and made an evaluation, one then breaks out into speech. That’s why directed thought and evaluation (**_**vitakka**_ **and** _**vicāra**_**) are called verbal fabrications."**

**Commentarial texts explain that these two mental factors are the "mainsprings" of vocal activity** 

**because a mind without directed thought (focusing on a topic) and evaluation (examining or commenting on it) is completely unable to make a verbal sound.** 

**Thus, your thought process is defined as the direct internal precursor and blueprint of speech.**

2. The Anatomy of Inner Speech: Directed Thought (_Vitakka_) and Evaluation (_Vicāra_)

**The Buddha did not seek to immediately suppress or eliminate thought.** 

**Instead, he provided a precise physiological map of how inner conversation is constructed.**

Verbal Fabrications (**Vacī-Saṅkhāra**): 

**The Buddha categorized inner talk as "verbal fabrication"—the raw material from which spoken language is made.** 

**It is composed of two primary processes: directed thought (**vitakka**) and evaluation (**vicāra**).**

The Mechanics of the Conversation:

**Vitakka (Directed Thought) is the act of choosing a topic and "lifting" the mind to place it there.** 

**It is like a hand aiming an arrow at a target.** 

**Vicāra (Evaluation) is the act of mulling that topic over, examining details, and asking questions.** 

**It is like the hand polishing a metal dish.** 

Turning Inner Talk into the Path: 

**You may have noticed,**

**when we meditate, we use these same verbal functions.** 

**Instead of letting the mind chatter randomly,** 

**we direct our thoughts to the breath, and then evaluate how the breath feels, naturally making adjustments to maximize comfort and ease.** 

**This is the most basic, skillful level of conversation we can have with ourselves, and the basis for how mindfulness and meditation is taught.**

3. When Inner Talk Runs Amok: Mental Proliferation (_Papañca_)

**When directed thought and evaluation are not anchored in wisdom, they can degenerate into a highly destructive mental state.**

The "Bad Behavior Club": **This is known in the texts as papañca (conceptual proliferation of the chattering mind running completely out of control.** _**Papañca**_ **acts as a "bad behavior club" fueled by three toxic drivers: craving (*taṇhā*), conceit (māna), and dogmatic views or biases (diṭṭhi**).** 

**My own practice. This frequently hijacks me and I start thinking about how I can teach others about this.** 

The Root Illusion of "I am the Thinker": 

**The entire avalanche of** _**papañca**_ **is born from a single, self-reflexive assumption: "I am the thinker".**

The Trapped Victim: **The moment we think** _**"I am this,"**_ **we objectify ourselves.** 

**We split our reality into "me" and "the world," transforming ourselves from the** _**agent**_ **of thought into the** _**helpless victim**_ **of our own projections.** 

**We begin spinning in circles, trapped in anxiety, worry, and conflict.**   
**We don't have to do this.** 

The Committee

**This concept of the "Internal Committee" is a central metaphor in contemporary Dhamma teachings used to explain the fragmented, multi-faceted nature of the mind and to help practitioners navigate their inner dialogue during meditation and in daily life.**

1. The Composition of the Committee

**In Buddhist psychology, your mind, ego, or sense of "self" is not a single, cohesive entity.** 

**Instead, it is experienced as a dynamic "committee" composed of many different voices, selves, and power centers.**

Selves as Strategies: 

**Every "member" of your committee represents a different sense of identity that you've created and nurtured at some point in your past as a specific strategy to attain happiness or satisfy a particular hunger (NEED), usually referred to as craving.**

A Corrupt City Council: 

**Because these past strategies were created at different times under varying circumstances, they often work at cross-purposes.** 

**Some members are highly strategic, honorable, and far-sighted,** 

**while others are crude, short-sighted, or deluded.** 

**The texts describe the untrained mind as being less like a communion of saints and more like corrupt politicians,** 

**where different factions constantly vie for power and make back-room deals.**

The Talking Mind: 

**This is why the mind constantly talks to itself.** 

**Different knowing centers are literally sending messages across internal compartments and walls that we have built to block out what we do not want to see.**

2. How the Committee Acts in Meditation

**When you sit down to meditate—such as deciding to stay with the breath—the conflict within the committee becomes highly obvious.**

Factions and Sabotage: **While one portion of the mind votes to focus on the breath, other factions want to go elsewhere, actively waiting to sabotage your efforts to pursue alternative desires.**

Underground Negotiations: 

**The short-sighted committee members often try to hide their real motives out of embarrassment, speaking in subliminal hints and whispers. They may even argue that if you don't give in to their unskillful demands, they will "go underground" where you cannot see them.**

Hijacking the System: 

**A disruptive faction (such as a desire or anger or lust) can hijack your verbal and physical fabrications.** 

**For example, the "anger faction" will alter your physical breathing to make it labored and tight,** 

**while flashing mental images of injustice across your inner screen** 

**to convince the rest of the committee that you must react unskillfully** 

**to find relief.**

3. Restructuring the Committee

**The goal of meditation is not to violently suppress or destroy the committee, but to bring its back-room dealings out into the open and establish clarity, honesty, and order.**

The Power of the Veto: 

**Recognizing that your mind is a committee helps you cultivate** _**anatta**_ **(not-self).** 

**Just because an unskillful or embarrassing impulse is proposed on the committee floor does not mean** _**you**_ **are inherently bad.** 

**Your spiritual responsibility does not lie in controlling what gets brought to the floor,** 

**but in your power to adopt or veto the motion.** 

Nourishing the Wise Factions: 

**You can use the more skillful members of the committee to train and pacify the less skillful ones.** 

**By practicing concentration (by simply knowing and returning to the breath),** 

**you provide the wise members with high-quality "food" (the nourishment of rapture, pleasure, and equanimity).** 

**As they grow stronger, they can band together to show the short-sighted members that a longer-term perspective of peace is in everyone's best interest.**

Empowering Weak Members: 

**Other spiritual qualities like the having morals, generosity, or compassion**  _paramis_ **already exist within your committee,** 

**though they may currently be weak.** 

**Meditation acts as a training ground to give these weak members more voting power on the committee floor.** WHAT ARE THESE?

These spiritual perfections include generousity, having morals, renunciation or letting go, wisdom, spiritual energy, patience, truthfulness, determination, loving-kindness, and equanimity or balance.

4. Ultimate Liberation: Emptying the Boardroom

**As you progress on the path, you learn to step back from the chaotic arguments of the crowd and identify with the most skillful voices.** 

**Over time, you put the unskillful, unruly members "out to pasture" simply by refusing to feed them with your attention.**

**Eventually,** 

**as the mind achieves deep unity, stillness, and liberation,** 

**the need for these constructed, defensive strategies falls away entirely.** 

**You gradually pare down the members of the committee** 

**until you reach a point of complete freedom** 

**where you no longer need anyone in the boardroom,** 

**and the various artificial senses of "self" are permanently laid to rest.**

HOW TO MAP OUR OWN INTERNAL COMMITTEE

Transformative Practices: How to Talk to Yourself Skillfully

**Briefly mention how we can talk to ourselves skillfully.** 

**How do we go about changing the nature of our inner dialogue?**

Practice A: 

**We can choose our words and listening to the Gaps.**

**Ajahn Sumedho developed a practice that uses deliberate thinking to break our identification with the thoughts themselves.**

**Instead of letting the mind drift in passive, random chatter, we can choose a completely neutral, deliberate statement** 

**such as:** _**"I am a human being"**_ **or** _**"I am an unenlightened person"**_**.**

**Say this phrase slowly and intentionally inside your head,** 

**while listening closely to the words as you think them.**

**Notice the empty pause before the first pronoun "I" arises,** 

**and then the empty space of silence after the sentence ends.**

**Ask yourself:** _**That which is aware of the thought—is that a person, or is it pure awareness?**_ 

**This exercise helps the mind rest in the silent space around thoughts, recognizing that we are the awareness, and not the chatter.**

Practice B: 

**We can also our own Mother.**

**When the mind becomes unruly, defensive, or demanding,** 

**Ayya Khema, was a highly regarded Buddhist teacher who recommended that we step into a dual parental role with ourselves.** 

**Recognize that the ego-driven, crying part of the mind is behaving like a frantic, demanding child.**

**She says to "Talk back to it. Be your own mother".** 

**Speak to your own mind with maternal tenderness and authority, saying:** 

_**"I am looking out for your welfare. I know what is truly good for you,**_ 

_**and we are going to take the most skillful path,**_ 

_**even if it is not the easiest"**_**.**

**Combine this with Thich Nhat Hanh's guidance of non-violence toward our emotions:** 

**when anger or despair arises, do not fight it. Instead, cradle your emotion in the arms of mindfulness, silently comforting it:** 

_**"Breathing in, I know that anger is in me.**_ 

_**Breathing out, I will take good care of my anger"**_**.**

Practice C: 

**For deep-seated trauma or childhood narrative patterns, Thich Nhat Hanh outlines a conversational practice using two cushions.**

**Place two cushions facing each other. Sit on the first cushion as your adult self and visualize your younger, vulnerable five-year-old self sitting on the empty cushion.**

**Speak aloud to the child:** _**"My dear inner child, I know you are there and have suffered so much.**_ 

_**But I am your adult self.**_ 

_**We have grown up.**_ 

_**We are safe now, and we can protect ourselves.**_ 

_**Please tell me your pain; I am really listening"**_**.**

**Then physically sit on the opposite cushion.** 

**Inhabit your child self, allowing yourself to complain, cry, or express fear in the raw language of a child.**

**Move back to the adult cushion to offer reassurance.** 

**This dialogue brings childhood fears out of the unconscious basement and exposes them to the healing light of present-moment awareness.**

Practice D: 

**And lastly,**

**When the mind is caught in a compulsive loop of thinking and talking to itself, Ajaan Fuang (fwahng - like bang or clang) recommends a physical intervention.**

**Instead of trying to fight the thoughts logically,** 

**locate where in the physical body the "talking part" feels located** 

**(which is often in the throat, the center of the chest, or the forehead).**

**Direct your breath straight into that exact physical spot.**

**Visualize the incoming breath dispersing, dissolving, or exploding the physical tension of those words away.**

**And my simple reminder. When we apply MINDFULNESS in any way this is like a brake. It's like reversing the two seats that Thich Nhat Hanh talked about. We are aware of what we're experiencing.**

Summary

**To summarize what we have covered here tonight.**

**"When we sit down and close our eyes, we all know what happens. The room gets quiet, but the inside of our head turns into a crowded room. We start hearing all these different voices arguing, worrying, judging, planning.** 

**Thanissaro Bhikkhu put it so clearly when he said that our suffering doesn't come from the SIGHTS OR SOUNDS around us—it comes from the endless commentary we pile on top of them.**

**We tend to think of Right Speech as something we do out in the world, like trying not to lie or gossip. But the truth is, Right Speech really starts in the silence of our own mind.** 

**How are we talking to ourselves when we mess up?** 

**When our fear or our old habits show up, do we meet them with a harsh inner critic,** 

**or can we meet them with the warmth of a good mother holding a crying child?**

**Our mind isn’t one solid person;** 

**it’s a whole committee of old coping strategies trying to protect us.** 

**The practice isn't about fighting those voices or driving them out of the room with a stick.** 

**It’s simply learning to pause, feel where that tension is gripping us in the body,** 

**and remember that we don't have to sign our name to every thought that crosses the floor.** 

**We can just let the breath soften the room, see the chatter for what it is, and rest in the quiet awareness that holds it all."**
