Japanese Elder Exercises for Strength and Balance
9737cb6b-ed16-4ed2-a2fd-f518d31e5092
Here are the six movements that Japanese elders practice daily, as presented in the "Senior Wellness" source, which are recommended for individuals over 50 to maintain strength, balance, and confidence [1]:

1. **Shikodachi (the sumo stance)** : This exercise builds leg strength essential for daily activities like getting out of chairs, climbing stairs, maintaining balance, and preventing falls. It activates more leg muscles than regular squats with less joint strain and helps to undo stiffness from prolonged sitting [1].
2. **Nekawashi Dachi (the cat stance)** : This posture focuses on improving balance by activating deep stabilizer muscles, sharpening ankle control, strengthening the arch of the foot, and engaging the core. It helps prevent slips and falls on uneven surfaces [2].
3. **Seiza (proper sitting posture)** : A centuries-old sitting position, Seiza helps reset posture by gently stretching tight hip flexors and quadriceps, which often contribute to a hunched posture. It also activates deep core and spinal stabilizers [2, 3].
4. **Zazen (seated meditation)** : This isometric posture, originating from Zen monasteries, not only aids in spiritual clarity but also builds physical strength and endurance. It activates the deep core muscles, stretches the hips, and stimulates the parasympathetic nervous system, helping to lower cortisol, calm the mind, and promote cellular repair [3, 4].
5. **Kibodachi (the horse stance)** : Used by samurai, this powerful, grounded stance builds deep leg strength, lateral stability, and awakens muscles crucial for preventing side-to-side falls, improving hip and knee tracking, and supporting joint health. It also enhances proprioception, the body's sense of space and movement [4].
6. **Wall squat (the invisible chair)** : While not traditionally Japanese, this movement is widely used by modern Japanese physiotherapists and coaches. It activates the quads, glutes, hamstrings, and core, stimulating slow-twitch muscle fibers responsible for endurance and postural support. Wall squats also promote capillarization (blood vessel growth), improve leg circulation, and are linked to maintaining bone density to prevent osteoporosis [4, 5].
   These movements, when practiced consistently for just 5-7 minutes a day, can compound daily into improved strength, balance, and confidence [6].
