Please Stop Mixing Baking Soda and Vinegar to Make Cleaning Paste

Caroline Mullen is a writer focused on cleaning and organizing. Every rug in her apartment is machine-washable, but you can blame her dog for that.

Baking soda and distilled white vinegar are two of the most used cleaners in my home. I often say that there’s not much you can’t clean if you have baking soda, vinegar, and dish soap on hand.

I reach for baking soda to scour baked-on grease from my stainless steel skillet and aluminum baking sheets. I mix a bit of distilled white vinegar and water in a spray bottle to make an effective glass cleaner, and with a drop of dish soap, it’s my go-to solution for deodorizing dog accidents on carpet.

But it drives me absolutely mad when I see people on social media combining baking soda and vinegar to make a cleaning solution.

Plenty of TikTok videos tout the benefits of mixing baking soda and vinegar to make a cleaning paste or drain cleaner, and it makes sense why people think this combo is effective. When you pour vinegar into baking soda, it bubbles and fizzes, creating the illusion of a hard-working solution.

But in reality, combining these two compounds neutralizes the effectiveness of both, making a much less powerful cleaner than if you were to use them separately.

To understand what exactly happens during this reaction, I reached out to Mark Lipke, a professor of chemistry at Rutgers University. (If you ever made a volcano for a science fair, though, you may have an idea.)

Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) is a base, which means it’s higher on the pH scale, or more alkaline, Lipke explained in a phone interview. Vinegar (diluted acetic acid), on the other hand, is an acid, so it’s lower on the pH scale. Both acids and bases want to move toward pH neutrality, he explained, so they have a tendency to react with other compounds.

The baking soda and vinegar chemical reaction

A diagram explaining how baking soda and vinegar makes sodium acetate and carbonic acid when combined. The sodium acetate stays the same, but the carbonic acid separates into carbon dioxide bubbles and water.

NYT Wirecutter

So when you add vinegar to baking soda, the solution quickly neutralizes, becoming carbonic acid and a salt called sodium acetate. The unstable carbonic acid then rapidly breaks down into water and carbon dioxide gas, which produces the telltale bubbles of the reaction. The carbon dioxide gas forces its way out of the liquid and dissipates into the air, and once the reaction is complete — that is, it stops fizzing — you’re left with just water and sodium acetate.

Why isn’t this an effective cleaner?

Think of it this way: You probably wouldn’t opt for salt water as a cleaning solution, right? That’s essentially what’s left over when you combine vinegar and baking soda.

Instead, you’re likely to get much better results by choosing either acidic vinegar or basic baking soda, depending on what you’re trying to clean.

When to use vinegar

Acidic cleaners like vinegar are particularly effective at removing mineral buildup and soap scum, according to Michael Capracotta, head of technical service, and Kayleigh Foster, director of marketing and technical services, at BASF, a chemical manufacturing company that makes ingredients such as enzymes and surfactants for home cleaning products.

Vinegar, which has a pH of about 2.4, can dissolve hard-water stains, soap scum, and mineral buildup on bathroom fixtures and shower doors, Capracotta and Foster pointed out. I’ve also had great success using it to descale my espresso machine. That’s because the acid reacts with alkaline minerals like calcium carbonate in limescale or hard-water deposits and breaks them down into soluble salts, water, and carbon dioxide, which you can easily wipe away.

As a general rule, it’s best to start with the least powerful cleaning product and then move up to heavier solutions if necessary.

If food-grade vinegar isn’t cutting it for a particular task, you might consider using a cleaning vinegar, which typically contains a higher concentration of acetic acid, or a stronger acid cleaner. Lipke suggests using citric acid (3 pH to 6 pH) or citric-acid products, which are usually more acidic (and smell better than vinegar).

Bar Keepers Friend (1.5 pH to 2.5 pH) is another acidic cleaner — and a favorite among Wirecutter staffers. It utilizes oxalic acid and excels at cleaning stainless steel, grout, and even mineral deposits from inside toilets.

You should not use acidic cleaners on natural stone, such as marble, granite, or limestone, or on hardwood, as they can etch or dull those surfaces. Some acidic cleaners work well on metal surfaces like stainless steel or copper, but always check the manufacturer’s instructions and test the cleaner on an inconspicuous patch before cleaning an entire item.

Whenever you use an acidic cleaner, wear gloves to prevent skin irritation. And never combine bleach with any acidic cleaners, as that can produce toxic chlorine gas.

When to use baking soda

Basic cleaners like baking soda are great at cutting through grease, oils, and everyday dirt, and some can work as disinfectants, as well.

Baking soda, thanks to its slightly abrasive texture, is ideal for scouring many hard surfaces without damaging them, such as stainless steel, oven interiors, metal fixtures, and glass on appliances such as ovens and microwaves. Baking soda can also neutralize odors in refrigerators or on rugs and upholstery because it reacts with acidic odor molecules and neutralizes them, similar to the reaction between baking soda and vinegar.

Baking soda (which has a pH of 8 to 9) isn’t the most alkaline cleaning product available, but bear in mind that the higher on the pH scale you go, the more corrosive a solution is, so take extra care when handling these substances.

Simple Green Industrial Cleaner & Degreaser (about 8.5 pH to 9.5 pH) and Krud Kutter Original Concentrated Cleaner/Degreaser (about 12.5 pH to 13.5 pH) are popular alkaline cleaners that work particularly well on soap scum, bathtub rings, and oily kitchen messes. They’re also great for removing oil and grease from motorized equipment and cleaning work gloves, according to Wirecutter writer Tim Heffernan.

Household chlorine bleach, which has a pH of about 12, is another option. Also, bleach and cleaners containing bleach can kill mold and mildew and disinfect a wide range of household surfaces, such as toilets, tubs, and sinks.

Use caution when applying basic cleaning products to soft metals such as aluminum or brass, to natural fibers like silk or wool, or to sealed wood. Refer to the manufacturer’s instructions for safe usage and dilution ratios, and pay special attention to warnings regarding ventilation. As with acidic cleaners, wear gloves when handling alkaline cleaning products and perform a small patch test on surfaces.

Baking soda and vinegar will continue to be mainstays in my lineup of cleaning products, and we still recommend both at Wirecutter for various tasks. As for combining the two, that’s better saved for a science-fair project.
