**Aquamation**, also known scientifically as alkaline hydrolysis, biocremation, resomation, flameless cremation, or water cremation, is an eco-friendly alternative to traditional flame cremation and burial [1, 2].

Here is a summary of the available information on aquamation based on the provided sources:

**How the Process Works**
The procedure accelerates the body's natural decomposition process using water, an alkali solution, heat, and pressure [1].

* **The Chamber:** The deceased is placed into a pressurized, stainless-steel vessel filled with a mixture of 95% water and 5% potassium hydroxide [1, 2].
* **Breakdown:** The solution is gently heated to about 300°F (150°C), breaking down all organic matter into a sterile liquid composed of amino acids, peptides, sugars, and salts over the course of 4 to 6 hours [1-3]. Alternatively, lower temperatures of around 208°F (98°C) can be used, which takes 14 to 16 hours [2].
* **The Remains:** The process completely disintegrates DNA and leaves behind sterile bone remains (calcium phosphate) [1-3]. These bones are dried, processed into a white powdery ash, and returned to the family in an urn [1, 3].
* **The Liquid:** The green-brown liquid byproduct is then safely disposed of through the sanitary sewer system or utilized as fertilizer in a green space [3].

**Key Benefits**

* **Environmental Impact:** Aquamation uses only about one-quarter of the energy required for flame cremation and produces no direct greenhouse gas emissions [4, 5].
* **Gentler on Remains:** Because it relies on water rather than destructive fire, the process yields about 20% more ash remains for families to keep [4].
* **Intact Medical Implants:** Implants such as pacemakers, artificial joints, and dental fillings are not destroyed during aquamation and can be safely recovered [4].
* **Pathogen Destruction:** The process is highly effective at inactivating viruses, bacteria, and prions, which is why it is also heavily utilized in the agricultural industry to safely dispose of infected animal carcasses [6].

**Cost, Legality, and Availability**
The process was originally patented in 1888 to process animal carcasses into plant food, and the Mayo Clinic began utilizing it for human cadavers donated to science in 2005 [6]. Today, it is used for both humans and pets [6, 7].

As of 2022, alkaline hydrolysis is legal for human disposition in 24 U.S. states and is also available in various regions across Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Mexico, and South Africa [8-12]. In California, the practice became legal for humans in July 2020 [7]. It typically costs between $3,200 and $5,000, making it slightly more expensive than traditional cremation but significantly cheaper than a formal casket burial [13]. For example, Pacific Interment Service in California charges $3,750 for direct aquamation if the deceased is picked up from a hospital, and $4,150 for a home or care facility removal [14].

**Religious Perspectives**
Religious views on aquamation are mixed and often align with a religion's stance on traditional cremation [15]:

* **Acceptance:** Former Anglican Archbishop Desmond Tutu famously chose to be aquamated following his death in 2021 [11, 15]. Native Hawaiians also view it favorably, as the clean bones it produces approximate their traditional burial rituals [16].
* **Opposition:** Religions that strictly forbid cremation, such as Judaism and Islam, are likely to reject aquamation [17]. Similarly, the Eastern Orthodox Church and the Baháʼí Faith discourage or forbid it [15, 17]. While the Catholic Church allows flame cremation in certain circumstances, leaders in the U.S. have largely condemned aquamation, arguing that flushing the liquid remains into the sewer system is disrespectful to the dignity of the human body [15].
