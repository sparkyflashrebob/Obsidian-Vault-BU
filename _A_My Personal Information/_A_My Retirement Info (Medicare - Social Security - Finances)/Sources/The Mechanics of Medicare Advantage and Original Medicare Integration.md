The short answer is **no, a Medicare Advantage plan does not totally supersede or replace your Medicare.** You do not leave the Medicare system when you join one.
Instead, a Medicare Advantage plan (also known as **Medicare Part C** ) acts as an *alternative delivery model* . You are choosing to have a private, Medicare-approved insurance company manage and administer your government Medicare benefits.
Here is exactly how they work in tandem and how the dynamic plays out in practice.

---

#### 1. The Legal Foundation: You Are Still in Medicare

To enroll in a Medicare Advantage plan, you **must** be enrolled in both Medicare Part A (Hospital Insurance) and Medicare Part B (Medical Insurance).

* **The Funding:** The federal government pays a fixed monthly amount to the private insurance company to take on your care.
* **The Premium:** You must continue to pay your monthly Medicare Part B premium to the government (along with any additional monthly premium the private plan might charge, though many offer a $0 premium).

#### 2. How the Benefits Work in Tandem

By law, a Medicare Advantage plan is required to cover **every single medically necessary service** that Original Medicare covers. The private plan essentially steps into the shoes of the federal government to handle your claims.
However, the day-to-day mechanics of how those benefits are delivered look very different from Original Medicare:

| Feature | Original Medicare (Parts A & B) | Medicare Advantage (Part C) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Provider Network** | **No network.** You can see any doctor or hospital in the U.S. that accepts Medicare. | **Restricted networks (HMO/PPO).** You generally must use the plan’s local network of doctors and facilities. |
| **Care Management** | Rarely requires **prior authorization** or specialist referrals. | Frequently requires **prior authorization** for procedures and referrals from a primary care doctor. |
| **Cost Structure** | You pay a deductible, then generally a **20% coinsurance** for Part B services. There is **no annual cap** on out-of-pocket costs. | You pay set **copays** (e.g., $20 for a doctor visit). By law, plans must include an **annual out-of-pocket maximum** to protect you financially. |
| **Prescription Drugs** | **Not included.** You must buy a standalone Part D plan. | **Bundled.** Most plans include Part D prescription drug coverage automatically. |
| **Extra Perks** | Does not cover routine dental, vision, hearing, or fitness programs. | Frequently includes extra benefits like routine dental cleanings, eye exams, hearing aids, or gym memberships. |

---

#### 3. The One Major Exception: Hospice Care

The clearest proof that Medicare Advantage doesn't totally erase your Original Medicare is how **hospice care** is handled.
If you are enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan and eventually require hospice services, the private plan steps aside for that specific care. **Original Medicare Part A** comes back to the forefront and directly covers your hospice benefits, while your Advantage plan continues to cover any remaining healthcare needs unrelated to the terminal illness.

#### The Core Takeaway

Think of it as a fork in the road for how your benefits are administered.
If you choose **Original Medicare** , you deal directly with the federal government, have total geographic freedom of providers, but need to buy separate pieces—like a standalone Part D drug plan and a supplemental **Medigap** policy—to cover the 20% financial exposure.
If you choose **Medicare Advantage** , you opt into a private insurance framework that packages medical, hospital, and usually drug coverage into a single entity with an out-of-pocket safety cap, in exchange for working within a localized network and navigating their care-approval rules.
