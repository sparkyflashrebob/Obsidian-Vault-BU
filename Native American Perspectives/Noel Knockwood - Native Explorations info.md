**Summary:** Noel Knockwood (1932–2014) was a profoundly influential Mi'kmaq spiritual leader, human rights advocate, educator, and _Keptin_ (Captain) of the [[Mi'kmaq]] Grand Council (_Sante' Mawi'omi_). He played a central role in revitalizing traditional Indigenous spirituality in Eastern Canada during an era of intense cultural suppression, serving as a vital bridge between centuries-old oral traditions and modern institutional spaces.
#mi-k_maq #Msit_No_-kmaq
### 1. Spiritual Leadership & the Grand Council

- **Keptin of the Grand Council:** As a lifelong member and Keptin of the _Sante' Mawi'omi_ (the traditional governing body of the Mi'kmaq Nation), Knockwood served as a spiritual custodian, keeper of the sacred pipe, and ceremonial leader.
    
- **Revival of Traditional Ceremonies:** During the mid-20th century, when centuries of colonial policy and church pressure had driven many traditional ceremonies underground, Knockwood worked fearlessly to revive the sacred sweat lodge, pipe ceremonies, sunrise greetings, and the medicine wheel framework for the younger generations of First Nations people.
    
- **Addiction & Healing Work:** He integrated traditional spiritual concepts—such as the Four Directions and sweetgrass purification—into addictions counseling and community health, emphasizing that holistic healing for Indigenous people requires reconnecting to ancestral spirit and the natural world.
    

### 2. Historical Milestone: Legislative Sergeant-at-Arms

- **First Indigenous Appointment:** In 1999, Noel Knockwood made Canadian history by being appointed **Sergeant-at-Arms of the Nova Scotia House of Assembly**, a post he held until his retirement in 2005.
    
- **Symbol of Reconciliation:** He was the first Indigenous person to hold this historic legislative role in Canada. His presence in the legislature—often wearing his traditional regalia and carrying the ceremonial mace—stood as a living symbol of Mi'kmaq sovereignty and peaceful coexistence.
    

### 3. Core Spiritual Teachings & Philosophy

- **All My Relations (_Msit No'kmaq_):** Knockwood’s prayers consistently emphasized the foundational Mi'kmaq principle of [[_Msit No'kmaq_]]—recognizing that humans, animals, plants, waters, and the cosmos form one interconnected web of kinship with no hierarchy of domination.
    
- [[**Seven Sacred Gifts:**]] In his teachings across schools, universities, and healing lodges, he centered personal conduct around ancestral values: [[Love, Respect, Courage, Honesty, Wisdom, Humility, and Truth]].
    
- **Earth as Living Sanctuary:** His invocations, including the Four Directions prayer published in _Earth Prayers_, reflect his belief that the land is not a resource to exploit, but an animate teacher that mirrors the inner seasons of the human heart.
    

### 4. Legacy and National Honors

- **National Aboriginal Achievement Award (2002):** Received the national award in the Heritage and Spirituality category for his dedication to preserving Indigenous sacred customs.
    
- **Korean War Veteran:** Prior to his public spiritual career, he served with the Royal Canadian Regiment in the Korean War (1951–1952), a period that informed his later commitment to peace, reconciliation, and healing post-traumatic wounds.


**Summary:** [[_Etuaptmumk_]] ([[Two-Eyed Seeing]]) is a foundational guiding principle introduced by Mi'kmaq Elder Albert Marshall in 2004. It teaches us to view the world through two distinct lenses simultaneously: using one eye to see the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing, and using the other eye to see the strengths of Western sciences and methodologies—learning to use both eyes together for the benefit of all living beings.

### 1. The Core Philosophy of _Etuaptmumk_

Elder Albert Marshall, from the Eskasoni First Nation in Unama'ki (Cape Breton, Nova Scotia), coined the term to provide a collaborative, respectful framework for research, ecological stewardship, healthcare, and education:

- **Complementary, Not Competitive:** Two-Eyed Seeing does not ask one worldview to assimilate, subordinate, or validate the other. Instead, it recognizes that every knowledge system has unique strengths and inherent limitations.
    
- **Weaving Perspectives:** Just as human binocular vision provides depth perception and spatial clarity that a single eye cannot achieve, using both ways of knowing offers a richer, multidimensional understanding of reality.
    
- **A Dynamic Process:** It is not a static set of rules, but an ongoing practice of mutual respect, humility, and co-learning. As Elder Marshall frequently emphasizes, _"You learn to see from one eye with the best in the Indigenous ways of knowing, and from the other eye with the best in the Western ways of knowing... and most importantly, learning to use both these eyes together."_
    

### 2. Contrasting and Complementing the Two Lenses

- **The Indigenous Eye (Relational & Holistic):**
    
    - **Interconnectedness (_Msit No'kmaq_):** Views all aspects of nature—plants, animals, waters, weather, and humans—as kin rather than resources.
        
    - **Generational Wisdom:** Grounded in thousands of years of place-based oral observation, cyclical patterns, and direct relationship with local ecosystems.
        
    - **Ethical Responsibility:** Science and knowledge are inseparable from spiritual duty, gratitude, and care for future generations (often framed as the Seven Generations principle).
        
- **The Western Eye (Analytical & Mechanistic):**
    
    - **Empirical Quantities:** Strengths in controlled experimentation, microscopic analysis, technological data gathering, and mathematical modeling.
        
    - **Categorization:** Breaks complex phenomena down into discrete, measurable variables to understand specific physical mechanisms.
        

### 3. Key Applications in Modern Fields

- **Environmental Science & Climate Stewardship:** Combining satellite remote sensing and meteorological data (Western) with Indigenous phenology—such as ancestral observations of animal migrations, ice behavior, and plant bloom times—produces far more accurate local climate adaptation plans.
    
- **Holistic Healthcare & Integrative Medicine:** Bridging modern pharmacological or surgical treatments with somatic, spiritual, and community-centered healing modalities that treat the whole person rather than just an isolated symptom.
    
- **Ecological Forestry & Fisheries Management:** Merging quantitative fish stock tracking with the Mi'kmaq ethic of _Netukulimk_ (harvesting only what is needed to sustain life without compromising the integrity of the natural environment).
    

### 4. Spiritual and Philosophical Significance

In contemporary contemplative and healing spaces, _Etuaptmumk_ serves as a powerful antidote to reductionism:

- **Restoring Wholeness:** It heals the false divide between the intellect and the heart, acknowledging empirical rigor while honoring the sacredness of the natural world.
    
- **Epistemic Humility:** It invites modern thinkers and practitioners to step away from intellectual dominance and sit in a circle of shared wisdom, recognizing that no single culture holds a monopoly on truth.


**Summary:** [[_Netukulimk_]] (pronounced _ne-du-goo-lemk_) is the traditional Mi'kmaq guiding philosophy and sacred law governing how humans harvest from the natural world. It defines a sacred covenant of sustainability: taking only what is necessary for individual and community sustenance, with deep reverence, without waste, and ensuring that the integrity and abundance of creation remain intact for the next seven generations. #sustainabiliity

### 1. The Core Meaning of _Netukulimk_

The Mi'kmaq Grand Council (_Sante' Mawi'omi_) formally defines _Netukulimk_ as the use of the natural bounty provided by the Creator for the self-support and well-being of the individual and the community by achieving adequate nutrition, clothing, shelter, medicine, and livelihoods:

- **[[Sacred Limit]]:** It forbids greed, hoarding, exploitation, or taking anything beyond one's true needs.
    
- **[[Non-Compromise]]:** A harvest is only legitimate if it does not impair, diminish, or compromise the natural balance and biological diversity of the ecosystem.
    
- **[[Co-Existence over Ownership]]:** Land, water, animals, and plants are not viewed as commodities or "natural resources" to be bought and sold, but as gifts provided by the Creator to be stewarded collaboratively.
    

### 2. The Four Pillars of the Practice

In daily life, hunting, fishing, foraging, and governance, _Netukulimk_ is enacted through four primary relational principles:

- **Responsibility (_Up-wul-tikt_):** Recognizing one’s personal and communal duty to care for the environment. Human beings do not stand above the food web; they are participants within it with an active obligation to protect it.
    
- **Respect (_Mawiomi_):** Offering gratitude to the spirits of the plants, waters, and animals that surrender their lives for human nourishment. This is traditionally accompanied by tobacco offerings and prayer before any harvest begins.
    
- **Reciprocity:** Living by the law of give-and-take. When something is taken from the earth, something must be protected, restored, or given back in return to maintain equilibrium.
    
- **Relationship (_Msit No'kmaq_):** Acting from the lived reality that all parts of creation are related kin ("All My Relations"). You do not pillage or deplete your own family.

### 4. Legal & Modern Ecological Significance

_Netukulimk_ is not merely an ancient concept; it forms the legal and cultural foundation of modern Mi'kmaq self-governance and treaty rights:

- **The Marshall Decision (1999):** The Supreme Court of Canada affirmed the Mi'kmaq treaty right to hunt, fish, and gather in pursuit of a "moderate livelihood." Mi'kmaq leaders and communities continually anchor the definition of a moderate livelihood within _Netukulimk_, distinguishing community well-being from industrial over-exploitation.
    
- **Community-Led Fisheries and Forestry:** Modern Mi'kmaq marine and forest managers use _Netukulimk_ as an ecological standard, setting seasonal limits, protecting spawning grounds, and rejecting clear-cutting in favor of selective, regenerative practices.


**Summary:** The **[[Seven Generations Principle]]** is an ancient philosophical and governing law originating with the _Haudenosaunee_ (Iroquois Confederacy / People of the Longhouse) via the Great Law of Peace ([[_Gayanashagowa_]]). It dictates that every major decision made today—whether social, political, or ecological—must be evaluated by its impact on the seventh generation yet unborn. It shifts human awareness from short-term personal gain to deep, multigenerational stewardship and accountability.
#great_law_of_peace 
### 1. Origins in the Great Law of Peace (_Gayanashagowa_)

The foundational roots of the Seven Generations principle come from the constitution of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy (composed of the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and later Tuscarora nations):

- **The Charge to the Chiefs:** When leaders sat in council, the Peacemaker instructed them that their skin must be "seven spans thick" (resistant to anger, offense, and personal ego) and their minds must look far ahead:
    
    > _"Look and listen for the welfare of the whole people and have always in view not only the present but also the coming generations, even those whose faces are yet beneath the surface of the ground — the unborn generation."_
    
- **The Council of Women:** Clan Mothers held significant authority in selecting and holding accountable the male chiefs, ensuring that governance remained focused on the long-term domestic, ecological, and spiritual security of future families.
    

### 2. The Seven-Generation Temporal Horizon (The Continuum of 150–200 Years)

Rather than viewing time as a flat linear timeline, the principle places the present generation directly in the **center** of a seven-generation continuum:

- **[[Looking Back Three Generations]]:** Honoring the wisdom, struggles, language, and cultural gifts handed down by **Great-Grandparents, Grandparents, and Parents**.
    
- **[[The Present Center]]:** The current generation living right now, acting as the responsible custodians and bridge.
    
- **[[Looking Forward Three Generations]]:** Taking active responsibility for the world that will be inherited by **Children, Grandchildren, and Great-Grandchildren**.
    
- **[[Direct Relational Span]]:** A person might reasonably hold their great-grandparents' hands as a child and their great-grandchildren's hands as an elder—making all seven generations an intimate, living circle of human relationship rather than an abstract future.
    

### 3. Core Pillars in Practice and Governance

- **[[Resource Conservatism & Restraint]]:** Trees are not clear-cut, waters are not fouled, and game is not over-hunted because doing so robs future generations of their basic birthright to clean air, fresh water, and fertile soil.
    
- **[[Consensus-Based Decision Making]]:** Community deliberations take time. Fast, impulsive decisions driven by immediate financial return or convenience are seen as inherently dangerous.
    
- **[[Intergenerational Justice]]:** Inheriting an intact world without debt—ecological, financial, or moral—is considered the primary ethical test of good leadership.
    

### 4. Contemporary Applications & Global Relevance

- **Modern Environmental Ethics:** The principle serves as the moral benchmark for global climate justice, opposing the dominant economic paradigm of quarterly corporate earnings and short-term political election cycles.
    
- **Ecological Economics & Law:** Legal scholars and environmentalists draw on the principle to argue for the "Rights of Nature" and the legal standing of future generations in constitutional and climate litigation.
    
- **Community Healing and Cultural Continuity:** Indigenous wellness initiatives use the Seven Generations framework to address intergenerational trauma—recognizing that while historical trauma may ripple forward, seven generations of intentional healing, ceremony, and language revival can permanently heal ancestral lines.

It represents one of the world’s oldest participatory democracies, pioneering a sophisticated federal system of checks and balances, individual liberties, consensus governance, and Clan Mother oversight that directly informed modern democratic principles and the structure of the U.S. Constitution.

### 1. Origins and the Great Peacemaker

Centuries ago, across what is now upstate New York and southeastern Canada, five sovereign nations—the **Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca** (later joined by the **Tuscarora**)—were trapped in cycles of blood feud and warfare.

- **The Peacemaker (_Dekanawidah_):** Brought the spiritual and legal message of _Skén:nen_ (Peace), _Kahsátstenshera_ (Strength through unity), and _Ka’nikonhrí:yo_ (a Good Mind / Righteousness).
    
- **Hiawatha (_Ayenwá:tha_) & Jigonhsasee:** Hiawatha helped articulate the vision and console grief, while Jigonhsasee (the "Mother of Nations") provided essential moral counsel, establishing women’s central governing role.
    
- **The Tree of Peace:** To seal the alliance, the nations buried their weapons of war beneath the roots of a giant White Pine at Onondaga. The eagle placed at the top of the tree serves as a vigilant guardian watching for threats to peace.

### 2. Structural Pillars of Governance

- **Bicameral / Multilateral Federalism:** Power is distributed across sovereign nations within an overarching confederacy:
    
    - **Older Brothers:** Mohawk (Keepers of the Eastern Door) and Seneca (Keepers of the Western Door).
        
    - **Younger Brothers:** Oneida and Cayuga.
        
    - **Firekeepers:** Onondaga, who host the central council, manage the agenda, and hold veto/reconciliation power.
        
- **Consensus-Based Deliberation:** Decisions pass back and forth between councils until universal agreement is reached, preventing a simple 51% majority from tyrannizing a 49% minority.
    
- **Matrilineal Balance of Power:** Clan Mothers hold supreme veto power. They select, advise, and possess the legal authority to depose (_"de-horn"_) male chiefs (_Hoyenah_) who display arrogance, dishonesty, or disregard for the Seven Generations.
    
- **Fundamental Civil Freedoms:** The Great Law explicitly guarantees freedom of speech, freedom of religion, protection against unwarranted entry into homes, and political asylum for refugees.
    

### 3. Historical Influence on Modern Constitutional Democracy

The Haudenosaunee framework provided a living, practical model of federal union for the American framers:

- **Benjamin Franklin’s Albany Plan of Union (1754):** Franklin directly cited the unity of the Six Nations when proposing the first formal union of the American colonies:
    
    > _"It would be a strange thing if Six Nations of ignorant savages should be capable of forming a scheme for such an union... and yet that a like union should be impracticable for ten or a dozen English colonies."_
    
- **1744 Treaty of Lancaster:** Onondaga orator Canassatego formally advised colonial leaders to unite their separate colonies as the Haudenosaunee had, handing them a bundle of arrows to demonstrate that while a single arrow breaks easily, a bundle bound together cannot be broken.
    
- **U.S. Senate Resolution 331 (1988):** The 100th U.S. Congress formally passed a concurrent resolution acknowledging:
    
    > _"The confederation of the original Thirteen Colonies into one republic was influenced by the political system developed by the Iroquois Confederacy, as were many of the democratic principles which were incorporated into the Constitution itself."_
    

### 4. Key Contrasts with Western Governance

- **[[Inclusion vs. Exclusion]]:** In the original Haudenosaunee model, women possessed direct political power and property rights—rights denied to women in Western democracies until the 20th century.
    
- **[[Earth Kinship vs. Property Ownership]]:** Governance under the Great Law includes an explicit duty of care to the natural world, plants, waters, and animals, rather than treating nature solely as an economic asset to be partitioned.
