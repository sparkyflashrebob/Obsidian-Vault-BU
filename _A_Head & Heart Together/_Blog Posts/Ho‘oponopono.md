# Ho‘oponopono: Untangling the Knots of the Heart

October 8, 2026  
#Mindfulness #Hooponopono #Forgiveness #Buddhism #Stoicism #HeadAndHeart #InnerHealing #PersonalReflection

---

Like many people, I first stumbled upon the Hawaiian tradition of **Ho‘oponopono** through four gentle, heartfelt phrases:

> *“I’m sorry. Please forgive me. Thank you. I love you.”*

For a long time, I carried those words like a quiet pocket stone. In moments when my mind felt overwhelmed or when a relationship felt strained, reciting them felt like a soft breath of air. 

Yet, the more I looked into the living roots of the Hawaiian tradition—and the more I looked honestly at my own stubborn habits of resentment—the more I realized that this practice is not a superficial positive-thinking exercise. It is an ancient, courageous technology for truth-telling, reconciliation, and the systematic untangling of human pain. 

I write these reflections not to offer advice or prescribe how anyone else should live, but simply to share how I am trying to work with my own heart when it gets tangled in judgment, grievance, and fear.

---

## What *Pono* Asks of Me

To understand why this practice resonates so deeply with me, I had to understand the language it comes from. In ‘Ōlelo Hawai‘i (the Hawaiian language), words carry *mana*—spiritual breath, intention, and living force.

At the center of it all is the word **Pono**. 

I used to think of "righteousness" in a rigid, moralistic way—about being "good" or being "right." But in Hawaiian understanding, *pono* is not about winning an argument or adhering to rigid dogma. It describes a state of **living harmony, natural balance, and right alignment**. To be in *pono* means that my thoughts, my speech, and my actions are in healthy relationship with myself, with the people around me, with my ancestors, and with the living earth. 

When a word in Hawaiian is doubled—reduplicated—it signifies an ongoing, deepening action. Thus, **Ponopono** means:
* To put completely in order.
* To rectify repeatedly and thoroughly.
* To straighten out what has become tangled.
* To tend continuously to balance, the way one tends a garden every single morning.

And the prefix **Ho‘o** is causative—it means *"to cause, to set into active motion."*

When I hold that in my mind, **Ho‘oponopono** becomes a daily commitment: **“to set thoroughly right; to restore balance.”** It isn't a passive hope that things will get better on their own. It is an invitation for me to do the quiet, honest inner labor of restoring harmony wherever friction has taken root in my life.

## The Knots I Carry in My Body

In traditional Hawaiian culture, disharmony was understood through the concept of **Hihia**—a word describing a tangled, knotted mess, like fishing nets snagged on coral reefs beneath the water. 

The elders recognized that when resentments, unspoken hurt, jealousy, or secret guilt linger between people, a knot forms. That knot blocks our vital energy, leaving us tight, exhausted, and sick at heart.

When I am completely honest with myself, I know that feeling intimately. 
* I feel it when my jaw clenches while replaying a past conversation.
* I feel it as a tightness in my chest when I read an email from someone I feel misunderstood by.
* I feel it in the restless energy that keeps me awake at 2:00 AM, holding a silent trial in my head where I am the prosecutor, the judge, and the jury.

In the traditional family gathering, the elders led the family through **Mahiki** (slowly untangling the threads of grievance without interruption) toward **Kala**—the act of unbinding. In Hawaiian, *Kala* literally means *"to loosen, to untie, to set free."* 

The core realization of *Kala* is something I have to remind myself of almost daily:  
**“I unbind you from my grievance, and in doing so, I unbind myself.”**

---

## Cleaning the Projector Behind My Eyes

A profound turning point in my personal practice came when I encountered the teachings of the Hawaiian kahuna Morrnah Simeona, who adapted this ancestral ceremony into an internalized spiritual practice. 

Her core insight struck a nerve that immediately connected with everything I have studied in Buddhist psychology and Stoicism:

**Most of my suffering does not come from what is happening out in the world; it comes from the movie my own mind is projecting onto reality.**

I often catch myself acting like someone in a movie theater who dislikes the movie and furiously runs up to the screen with a bottle of spray cleaner, trying to scrub the actors off the wall. 

When someone upsets me, my first instinct is almost always to rush up to the "screen"—to try to change them, fix them, lecture them, or demand that they admit they were wrong. 

Yet, no matter how hard I scrub the screen, the picture never changes. The image isn't originating on the wall out there. **The image is originating in the projector sitting right behind my own eyes.**

The light coming out of my projector is colored by my own unexamined memories:
* My old childhood fears of being overlooked or dismissed.
* My defensive need to feel competent and in control.
* The accumulated scars of past betrayals.

In Buddhism, these are the *saṅkhāras*—the mental fabrications and conditioned perceptions that color everything I see. The Buddha observed that holding onto anger is like grasping a red-hot coal with the intent of throwing it at someone else: *I am the one whose hand is blistering.*

For me, Ho‘oponopono is the conscious decision to stop fighting the screen, turn around, walk up to the projection booth, and gently clean the lens of my own heart.

---

## The Stoic Inversion: Where My Responsibility Lies

This insight directly mirrors what the Stoic philosophers called the *Dichotomy of Control*. 

Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius constantly reminded themselves of a simple, sobering truth: other people’s actions, words, opinions, and past deeds do not belong to me. They are outside my control. The only territory that belongs to me is my own *hegemonikon*—my ruling center, my interpretations, my judgments, and my character.

Marcus Aurelius wrote in his private journal:
> *“If you are distressed by anything external, the pain is not due to the thing itself, but to your estimate of it; and this you have the power to revoke at any moment.”*

When Ho‘oponopono speaks of taking 100% responsibility, my ego initially recoiled. I thought, *“Why should I take responsibility when they were the one who acted badly?”* 

Over time, I began to understand: **responsibility is not fault or blame.** 

Responsibility simply means acknowledging that **the knot is living inside of me.** The other person may be across town or asleep in another bed, but the constriction is in *my* throat, the elevated cortisol is in *my* blood, and the bitterness is clouding *my* day. 

The Stoic inversion changes the questions I ask myself:
* *Instead of asking:* “Why did they do this to me, and how can I force them to realize it?”
* *I try to ask:* “What unexamined expectation or wound within my own projector is making this moment feel intolerable? What in me needs healing so I can respond with dignity and peace rather than reactive bitterness?”

## How I Am Trying to Practice in My Own Daily Life

I do not write this as someone who has mastered this art. Far from it. I still get defensive. I still catch my internal Judge and Jury banging the gavel in traffic or during difficult family moments. 

But when I remember to pause, here is the personal process I try to walk myself through—offered simply as something you might consider in your own quiet moments:

1. **I try to feel the knot first, before I speak:** When resentment or irritation flares up, I try to stop talking. I place a hand on my chest or stomach and simply notice the physical tightness. I tell myself: *“A knot is forming here. Breathe into it.”*
2. **I remember the projector:** I take a slow, deliberate breath down to my feet. I remind myself that my reactive interpretation is not the whole truth. It is just my mind's current projection.
3. **I speak the four phrases inward to my own reactive mind:** Rather than chanting them outward at someone else, I direct them to the hurt or frightened part of myself:
   * *“I’m sorry”* — for how quickly I abandon my peace and jump into judgment.
   * *“Please forgive me”* — for holding onto resentment and punishing myself with this tightness.
   * *“Thank you”* — for this difficult friction, because it shows me exactly where my heart is still guarded, unhealed, and in need of grace.
   * *“I love you”* — returning my attention to compassion, honoring the fragile, messy humanity in myself and in the person across from me.
4. **I practice the unbinding (*Kala*):** I deliberately exhale and imagine letting the cord drop. Like the Hawaiian elders concluding their gathering, I try to draw a quiet line under the grievance: *I do not need to carry this stone into tomorrow.*

---

## A Final Reflection

None of this means becoming passive or tolerating abuse. Setting healthy boundaries and speaking hard truths is an essential part of living in *pono*. 

The difference lies in where the action comes from. 

When I act out of an uncleaned projector—fueled by anger, righteousness, and fear—I almost always make the fire hotter. But when I take the time to clean my own lens first, whatever boundary I need to set is quiet, steady, and clear. 

At this stage in my life, I find I have very little interest in winning debates or collecting proof that I was right. I am far more interested in whether my heart is open, whether I am sleeping peacefully at night, and whether my presence brings a little more ease into the room.

I leave these reflections with you not as an instruction, but as an open mirror:  
*What knots might you be carrying in your own heart today? And what would it feel like to gently set them down?*
