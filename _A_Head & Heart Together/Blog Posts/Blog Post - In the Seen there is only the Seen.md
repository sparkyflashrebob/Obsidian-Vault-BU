> "In the seen, there is only the seen; in the heard, there is only the heard; in the sensed, there is only the sensed; in the cognized, there is only the cognized... When there is no 'you' in connection with that, there is no 'you' there. You are neither here nor there nor in between. This is the end of suffering."
> 
> — **The Bāhiya Sutta** (as translated by Joseph Goldstein)

A lot of the exhaustion we carry through an ordinary day doesn't actually come from the things that happen to us. It comes from the heavy mountain of story we pile right on top of them.

Think about something as simple as getting a flat tire on the side of the road. Physically, there’s a whole situation right there in front of you: a car, a tire that's gone flat, and a jack and a spare sitting in the trunk. That is the whole physical reality. But within ten seconds—usually quicker—the mind kicks in, and we start compounding our discomfort of the situation. We mutter about the money it’s going to cost out of a tight fixed retirement budget, the appointment we’re going to miss, and why on earth we always seem to have such bad luck. We aren’t just turning a lug wrench; we’re wrestling with an entire story about our situation, how badly we feel about it, and why the world treats us this way.

Most days, it feels like we walk around wearing a heavy Velcro jacket. Every unexpected expense, every sharp ache in a joint, and the general wear and tear of navigating the day with limited physical stamina hooks right into us. It sticks, and we burn so much energy trying to rearrange the outside world just so we don’t have to feel uncomfortable inside.

When you look at how our senses actually work, they are simply taking in the world. The eyes register light so we can see what’s going on. The ears catch sounds to alert us to our surroundings. The skin tells us if it’s hot or cold so we know whether to put a jacket on or take one off. That incoming stream of life is completely neutral—it's just information.

Where we get caught is the split second right after we become aware of it. We immediately ask: _What does this mean to me? Is it for me or against me?_

If a sudden, loud clatter breaks out in a room, the sound itself isn't hurting us. What hurts is the inner ledger complaining afterward that it shouldn’t be there, or that folks ought to be more considerate. As the meditation teacher Ajahn Chah used to say, the sound isn’t bothering us; we are bothering the sound.

I catch myself doing this all the time. I'll be sitting quietly and suddenly get this tight feeling in my chest, a clenched jaw, or a throbbing headache. That pain isn't a defect or a failure on my part. It’s just an honest signpost reminding me that I’m arguing with reality—demanding that this moment be different than it already is.

Science reminds us of something humbling here: there is a real physical delay between when an event occurs, when our nervous system registers it, and when our conscious mind realizes it happened. By the time we become aware of a sound, an ache, or a comment, it has already taken place. It is already part of the past. There is nothing in the universe we can do to make it un-happen.

So any fight we put up in that exact moment is purely a reaction. When we see that, it points to the immense value of a simple practice: the pause when agitated.

That pause isn’t wasted time. It gives us a little breathing room to meet our experience with compassion for ourselves first. When we notice that inner tightening—that knee-jerk urge to snap back or stew in frustration—we can treat the discomfort itself as a gentle signpost. Instead of reacting, we catch ourselves right there. We relax the body, soften the shoulders, and steady the nervous system with whatever grounded tool fits the moment. Maybe that means resting our attention on a slow, natural breath. Maybe it's a simple, soothing practice like holding one finger with the opposite hand. We aren't trying to magically fix the world; we're just calming our own storm so we don't bite the hook.

Where this rubber really meets the road is in our everyday relationships and work. It is one thing to sit quietly on a cushion, but it is another thing entirely when life gets chaotic around us.

Take walking into a hospital room to visit a sick friend. So often, we go in wanting to be helpful, but within minutes our own internal discomfort takes over the driver's seat. We get uneasy with their groaning, or uncomfortable with their sharp tone, or anxious about the sterile beeping machines. Suddenly, without even meaning to, our mind starts judging the situation or figuring out how to protect our own comfort. It stops being about simply showing up for our friend in their pain, and it quietly becomes all about _us_—our unease, our reaction, our need to fix or escape the feeling in the room.

Or think about being in the middle of a chaotic work environment, where a coworker is spinning out, talking nonstop, and creating little tornadoes of stress in every direction. It’s so easy to let our nervous system get pulled around by the nose, getting angry or trying to manage their mood.

With mindfulness, we can pause right in the middle of the noise and quietly ask: _What is actually needed in this moment?_

Sometimes, the most skillful thing we can do isn't making a grand statement or trying to change someone else. Sometimes it’s just putting our head down, putting on gentle blinders, and doing the simple task sitting right in front of us. We respond kindly when spoken to, we steady our own breath, and we just do our work. That isn't running away; it's bringing a quiet, unshakeable stability into a room that desperately needs it.

When we catch that inner split happening—whether in a hospital room or a noisy office—we don't have to beat ourselves up for feeling rattled. We just notice it. We clear our own side of the street, drop the internal grievance, and come back to the simple reality right in front of us.

This is the exact wall the seeker Bāhiya hit in the ancient teaching story. He had a deep sense of urgency about his life and sought out the Buddha, who cut straight to the point: In the seen, there is only the seen. In the heard, only the heard. In the sensed, only the sensed.

When we meet life right at the doorway of our senses—without an anxious ego scrambling to defend an image, win an argument, or take everything as a personal attack—that inner friction totally collapses.

Earth-based and indigenous cultures have modeled this relationship with life for generations. We aren't separate bosses trying to dominate or control reality; we are simply living participants within a shared, interconnected world.

When we forget this, we turn every bump in the road into a battlefield. You see it online when people get into furious outrages over the plot of a movie, treating light on a screen and sound from a speaker like a personal betrayal because their identity is tied up in it. Our broader cultural discourse runs on that exact same loop with much higher stakes. Genuine human struggles get buried under manufactured panic and tribal flags. We get so caught up in demanding that our team wins the argument that we stop asking if an action actually helps a real person in need.

Living with this kind of simplicity doesn't mean looking away from the hard realities of the world. Pain, economic strain, ecological harm, and the deep divides in our communities are heavy, real things. When we see suffering, that ache in our chest is a healthy sign that care is needed. But if we show up to these monumental challenges carrying our own rage, entitlement, and panic, we are just pouring fuel on a room that is already on fire.

Meeting life with clear seeing gives us room for quiet, frictionless action. When someone is hungry, you share food. When an ecosystem is being harmed, you do what you can to care for the land. When communication turns harsh, you pause, take care of your own nervous system, and step in with kindness and clarity. You don't have to carry the crushing burden of fixing the entire universe all by yourself.

First things first: we drop the extra story, step out of the argument with what has already occurred, and bring steady hands and an open heart to whatever is sitting right in front of us.

Taking a look back over this past week, was there a moment—maybe sitting in traffic, waiting in line, or reading a comment online—where a completely neutral event suddenly had an entire story glued to it? What did that extra narrative look like?

And when things start feeling loud or chaotic around you, what helps you take that pause when agitated? How do you drop the added commentary and step back into just the seen and just the heard?
