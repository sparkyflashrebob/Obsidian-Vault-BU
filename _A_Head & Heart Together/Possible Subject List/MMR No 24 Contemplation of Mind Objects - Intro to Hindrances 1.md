MMR#24 Contemplation of Mind-Objects/Intro to the Hinderances
Last week, we concluded our review of Mindfulness of Mind and talked about the Right Attitude towards meditation as recommended by Ajahn Tejaniya. 
Today we continue on this exploration of the Sattipatthana Sutta, moving into the 4th Foundation of Mindfulness, which is being mindful of mind-objects.

The work of meditation is done purely by the mind; that is the meditation!; as Ajahn Tejaniya reminded us, it is not the objects that are being known or observed that define the meditation but our observation, our awareness itself.

In the Satipatthāna-sutta, four basic objects are mentioned: body, feelings, mind, and, lastly, dhamma (or nature). These objects, that can be observed, are always there, ever-present.

What is not ever-present is this awareness (sati) or a mind that knows these objects;
There are other states that also are not ever-present:
there is samādhi, or stability of mind,
there is faith in the practice (saddhā)
there is wisdom (paññā)
and there is right effort (viriya)
These are not always present.
The word rendered here as “mind-objects” is another name for the polymorphous dhammā (the
dhamma that comes in many forms). In this context dhammā can be understood as comprising
all phenomena classified by way of the categories of the Dhamma, the Buddha’s teaching of
actuality.
There are five areas covered in the fourth Satipattanha
There are the Hindrances, The Aggregates, The Six Bases, The Seven Enlightenment Factors
and this contemplation reaches its climax in the penetration of the teaching at the heart of the
Dhamma— the Four Noble Truths.
Today we will review the first of these, The Five Hindrances.
Read the section:
So have have this new term, pañca-nī-vara-ṇāni, or the five hindrances.
These five hindrances are what are considered to be the main inner obstructions to the
development of concentration, insight, and tranquility. As a result, these five mental states can
hinder progress in both meditation and in our daily lives.
The 5 Hindrances are:
1) Sensual Desire,
2) ill will,
3) sloth and torpor,
4) restlessness and remorse,
5) and, lastly, skeptical doubt.
Sensual desire arises through attending unwisely to a sensually attractive object It is a type of

wanting that seeks for happiness thru the five senses of sight, sound, smell, taste and physical
feeling; Sensual desire is said to be abandoned by meditation on a foul object that we learned
about in the MIndfulness of the Body. It is my personal belief though that Sense Desire can also
be abandoned thru proper wisdom as we noted with the removal of distracting thoughts; In the
Sutta, the Buddha wants us to simply note if there is sense desire in us or there is no sense
desire in us.
ill will arises through attending unwisely to an object we don't care for. It includes all kinds of
thought related to wanting to reject, which include feelings of hostility, resentment and
bitterness; Ill Will is abandoned by developing loving-kindness; In the Sutta, the Buddha wants
us to simply note if there is ill will in us or there is no ill will in us.
sloth and torpor arise by submitting to boredom and laziness. It is a kind of heaviness of body
and dullness of mind which drag one down into disabling inertia and thick depression. Sloth and
torpor are abandoned by arousing energy; In the Sutta, the Buddha wants us to simply note if
there is sloth and torpor in us or there is no sloth and torpor in us.
restlessness and remorse arise through unwisely reflecting on disturbing thoughts. It is the
inability to calm the mind. These are abandoned by wisely reflecting on tranquillity; ***** In the
Sutta, the Buddha wants us to simply note if there is restlessness and worry in us or there is no
restlessness and worry in us.
doubt arises through unwisely reflecting on dubious matters. Dubious matters are things that are
uncertain, questionable, or that we lack confidence or trust in. Doubt is abandoned by study,
investigation, and inquiry. In the Sutta, the Buddha wants us to simply note if there is doubt in us
or if there is no doubt in us.
The hindrances are presented differently within the different traditions depending on the way
each tradition teaches the practice of meditation.
In the Contemporary Insight Meditation tradition, otherwise known as Western Buddhism, the
use of the R.A.I.N. method is used for investigating the hindrances. This is
In the Theravada tradition, which is the one I am most closely associated with, the hindrances
are obstacles to the jhanas, deep stages of concentration.
In the Mahayana tradition, the five hindrances are considered to be obstacles to Samatha or
tranquility meditation.
Ultimately these methods achieve the same result leading to liberating insight.
As we know, Buddhism loves its lists. I mentioned the hindrances above but you could also
describe them as the 3 poisons (greed, hatred, and delusion) that we know from our exploration
of Mindfulness of Mind, plus restlessness or worry and doubt.
These hindrances are the five primary things we can watch for that can obstruct your
meditation.

There is an excellent teaching on how one might be able to understand how the hindrances
affect us thru a simile (comparison) to Water.
When you try to meditate, think of the mind as a bowl of water with 5 different emotional states
that obstruct the pure, clear bright nature of the water.
Desire for the Sense Pleasures is like a bowl of water with all these beautiful dyes in it. Blue
dyes, red dyes, green dyes, yellow dyes. It looks great with all of the beautiful colors but
ultimately you can't see thru it. All you really notice is the color itself.
Likewise, anger and hatred is like seeing thru water that is boiling hot, and bubbling. If you
notice boiling water it is very agitated, the bubbles obscuring everything else.
Drowsiness or lethargy (sloth and torpor) is like stagnant, stale water. Though the water is still,
there is all this algae, moss and slime growing in it. There are no life-giving qualities to it.
Restlessness and worry is like water whipped up by the wind so there are lots of ripples and
waves crashing to and fro, it can't be still. Because it is not still, you also can't see thru it.
Doubt is like dirty, murky water, like a cloudy suspension that been set in a dark place. You can't
see thru it either.
Ajahn Chah likened the true nature of the mind as a clear forest pool. A mind that has
abandoned and overcome the 5 hindrances is like a clear, still, pure pool of water in a bright
place where you can see through clear to the bottom.

Although we will speak about each of these five in the coming weeks I'd like to briefly speak
about how we might deal with them.
As I mentioned above, the Buddha's primary advice is to be mindful of their presence.
But another way we might look at them when we notice them being present is to apply the
antidote.
The antidote to sensual desire is impermanence. All things in nature are the result of causes
and conditions, are compounded, as a result of two or more things. It is by this same very
nature - that all things change. They appear, are here for a bit, change, and then pass away.
The antidote to ill-will is lovingkindness and compassion. The reason we have ill will is because
we see other people as different than us, as outside of us. We do not see the
interconnectedness of life.
As we reflect on our lives we understand that we are striving to be happy and not to suffer and
that you are not alone. We see that we are no different than others.
For the antidote to Sloth and Torpor or their kindred Apathy and Laziness the answer can be
quite simple - it is to rouse energy. You can jump up and down, open a window and let some air
in, splash water on your face, or have a cup of tea. Another way is to reflect on time passing
and asking yourself, "What are you doing?. This is similar to reflecting on death as we did with
Mindfulness of the Body.
The antitode to anxiousness is developing contentment, calming and relaxation: this can be

done thru conscious breathing techniques or body scanning meditation, or to nurture oneself in
other means, like a hot-tub.
And lastly on doubt. The antidote to doubt is having trust or faith. You might talk to a teacher, do
some research gathering clear instructions or directions, and by nurturing self confidence.
In meditation there are five mental factors that counteract the five hindrances. B. Alan Wallace
identifies five mental factors that counteract the five hindrances, according to the Theravada
tradition:[3]
Coarse examination (vitakka) counteracts sloth-torpor (lethargy and drowsiness)
Precise investigation (vicāra) counteracts doubt (uncertainty)
Well-being (pīti) counteracts ill-will (malice)
Bliss (sukha) counteracts restlessness-worry (excitation and anxiety)
Single-pointed attention (ekaggatā) counteracts sensory desire
These five counteracting factors arise during the first jhāna (stage of concentration).

I can't pass up this opportunity to read an except from "The Life of Shabkar", teachings of an
18th century wandering Tibetian yogi.
Another day, I went for some fresh air to a meadow covered with flowers. . . . While singing and
remaining in a state of awareness of the absolute view, I noticed among the profusion of flowers
spread out before me one particular flower waving gently on its long stem and giving out a
sweet fragrance. As it swayed from side to side, I heard this song in the rustling of its petals. . . .
Listen to me, mountain dweller: . . .
I don’t want to hurt your feelings, But, in fact, you even lack awareness Of impermanence and
death, Let alone any realization of emptiness. For those with such awareness, Outer
phenomena all teach impermanence and death. I, the flower, will now give you, the yogi, A bit of
helpful advice On death and impermanence. A flower born in a meadow, I enjoy perfect
happiness With my brightly colored petals in full bloom. Surrounded by an eager cloud of bees, I
dance gaily, swaying gently with the wind. When a fine rain falls, my petals warp around me;
When the sun shines I open like a smile. Right now I look well enough, But I won’t last long. Not
at all.
Unwelcome frost will dull the vivid colors, Till turning brown I wither. Thinking of this, I am
disturbed. Later still, winds— violent and merciless— will tear me apart until I turn to dust. . . .
You, hermit, . . . Are of the same nature. Surrounded by a host of disciples, You enjoy a fine
complexion, Your body of flesh and blood is full of life. When others praise you, you dance with
joy; . . . Right now, you look well enough. But you won’t last long. Not at all. Unhealthy ageing
will steal away Your healthy vigor; Your hair will whiten
And your back will grow bent. . . . When touched by the merciless hands Of illness and death
You will leave this world For the next life. . . . Since you, mountain-roaming hermit, And I, a
mountain-born flower, Are mountain friends, I have offered you These words of good advice.
Then the flower fell silent and remained still. In reply, I sang: O brilliant, exquisite flower, Your
discourse on impermanence Is wonderful indeed. But what shall the two of us do? Is there
nothing that can be done? . . . The flower replied: . . . Among all the activities of sāṃsara, There
is not one that is lasting. Whatever is born will die;
Whatever is joined will come apart; Whatever is gathered will disperse; Whatever is high will fall.

Having considered this, I resolve not to be attached To these lush meadows, Even now, in the
full glory of my display, Even as my petals unfold in splendor . . . You too, while strong and fit,
Should abandon your clinging. . . . Seek the pure field of freedom, The great serenity.
