- [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity and Compassion]][[Brahmaviharas]]
    - [[Equanimity or equipoise]], upek(k)hā, from an etymological perspective suggests a mental attitude of “looking upon”, not an indifferent “looking away”. The term thus conveys an [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/awareness]] of whatever is happening combined with [[mental balance]] and the [[absence of favoring or opposing]].
    - **[[Compassion to relate to Equanimity]]**
        - works for [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/sympathetic joy]]
            - seems natural
            - [[compassion]] does not mean to commiserate and become sad oneself
        - for some it can seem less straightforward that equanimity somehow relates to compassion
            - difficulty lies with compassion in the form of a bodhisattva's wish to liberate others.
            - [[motivation]] to liberate others appears lacking
            - not easy to see how this would lead to an attitude of equanimity
            - later [[Equanimity reinterpreted]]
                - redefined the brahmavihara of equanimity as the intention to liberate living beings from their defilements
                - seems bougie - better than
            - [[compassion]] for those who are suffering
                - tend to aversion
            - [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/sympathetic joy]] for those who are happy
                - tend to passion
            - [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/metta]] applies to those who are neither
                - tend to ignorance
            - **[[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity]] comes as the wish for each of these types of living beings to be free from the respective defilement**
        - aspiration to be free from aversion, passion and ignorance
    - **Early Discourses**
        - not the case
        - also solution adopted in later times involved shifting [[equanimity as a preliminary exercise]]
        - diminish hatred or desire
        - then used to cultivate the other brahmaviharas
        - make equanimity 1st of the 4
        - [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity]]can be skillful means where one is struggling with a defiled condition of mind
        - need not be the sole mode of engaging in the practice of equanimity
        - [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity]]beneficial to use equanimity for the cultivation of all of the brahmaviharas, embracing them all
            - include equanimity as the culmination point of brahmavihara meditation
            - equanimity does not replace the other brahmaviharas
        - one who cultivates the brahmaviharas embraces them all
        - it does mean that
            - compassion can result in and mature in equanimity
        - [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity]]is not indifference
            - Mental equipose that rounds off a systematic opening of the heart
            - brought about thru cultivation of the other three divine abodes
    - Two Types of [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity]]
        - one to be developedone to be avoided
        - wholesome or unwholesome repercussions
        - equanimity needs to be cultivated in such a way that it enhances and complements the other divine abodes
        - be careful to avoid mere indifference
            - Two of the Buddhas greatest disciples (Ananda and Sariputta) disciplined for remaining unconcerned where compassion was called for.
    - Complementary Relationship Between Equanimity and Compassion
        - when teaching someone engages in the most prominent expression of compassion
        - reaction from the audience to teachings can then take the form of sympathetic joy
        - [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity]]Not always the case
            - teacher may face those who not only lack sympathetic joy but may even be part or wholly inattentive.
            - if this were to occur then the Buddha would dwell in equanimity
    - Transition from Compassion to Equanimity
        - 1
            - refusal of Monk to take only 1 meal per day
                - Buddha compromised with the Monk
                - Monk further declined to follow the rule
                - Buddha equanimous
                - Monk eventually comes to realize the inappropriateness of his behavior
        - 2
            - Buddha approach a group of monks quarreling
                - compassionate intervention
                - they did not stop
                - Buddha too his robes and bowl and went off on his own
        - attitude shifted from compassion to equanimity
    - [[Equanimity as a Divine Abode]]
        - Shows that equanimity at times can be the **appropriate attitude**
        - When compassion meets with careless or cold response then perhaps the time has come to move on to equanimity
        - Give up attempts to control the situation and change it for the better
        - allows others to take responsibility for their own actions and attitudes
        - Liberating compassion from expectations of results
        - Compassion leading to equanimity does not need to be attached to it bearing fruit and being successful

    - Comments About Compassion and other Divine Abodes
        - Basic thrust of compassion is the wish for others to be free from affliction
        - Means One is often concerned about those who are less well of than oneself
        - Same can be found in teaching situations
        - Potential pitfall of compassion
            - perceived to be in a lower and inferior position
        - Required counter-balancing this to avoid becoming a habit
            - could give rise to conceit and feeling of superiority
        - To protect against this - cultivate other brahmaviharas
        - Metta
            - basic attitude of kindness and well-wishing
            - Treating everyone equally as one's friend
        - Can function as the **appropriate attitude** when one is faced with various forms of aggression
        - Cultivation of Compassion complemented by sympathetic joy
        - Sympathetic Joy ensures that, after what is negative has received due attention, what is positive will equally be taken into account.
        - With Equanimity a further complement comes into play with the other brahmaviharas
        - The orientation continues but there is no need to be active in reaching out at all times - not turned into a task
        - Equanimity rounds off compassion by freeing it from an trace of compulsion and obsession.
        - Equanimity completes the 4 divine abodes
        - The Brahmaviharas themselves offer those of us on the path a number of choices for the appropriate mental attitude for any situation
        - The Brahmaviharas themselves are mentioned frequently in the early discourses and are considered of upmost importance for systematic cultivation of the mind
        - Also a regular form of practice in those early times due to the benefits that they provide
        - So what we find in early Buddhist though is that compassion or metta by way of body, speech, and mind was its foundation
        - It played out in many ways, from kindness in daily life situations to facing the most dramatic forms of aggression.
        - Based on metta, compassion in turn finds it complement in sympathetic joy and equanimity.
        - The practice is actually cumulative.
        - By taking all of the brahmaviharas to heart we enrich our actual practice and avoid the possible pitfalls that could result from taking on compassion without the support of the others.
- [[Roam-Export-1674627649766/Equanimity]] [[Equanimity as a Skill]] 
    - Using equanimity as a skill. 
    - Equanimity as that place of perfect balance where we are not making waves.
        - This is probably what was meant last week when we talked about Ajahn Chah’s emphasis to Westerners to develop both equanimity and patience so that they might be more skilled at the application of Mindfulness. 
        - But there are other ways of thinking of Equanimity.
    - Equanimity has many applications. As one of the divine abodes and is considered:
        - Neither a thought nor an emotion, it is rather the steady conscious realization of reality's transience. 
        - It is the ground for wisdom and freedom and the protector of compassion and love. 
        - Some think of equanimity as dry neutrality or cool aloofness, others mention mature equanimity as something that produces a radiance and warmth of being. 
        - The Buddha described a mind filled with equanimity as "abundant, exalted, immeasurable, without hostility and without ill-will."
- —
    - Equanimity is useful and can also be cultivated through meditation
    - As we know Meditation is also a contemplative practice.  It has been shown to associate with greater development of equanimity, allowing people to face all extreme states of mind or whatever arises at the present moment. During the meditative state, meditators can practice the technique called "single-pointed concentration" where the mind pays attention to one thought or emotion in the present moment and notices how the feeling is arising. This leads to awareness of the moment. With time and practice, it trains the mind to go from "ordinary conceptual modes of operation to greater stillness and equanimity.” In Vipassana meditation, practitioners can come to understand and see clearly into the nature of reality, the impermanence of all experience. From this newly developed perspective of equanimity, the mind becomes less easily disturbed and suffered from unexpected conditions and emotional states. Meditation, indeed, can help to train the mind to be sensitive and flexible which results in developing and maintaining a state of composure, peace, and balance in all sorts of experiences.
    - For the most part, when we talk about the practice of meditation we are coming from a position of knowing - just this bare knowing of what our experience is.  This is one way to practice and the one I will emphasize for the most part.  This place of mental calmness and composure.
    - However, there are many, many aspects where practice is a skill - a training - and the two intermingle.  A lot of the practice is knowing when and where to place effort and when to ride it out.  Reminding us of the ways to cross the floods - not standing still nor moving forward.
    - There is a simile where the practice of mindfulness and meditation can be equated to a herdsperson watching over a herd of cows. If the cattle are in danger of getting into trouble then you have to make the effort to corral them in.  And when the cows are not in danger then relaxing our attention.
    - This is the same with our practice.  When we are experiencing any of the hindrances or any of the other difficulties that we discussed a little ways back, then it behoves us :) to corral ourselves in, to take appropriate action, and, if not experiencing any suffering at that moment, then we can rest in this base of knowing our experience as it arises and passes away.
    - Now when we speak of equanimity, unlike the other Bramavihara’s - this is not something that you can practice on its own. Equanimity is always working in conjunction with other good qualities of mind.
    - It’s interesting that the four sublime attitudes, the seven factors of awakening, and the 10 perfections all end up with equanimity - but that’s not really the case - they are to be developed to take us beyond equanimity. And what is beyond equanimity? It is the unconditioned - true happiness - ultimate freedom from anything created.  And in order to go beyond - we have to develop equanimity with the other factors in these lists.
    - When we keep in mind the principle of karma - we know that there are certain things that you cannot change based on past actions. Like when we have hurt someone - we have hurt them -  and we cannot undo that.  Fortunately not everything in the present moment is determined by the past. We are making choices in the present also.
    - There is a story of the Buddha who had a discussion with a rival sect that existed at the time of the Buddha. This particular group of Jains believed that everything in the present was shaped by the past.  So one of the practices they endured was self-torture - thinking that by doing this they would burn off negative effects of their past karma.
    - The Buddha asked them, “ Have you ever noticed that this pain you feel during your torture ends when you stop doing torture?”.
    - This is a clear sign that pain does not only come from the past but from what you are doing right now. Our choices can make a difference. And what we are doing right now is not something we can be equanimous about. We have to be equanimous about the fact that there is a pattern for cause and effect. __“When we do this, that happens.”__ Certain results will come. And it is these results that we have to accept.
    - But we have choices of the kind of things we want to do, and we base the results we are looking for from those past actions. For instance, we realize that the results of our drinking brought about not so wonderful results at a certain point.  But now, as a result of those past actions and our choice at this moment we are here in a Marin Mindful Recovery Group meeting looking for ways to go about living our lives more skillfully - leaning towards freedom.
    - In our meditation, in the here and now of the moment -  you are making a choice.  Maybe you make the choice to focus on the breath for the whole duration of the meditation. If there is some pain that you are experiencing that comes from past actions, past injuries - you work around them. You find areas in the body that are not in pain and you turn your awareness there.  This is “resourcing”. You can think of good breath energies nourishing those parts of the body. Maybe even strengthening those parts.
    - And when you get a solid footing - then spreading from there to go thru the area where the pain exists  -- or at the very least relaxing any tension around the pain.  You have a choice of where you are going to focus your attention right now and how you react to what you find, in the here and now. This is the creative aspect of practice.
    - So when we think of equanimity in terms of karma, we do not just rest there where we are.  We will want to focus our attention on what we can do.
    - Thanissaro Bhikkhu tells a story of him being in Paris to give a talk.
    - “...one evening toward the end of his stay, he was standing on the sidewalk in front of the hotel, waiting for a ride. When he suddenly realized that this was his first time in the country without a translator around and wondered what he would I do if someone came up and asked him a question? Well, sure enough, there was a telephone lineman working across the street. He saw him and came across the street, saying, that he just the person he wanted to see. That he had a miserable job, was surrounded by dishonest people,” he said. “How can I find happiness in life? How can I find peace in life?” So Thanissaro Bhikkhu talked to him about generosity, virtue, and meditation. When the guy asked him how to meditate he gave him the address for his website. The guy seemed pleased, shook my hand, and went back to work.
    - The irony was that night he was going to give a talk at a vipassana center on the topic of how the present moment is not the goal, that we’re not here simply to accept what’s happening in the present moment and to try to be happy with that. So when he told them the story of the lineman he said that if he had told him, “Well, to be happy, just learn how to accept, be equanimous about your miserable job and your dishonest friends,” he would have had the good sense to walk away.
    - The Buddha never told us to just sit there and accept things. We accept the way things are but then we train our minds to use the principle of cause and effect to create a path. Equanimity is part of that path, but it’s not the whole path—and it’s not the goal.
    - Buddhism is not about just sitting back and accepting.  There may be some times for this - like when we do not know what course of action to take.  Equanimity has a role in developing a skill and in the battles we face. If you’re searching for something, you look and look and look and at some point - when you don’t find it where you think it should be, you accept that fact. And then you go and look for it someplace else. If you’re in a battle and there are setbacks, you accept the fact that there are setbacks, but you don’t let them defeat you. You work your way around them.
    - When you’re developing a skill, you use equanimity to look at the results of what you’re doing, to watch your actions, to look at the results, and if the results aren’t satisfactory, you accept that fact and then go back and change your actions to be better.
    - Equanimity is selective. For instance, right now: Things outside, you put those aside. Issues in your home, people for whom you’re responsible, you just put them aside for the time being. Focus on your own mind. But remind yourself that you’re doing this in a way that doesn’t benefit only you.
    - You’re getting your mind under control, and you can develop qualities of discernment, mindfulness, and alertness to learn how to put aside your greed, aversion, and delusion. When you can do that, you benefit and the people around you will benefit, too. This is a part of your motivation for being here: that it’s going to be better for the people around you. Your equanimity for those people at the moment is what allows you to develop the skill you need right now. So that’s one function of equanimity: to put anything that’s not related to what you’re doing right now out of your mind. It’s not your business right now.
    - Then, of course, the second function is to look at what you’re doing. Are you getting the results you want? If you’re not, you can ask yourself, “What could I change? The spot I focus on? The way I breathe? The way I conceive of the breath? Should I learn how to think of the breath as energy?” Learn to see your sense of the body as you feel it from within as all energy. It’s all breath. As you breathe in, there are no hard spots or solid spots that you have to push the breath through. You’re simply allowing more energy to come suffusing into your energy field. Good energy comes in; bad energy goes out.
    - There are lots of ways you can change the way you relate to the breath in the present moment, the way you understand the breath in the present moment, what you do with the breath in the present moment. Equanimity is there to judge the results, to look fairly and objectively at what’s going on so that you can be more effective in making changes.
    - So with equanimity, we accept the principle of karma -  that actions have results - in line with the quality of the intention. That means you have to learn the pattern of which intentions work well and which ones don’t. But then it leaves open the possibility that you can master that pattern and use it for the sake of what you want, true happiness, because that’s what the goal of the practice is.
    - We’re not here to arrive at equanimity. We’re here to arrive at the ultimate happiness. Equanimity, as part of the path, helps get you there. But it’s not the whole path, and it’s not the essence of where we want to go. Always keep these points in mind so that you can develop the skillful kind of equanimity that helps you. Stay away from the unskillful kinds: the kinds that are lazy or defeatist or, what has been called, the small-hearted equanimity (SEUDO)  that just gets depressed and gives up. Those are not the equanimities that the Buddha was teaching. He was teaching large-hearted equanimity that has space for the effort that needs to be put into the practice. And happiness is going to result, both along the way and when you arrive at the goal.
