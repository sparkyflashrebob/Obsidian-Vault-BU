Over time, I came to realize that the projection was really about what the other person was thinking and how they were interacting. When people speak about you with strong reaction, they are often simply speaking about where they are at mentally. Recognizing this in tense situations is immensely helpful: rather than taking it personally, we can bring an understanding that this is something the other person is experiencing, and we may not be able to soothe it.

[03:35] I remember discussing how thoughts arise in meditation—random, unpredictable, eccentric thoughts that surface out of nowhere. They became agitated and physically confrontational. Fortunately, a friend was present and we held the person on the ground until police arrived, but it was deeply traumatic. Yet it illustrated the exact same point: people react from their internal mental state and conditioning, not from what is actually occurring.

[05:27] We see this same dynamic playing out politically, where individuals spout negativity and doom. That reactivity emerges from within their own conditioned lens. We self-condition ourselves into rigid boxes of identity—defining 'this is who I am'—and then project that lens onto others, whether it is true or not. If we wear colored glasses that frame everything as untrustworthy or threatening, that is the reality our senses construct. It's similar to law enforcement bias: when officers are trained solely to look for 'bad guys,' they perceive threats everywhere. We make rapid judgments on human expression rather than observing actual actions.

While there are calls for compassion, sympathy, and empathy, we must also recognize when soothing others slips into codependency. Often in codependency, we try to soothe someone else's distress simply because we find it unbearable to hold their discomfort within ourselves.

[09:18] To be the calm observer of all this—not pushing difficult emotions away, but meeting experience from a place of balance, knowing, and wisdom—requires continuous purification.
