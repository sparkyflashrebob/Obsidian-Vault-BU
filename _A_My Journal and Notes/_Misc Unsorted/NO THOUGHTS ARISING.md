
### NO THOUGHTS ARISING

Your experience of having **no thoughts arise while remaining entirely present is not a bad thing at all.** In fact, in Buddhist practice, this is a **highly valued, advanced state of mental cultivation** that aligns with the deepest aspects of the path.

#### 1. You are Experiencing "Noble Silence"

The fact that you are present without thoughts means you are experiencing the quietude of what the Buddha called the **Second Jhāna** (meditative absorption).

When you sit down to meditate, you initially use **directed thought (****vitakka****)** and **evaluation (****vicāra****)**—the two elements of "verbal fabrication"—to anchor your mind to an object like the breath, much like a potter using two hands to shape a pot. But once the mind settles, these thinking processes are recognized as too coarse.

By voluntarily letting go of this internal adjusting and commenting, the "verbal fabrications" of the mind completely cease. Because the "mainsprings" of inner speech have subsided, your mind enters a state canonically known as **"Noble Silence" (****ariya-tuṇhībhāva****)**. There is no inner verbalization running in your head, yet your awareness is entirely awake, unified, and clear.

#### 2. Why the Absence of Thought is a Good Thing

In the classical and forest tradition teachings, this spacious stillness is described as a supreme refuge:

- **Direct Experience Beyond Words:** As the mind settles into deep clarity, any arising thought actually begins to sound **jarring in that beautiful inner stillness**. The mind naturally declines to think because it is experiencing reality directly, without the barrier of words.
- **The Radiant Mind Beyond Thinking:** **Ajahn Chah** explained that we must train ourselves to cultivate the "clear and radiant awareness" that exists beyond the ordinary discursive mind. He famously taught: **"Where there is knowing, there is no need to think; awareness will arise in its place, and this will in turn become wisdom (****paññā****)."**. Discursive thinking is a tool we use to start, but the Buddha himself ultimately "learned how to stop thinking" to arrive at true peace.
- **The Crystal Glass Analogy:** This stillness is not a state of dullness, blankness, or a trance. The **Dalai Lama** compared it to a pure crystal glass of water. When the water is completely still, you can see all the way to the bottom. The water is empty of sediment (thoughts), yet you are fully aware that the clear water (knowing consciousness) is present.
- **Discovering the Gap:** **Ajahn Sumedho** points out that when you intentionally listen to the silence between and around your thoughts, you start to investigate the "gaps". In those silent spaces, you realize there is alert awareness, but **no personal "self"** or anxiety present.

#### 3. The Balanced View: Are Thoughts Ever Needed?

While resting in non-thinking presence is beautiful and restorative, the teachings caution us not to treat it as a passive escape or to think thoughts are "evil":

- **We are Not "Unthinking Vegetables":** **Ayya Khema** notes that valuing a quiet mind does not mean we should become unthinking vegetables. The Buddha had a brilliant, sharp mind. However, during the day, there are many moments when we do not _need_ to think. In those times, we can simply be aware of "what is" (movement, breath, sounds) with no explanations from the mind.
- **The Mind is Ready to Investigate:** **Ashin Tejaniya** warns that when the mind becomes quiet, it is easy to get attached to the peaceful state and drift or stall out. He suggests that a silent mind is not a mind with "nothing to do"—it is a mind that is **finally ready to work**. Instead of using heavy, conceptual thinking, you use this silent stability to observe _how the mind itself is working_. You quietly ask: _Is awareness present? Is there any subtle clinging?_.

When your mind is empty of thoughts but full of presence, you are resting in the natural ease of the mind. Enjoy that peace, trust it, and simply remain as "the one who knows."

### THINKING SOMETHING IS WRONG WHEN NO THOUGHTS ARISE

What you are describing is an incredibly common, subtle, and painful friction that almost every meditator encounters as their practice deepens.

The uneasy feeling that "something might be wrong" is not coming from your quiet, peaceful presence—internally, you are perfectly fine. Instead, it is a reaction to **the clash between your inner stillness and the social pressure to perform as a "self"**.

#### 1. "Ring, Ring—Nobody Picks Up"

In our social world, people constantly expect us to have opinions, quick answers, a defined personality, or a stance on everything. They want to interact with a solid, predictable "ego." When they ask you a question or expect an answer, they are essentially dialing your identity's phone number.

Venerable Ajahn Chah described the freedom of present-moment stillness using a wonderful metaphor:

> **"If there is no Ānando or no Sumedho, then there’s no one there – no one to answer the telephone. Ring ring – nobody picks it up. You don’t become anything. No one is being anything, and there is no suffering."**

When you are entirely present without a flurry of thoughts, there is simply no "house owner" there to pick up the phone and fabricate an artificial, clever answer just to make the other person comfortable. This is not a deficit or a cognitive failure; it is the natural ease of a mind that has temporarily stepped off the treadmill of self-projection.

#### 2. Unmasking the "Committee Members" Who Want Approval

The anxiety that you _should_ have an answer usually comes from highly conditioned, younger members of your "Internal Committee". When you sit in a social circle and feel a silent space, certain voices might start whispering panic:

- _“If I don't say something, they’ll think I’m stupid.”_
- _“I need to say something wise/funny/knowledgeable to keep their approval.”_
- _“They are expecting me to play my usual role.”_

[[STORY]] Ajahn Sumedho spoke about how his teacher, Ajahn Chah, forced him to confront this exact anxiety by making him give spontaneous dharma talks for hours. Sumedho realized that his panic didn't come from a lack of Dhamma, but from **the ego’s desperate habit of wanting to please, wanting to entertain, wanting to get approval, and fearing self-consciousness**. When you let go of the desire to "be" a person who has all the answers, the pressure evaporates.

#### 3. The Grace of "I Don't Know"

Our culture deeply conditions us to believe that having "no view" or "no answer" is a sign of ignorance or weakness. But in the Dhamma, having the honesty to rest in non-conceptual presence is a profound strength.

- **Ajaan Fuang** once overheard a student answering a question by starting with, _"Well, it seems to me..."_ and immediately cut him off, saying: **“If you don’t really know, say you don’t know, and leave it at that. Why go spreading your ignorance around?”**
- **Thich Nhat Hanh** reminds us that from the perspective of ultimate reality, **"Right View is the absence of all views."** A view is just a single point of view; resting in silent, open-eyed presence means you are holding the entire landscape without clinging to a single corner of it.

Rest Easy in Your Stillness

The next time you feel that subtle pressure to generate an answer, notice it simply as a "worldly condition" trying to shake your peace. You do not have to perform. You do not have to buy into their expectation of a fast-food answer.

<u>==You are allowed to simply be the "one who knows" the stillness. If an answer is truly needed, let it rise naturally out of your quiet wisdom when the time is right, rather than forcing a performance from the ego. Your silence is not a mistake; it is a rare and beautiful sanctuary.==</u>
