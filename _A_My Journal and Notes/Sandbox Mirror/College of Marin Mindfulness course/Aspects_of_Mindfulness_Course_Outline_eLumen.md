### Course Title
**Aspects of Mindfulness**

### Catalog Course Description (2–5 sentences, present tense)
This course explores the foundational principles, cognitive science, and lived practices of mindfulness and self-compassion for personal and academic well-being. Students investigate the nature of the mind, examining how attentional drift, digital distraction, and habitual stress patterns impact daily focus and emotional vitality. Through contemplative inquiry and evidence-based training, students strengthen attentional stability, executive cognitive control, and the "two wings of practice"—clear seeing and an open, caring heart. The curriculum emphasizes the practical integration of somatic awareness, cognitive discernment, and single-tasking persistence into academic routines, workplace settings, and interpersonal relationships.

In an era characterized by continuous digital connectivity, fractured attention spans, and rising academic and economic stress, community college students face substantial cognitive fragmentation and emotional strain that directly undermine academic persistence, retention, and deep learning. *Aspects of Mindfulness* offers students rigorous, evidence-based practices for attentional training, executive cognitive control, somatic self-regulation, and emotional resilience. Rooted in both classical contemplative inquiry and contemporary cognitive neuroscience, the course fulfills College of Marin’s General Education Area E (Lifelong Learning and Self-Development) by equipping students with lifelong tools to sustain focus on tasks, manage stress, nurture interpersonal connection, and redirect vital energy toward healthy, productive living.

## Tab 5: Specifications

### Primary Method of Instruction
* **Methods:** Lecture, Experiential Contemplative Labs, Attentional Training Exercises, Socratic Discussion, Guided In-Class Inquiry, Collaborative Small-Group Reflection, Multimedia Case Analysis.
* **Instructional Rationale:** Instruction balances conceptual exploration (cognitive neuroscience, attention networks, evolutionary biology, and stress physiology) with direct experiential practice (focused-attention anchoring, open monitoring, somatic awareness, mindful listening, and single-tasking laboratory sessions). Students are invited to observe their attentional habits and lived experience directly rather than accepting theoretical propositions passively.

### Representative Out-of-Class Assignments
1. **Attention & Single-Tasking Field Lab (Weekly Attentional Audit):**  
   Students track their attentional patterns across specific academic or workplace tasks (e.g., reading a textbook chapter, studying for an exam, or completing a technical project). Students log instances of attentional drift, digital distraction impulses (such as reaching for a phone or opening browser tabs), and mental fatigue. Students apply specific attention-anchoring techniques (such as returning awareness to physical breath anchors or sensory landmarks), measuring and reflecting upon changes in task persistence, comprehension, and completion time.
2. **Primary Sensation vs. Narrative Proliferation Case Study (The Two Arrows):**  
   Students analyze an everyday personal challenge (e.g., an unexpected bill, a difficult customer service interaction, academic exam anxiety, or physical pain). In a 3–4 page essay, students deconstruct the situation:
   * Identifying the primary event (the raw sensory data, neutral circumstance, or initial physical discomfort).
   * Identifying the secondary narrative (the catastrophic thinking, self-criticism, resistance, or blame added on by the mind).
   * Proposing a grounded action protocol that addresses the actual life circumstance without adding unnecessary internal suffering or depleting vital energy.
3. **Contemplative Practice & Somatic Field Log:**  
   Students engage in 15–20 minutes of daily secular mindfulness practice (e.g., breath focus, body scan, mindful walking, or self-compassion inquiry). Students maintain a structured weekly log recording somatic observations (e.g., noting physical contractions such as belly tightness or shoulder elevation during stressful encounters) and the conditionality of these responses, observing how mindful pauses allow physiological equilibrium to be restored.

### Methods of Evaluation
* **Weekly Reflective Practice & Attentional Logs (25%):** Ongoing evaluation of depth of inquiry, observational clarity, tracking of attentional focus, and practical integration into daily tasks.
* **Analytical Essays & Written Case Studies (25%):** College-level critical papers evaluating cognitive attention models, neurobiological stress responses, and empirical research on contemplative interventions.
* **Midterm Examination / Conceptual Synthesis (20%):** Evaluates understanding of foundational terminology, the biology of attention and the nervous system, cognitive appraisal, and historical models of mindfulness.
* **Interactive Class Discussions & Experiential Peer Dialogues (15%):** Active engagement in seminar reflections, mindful communication dyads, and collective attentional inquiry.
* **Culminating Capstone Project / Personal Mindfulness & Focus Plan (15%):** A synthesized, personalized lifelong framework integrating attention-training routines, somatic self-regulation, and compassion practices into academic, occupational, and interpersonal life.

### Representative Assigned Texts & Materials
*(Complies with COM policy requiring at least one text published within the past five years).*

1. **Jha, Amishi P.** *Peak Mind: Find Your Focus, Own Your Attention, Invest 12 Minutes a Day.* New York: HarperOne, **2021**. *(Key text on attention networks, executive control, working memory, and staying on point amid distraction).*
2. **Brach, Tara.** *Trusting the Gold: Uncovering Your Natural Goodness and Healing the Heart.* Boulder, CO: Sounds True, **2021**. *(Core text on the Two Wings of practice, self-compassion, and emotional resilience).*
3. **Williams, Mark, and Danny Penman.** *Mindfulness: An Eight-Week Plan for Finding Peace in a Frantic World.* New York: Hachette Books, **2021** (10th Anniversary Edition). *(Evidence-based clinical mindfulness curriculum).*
4. **Neff, Kristin, and Christopher Germer.** *The Mindful Self-Compassion Workbook: A Proven Way to Accept Yourself, Build Inner Strength, and Thrive.* New York: The Guilford Press, **2018 / 2021**. *(Standard secular workbook for emotional regulation and resilience).*
5. **Kabat-Zinn, Jon.** *Full Catastrophe Living: Using the Wisdom of Your Body and Mind to Face Stress, Pain, and Illness.* Revised and Updated Edition. New York: Bantam Books, **2013**. *(Foundational text of secular mindfulness in healthcare and education).*

## Tab 6: Learning Outcomes

### Course Objectives (Discrete Knowledge and Skills)
Upon completion of this course, students will be able to:
1. Explain the historical, philosophical, and empirical origins of mindfulness and contemplative science.
2. Differentiate between the three primary neural networks of attention: Focused Attention (the spotlight), Open Monitoring (the floodlight), and Executive Function (the coordinator).
3. Apply attention-anchoring techniques to notice cognitive drift, reduce mental fatigue, and sustain focus on academic and personal tasks amid digital distraction.
4. Differentiate between primary sensory data (the initial event or sensation) and secondary cognitive narratives, judgments, and catastrophizing (the mental fiction added on).
5. Identify autonomic nervous system responses (sympathetic arousal and parasympathetic recovery) and explain how somatic contractions (e.g., abdominal tightness, shallow breathing) represent conditioned biological survival reflexes rather than personal failures.
6. Apply the "Two Wings of Practice"—Mindfulness (clear seeing of facts) and Compassion (an open heart meeting vulnerability with caring action)—to personal, academic, and interpersonal challenges.
7. Contrast mammalian threat recovery in nature with human cognitive stress responses, articulating how mental rumination perpetuates chronic alarm when no immediate physical danger exists ("thinking about the tiger when there is no tiger").
8. Formulate practical strategies to interrupt chronic mental stress habits, liberating physical and psychological energy for grounded, skillful decision-making.
9. Demonstrate techniques of mindful communication and deep listening across diverse cultural, familial, and professional settings.
10. Construct an individualized, sustainable personal practice framework that supports long-term emotional resilience, cognitive focus, physical health, and ethical community engagement.

### Student Learning Outcomes (SLOs)
*(5 broad, observable, assessable outcomes utilizing Bloom's Taxonomy active verbs).*

* **SLO 1 (Attentional Stability & Cognitive Control):**  
  *Demonstrate attentional stability and executive cognitive control by applying focused attention and open monitoring practices to sustain focus, mitigate cognitive distraction, and enhance task persistence in academic, personal, and workplace environments.*
* **SLO 2 (Cognitive Discernment & Narrative Regulation):**  
  *Analyze the distinction between direct sensory experience and secondary cognitive appraisals, evaluating how narrative habits contribute to chronic stress and emotional suffering.*
* **SLO 3 (Somatic Awareness & Biological Regulation):**  
  *Demonstrate experiential competence in recognizing somatic tension and biological threat responses as conditioned natural processes, utilizing mindful self-regulation techniques to restore physiological equilibrium.*
* **SLO 4 (Compassionate Action & Mindful Communication):**  
  *Formulate and apply mindful, compassionate communication and conflict-resolution strategies in daily relationships, honoring diverse cultural perspectives and interpersonal dynamics.*
* **SLO 5 (Personal Regimen & Lifelong Wellness):**  
  *Design and implement an evidence-based personal mindfulness regimen that integrates regular contemplative practices to enhance physical vitality, mental clarity, and task engagement throughout life.*

## Tab 8: Outlines (Course Content)

### Lecture Topic Outline (18-Week Semester Model)

```markdown
1. Foundations and Landscape of Mindfulness
   a. Historical, cultural, and philosophical origins of contemplative practices
   b. Contemporary secular adaptations: Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and clinical applications
   c. Defining mindfulness: observational presence, non-judgmental attention, and intentional awareness
   d. Misconceptions of mindfulness: passive resignation vs. clear, courageous engagement

2. The Architecture of Attention: Focus, Working Memory, and Executive Control
   a. The cognitive neuroscience of attention: Focused Attention (the spotlight), Open Monitoring (the floodlight), and Executive Function (the director)
   b. Neuroplasticity and attention training: strengthening prefrontal cortex networks and down-regulating default mode network (DMN) rumination
   c. The mechanics of attentional drift: understanding mind-wandering as a natural brain function
   d. Developing meta-awareness: noticing when attention has drifted without self-criticism and gently returning to the task

3. Attention, Distraction, and Task Persistence in the Digital Age
   a. The impact of continuous partial attention and digital multitasking on cognitive performance
   b. Dopamine loops, notification friction, and attention fragmentation
   c. Principles of single-tasking: cognitive load theory and the cost of task-switching
   d. Practical protocols for deep work and study sessions: setting intentional anchors, managing environmental cues, and sustaining focus through challenging tasks

4. The Two Wings of Practice: Mindfulness and Compassion
   a. First Wing: Clear Seeing (Wisdom and Discernment) — looking squarely at what is occurring without distortion
   b. Second Wing: The Open Heart (Warmth and Compassion) — meeting vulnerability with kindness and caring action
   c. The imbalance of mindfulness without heart: cold self-evaluation and perfectionism
   d. The imbalance of sentimentality without clarity: emotional overwhelm and lack of discernment
   e. Uniting Head and Heart: balancing cognitive discernment with genuine self-kindness

5. The Biology of Stress and Somatic Conditionality
   a. Neurobiology of the stress response: the autonomic nervous system, amygdala, and HPA axis
   b. Somatic contractions: why the stomach gets tight or knotted up, throat constricts, and breathing shallows
   c. The principle of conditionality: physical reactions as natural biological survival reflexes, not moral or personal failures
   d. Removing self-judgment: understanding the body's evolutionary protective machinery

6. The Nature of the Mind and the Threat Response
   a. Evolutionary biology: how animals process immediate threat versus how humans manufacture continuous threat
   b. The deer in the meadow: running from danger, discharging the physical shock, and returning peacefully to grazing
   c. The human dilemma: "Thinking about the tiger when there is no tiger"
   d. How cognitive rumination and anticipatory anxiety trap the nervous system in perpetual alarm

7. The Two Arrows: Primary Sensation vs. Secondary Mental Narrative
   a. The First Arrow: unavoidable sensory input entering through the senses (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind)
   b. Grounding in everyday realities: unexpected bills, rude remarks, checkout counter delays, physical discomfort
   c. The Second Arrow: what the frantic mind adds on (stories of doom, victimhood, self-blame, resentment)
   d. Mindful observation: arresting the second arrow to prevent unnecessary psychological suffering

8. Direct Sensory Experience vs. Mental Fiction
   a. Deconstructing perception: what is directly heard, seen, and physically felt vs. what is inferred or assumed
   b. Recognizing narrative loops and mental projections
   c. Discerning factual situations from runaway internal commentary
   d. Developing the capacity to rest as "the observer" rather than merging with the thought stream

9. Rewiring the Habit of Stress into Skillful Energy
   a. How stress, agitation, and distraction become chronic neurological habits of mind
   b. Immediate real-time somatic relief vs. long-term habit transformation
   c. Reclaiming depleted vital energy: shifting from energy wasted on friction to energy invested in grounded solutions
   d. The pause as an instrument of freedom: creating space between stimulus and response

10. Working with Difficult Emotions and the Practice of RAIN
    a. Recognize: noticing what is currently present without avoidance
    b. Allow: letting the experience be here without immediately trying to alter or suppress it
    c. Investigate: bringing gentle curiosity to the felt sense in the body (throat, chest, knotted stomach)
    d. Nurture / Non-Identification: offering comforting warmth to oneself and recognizing that "I am not this emotion"

11. Cognitive Biases, Perception, and Mindful Focus
    a. Negativity bias: evolutionary roots and modern psychological implications
    b. Confirmation bias and emotional reasoning: how fear colors perception and impairs task performance
    c. De-centering: holding thoughts lightly rather than treating them as absolute truths
    d. Somatic anchor points: using physical sensation (feet on the ground, the breath, or sound) to anchor scattered attention

12. Mindfulness in Everyday Life and Action
    a. Informal mindfulness: walking, eating, hygiene, and daily routines
    b. Meeting friction at the checkout counter, during commute delays, or with difficult news
    c. Mindful technology usage: establishing digital boundaries and intentional offline periods
    d. Creating mindful micro-pauses throughout the academic and workday to reset focus

13. Mindful Communication and Interpersonal Attunement
    a. Deep listening: hearing without formulating immediate rebuttal or judgment
    b. Speaking from clarity: truthfulness, timeliness, and gentleness
    c. Conflict de-escalation: pausing when the body signals defensive contraction
    d. Cultivating interpersonal warmth and empathy in challenging relationships

14. The Science and Practice of Compassion and Goodwill
    a. The neuroscience of compassion: oxytocin, vagal nerve stimulation, and emotional safety
    b. Evidence-based practices of goodwill: extending well-wishing to self, loved ones, neutral persons, and challenging individuals
    c. Dissolving self-judgment: recognizing common humanity and shared vulnerability
    d. Compassion as active courage rather than passive tolerance

15. Forgiveness, Grief, and Letting Go
    a. The architecture of resentment: how holding grudges poisons our own well-being and drains cognitive energy
    b. Forgiveness as an internal liberation rather than condoning harm
    c. Processing somatic grief and transition with gentle presence
    d. Letting go of rigid expectations and unhelpful identities

16. Ethical Discernment, Mindfulness, and Society
    a. Classical foundations: mindfulness as inseparable from ethics, non-harming, and integrity
    b. Modern "McMindfulness": critiques of corporatized or self-centered mindfulness trends
    c. Social mindfulness: understanding systemic stress, equity, and collective well-being
    d. Community stewardship: extending mindful presence into environmental care and civic life

17. Cognitive Endurance and Integration into Academic/Professional Work
    a. Building mental stamina: pacing, intentional breaks, and preventing burnout
    b. Mindful problem-solving: maintaining focus through complex intellectual and creative tasks
    c. Integrating attentional anchors into study sessions, writing, and analytical work

18. Designing a Sustainable Personal Life Practice
    a. Identifying personal obstacles: restlessness, sluggishness, doubt, and perfectionism
    b. Overcoming self-criticism: beginning again with gentle consistency
    c. Establishing home practice environments, daily rhythms, and accountability structures
    d. Developing a lifelong personal mindfulness, attention, and self-compassion action blueprint
```
