### Buddhist Teachings on the Root Causes of Human Conflict

- Greed, hatred, and delusion are identified as the three core "tea poisons" within the human mind that drive aggressive, selfish, and destructive behavior.

- Insatiable desire for sensual pleasures, wealth, and power is described as a primary driver of conflict, referencing the Buddha's teaching that even a rain of gold coins would not satisfy human desire.

- Competition arises when individuals acquire things and label them as "mine," creating a possessive mindset that fuels conflict over a limited pool of resources.

- Attachment to views and ideologies is presented as a second major cause of conflict, where people become dogmatic and adopt an "I'm right, you're wrong" stance toward those who think differently.

- Such ideological rigidity is noted to lead to argument, rationalism, and even religiously inspired violence, where individuals feel a sacred right to punish those who oppose their beliefs.

- At the deepest level, conflict is rooted in the delusion of a separate self, the notions of "I, me, and mine," which leads to a mental process of objectification that divides the world into "us" and "them."

- Through this objectifying lens, other people are viewed either as sources to fill one's desires or as obstacles to happiness, fostering alienation and discrimination.

### Buddhist Psychological Approaches to Resolving Internal Conflict

- Fighting is described as ultimately stemming not from external circumstances or bad people, but from an internal failure to understand suffering.

- Resolving conflict requires retraining the mind, abandoning unawakened habits, and cultivating wisdom, rather than merely suppressing negative emotions, which only drives them deeper into the subconscious.

- Applying antidotes through thought substitution is presented as a first practical strategy, based on the principle that the mind cannot hold two contradictory thoughts simultaneously.

- Greed is to be countered with detachment and generosity, sensual lust with contemplation of impermanence, and hatred and anger with the cultivation of loving kindness and compassion.

- Mindful acceptance and non-violence toward oneself is offered as a second solution, where negative emotions are treated as organic parts of the mind that can be composted rather than fought or repressed.

- Anger, for example, is to be embraced with mindfulness using an internal acknowledgment such as "I see anger, dear one, I am here for you," so that the emotion is observed without identification, causing it to lose its energy to survive.

- Deconstruction of the illusory self is presented as the ultimate solution, achieved by breaking experience into parts such as body, feelings, thoughts, and agendas, and repeatedly reflecting that "this is not mine, this is not myself."

- When one deeply understands that there is no solid self to protect or defend, the suffering and happiness of others are recognized as inseparable from one's own, causing anger and the desire to punish to naturally vanish.
