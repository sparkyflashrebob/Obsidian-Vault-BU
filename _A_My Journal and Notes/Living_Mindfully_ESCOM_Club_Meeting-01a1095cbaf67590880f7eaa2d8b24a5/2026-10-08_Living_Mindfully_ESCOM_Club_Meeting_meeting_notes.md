### Reading on the Practice of Non-Striving from Wherever You Go There You Are

- Non-striving, as described by John Kabat-Zinn, is the practice of allowing oneself to simply be in the present moment without attempting to fix, adjust, manage, or improve one's experience.

- Rather than manufacturing a preferred emotional state, the practice involves befriending whatever feeling is currently present, whether that is tiredness, anxiety, or discomfort.

- Stopping the effort to produce a better feeling is described as lifting an enormous burden, making non-striving a deeply liberating act.

- Older adults in particular are noted to face internal pressure to remain productive, which can conflict with the restful, accepting quality that mindfulness practice encourages.

- The instinctive nature of the senses, constantly reaching out to grasp and land on stimuli, is identified as a root cause of the compulsion toward productivity and mental busyness.

- Non-striving also serves to prevent mindfulness itself from becoming another task to succeed at or check off, which would undermine its core purpose.

### The Relationship Between Striving, Distraction, and Deepening Meditation Practice

- Striving is personally experienced as a source of stress, and its reduction is seen as a marker of deepening meditative practice.

- At the first level of deep meditative absorption, sound is identified as the primary distraction, as the ears instinctively reach out and grasp auditory stimuli in the same way the mind grasps thoughts.

- Distractions become increasingly subtle as practice deepens, meaning that virtually any sensory or mental phenomenon can pull attention away from stillness.

- Practicing doing nothing and observing the mind's urge to engage with something is described as a valuable standalone exercise that can be done outside of formal meditation.

- External stimuli such as people passing by a window illustrate how effortlessly attention is pulled away, reinforcing the relevance of non-striving in everyday moments.

### Mindfulness as a Biofeedback Mechanism

- A separate mindfulness group focused on nature walks approaches the practice without allegiance to any specific tradition such as Theravada or Zen.

- That group's facilitator frames mindfulness meditation as a biofeedback mechanism, meaning that awareness of one's experience feeds information back to the body, which in turn feeds back to the observer.

- This framing is seen as a valuable complement to more traditional approaches, as it centers the practice on being the observer of one's own awareness.

- Physical sensations such as tension above the eye are noted as examples of the subtle bodily phenomena that become more perceptible during meditation.

- Paying deliberate attention to a sensation can cause it to intensify and move further into focus, which is an important dynamic for practitioners to understand.

- Whatever one directs attention toward becomes amplified in experience, which is beneficial when the object of focus is constructive but can be challenging when the experience is unwanted.
