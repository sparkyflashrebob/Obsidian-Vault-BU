# Talking to Yourself: Transforming Our Internal Commentary

![[Pasted image 20260812130732.png]]

Recently, I came across an Abhayagiri reflection by Thanissaro Bhikkhu titled _“Talking to Yourself”_. The core theme he presents is simple yet profound: **we largely suffer because of the way we talk to ourselves.**

His contention is that we do not suffer directly from what we experience through our senses, but rather from the ongoing commentary we create about those experiences. This is something we can directly observe in our daily lives.

## Observing the Reactive Mind

I notice my eyes constantly moving toward things that are attractive or unattractive, while largely ignoring everything in between. I am not always sure how much control I have over that initial impulse. However, as part of my mindfulness practice, I try to pay attention to this exact process. If I do not, this is precisely where I get caught in reactivity.

There is nothing inherently wrong with being drawn to things we like. Much of it is instinctual, and are not necessarily good or bad. Yet, it makes me reflect on how vulnerable we are to our basic conditioning.

We sometimes see people lose their faculties as they age, allowing their unfiltered personality traits or character defects to come to the surface. This can be deeply painful for loved ones to experience. Even with a dedicated practice, none of us can guarantee what our minds will look like down the road. None of us want to cause harm, but there may come a day when our ability to consciously control out actions is diminished.

Spiritual practice is partially about cultivating inner clarity today so that compassion becomes our default, while also recognizing that emotional hurt often arises when we take things personally.

## The Projection of Judgment

It is easy to find fault in others. Some people take it personally when others forget names or personal details, yet we all have distinct limitations that arise temporarily or appear to permanent. We cannot always help how we are wired, nor should we accept being faulted for natural human limitations.

When I catch myself judging others, I try to reflect inward:

- _Where am I exhibiting this same tendency?_
    
- _How can I use this awareness to grow, rather than placing judgment on someone else’s perceived weakness?_
    

It is so easy to project outward—to criticize the external world while avoiding looking inward. To open ourselves to the world is to invite criticism, but that does not give us license to constantly criticize others. Unless someone’s actions are causing direct harm, why must we constantly judge differing opinions?

As Thanissaro Bhikkhu notes, the heart of practice is learning to talk to ourselves in new ways. Looking inward can be uncomfortable. When we do not like what we find, our ego can eaily move to build defenses. It is far easier to point fingers at others than to examine our own habits and character defects.

## Facing Ourselves with Forgiveness

Examining our own habits requires continuous effort. Crucially, we must forgive ourselves when we stumble or catch ourselves engaging in harsh self-criticism. We need to notice the moods and emotions that arise, hold them with awareness, and keep moving forward.

The reality is that we are not truly tested until we are tested. It is easy to theorize about how we will behave, but quite another to face reality and see what actually arises.

In his essay, Thanissaro Bhikkhu points out that the Dhamma—the truth of how things are—is not “domesticated.” By domesticated, he means an experience that has been conditioned to the point of rigidity, like jumping on command.

Society conditions us into rigid boxes of right and wrong, good and bad. So much of our conflict comes from our fixed views and perspectives—insisting that our personal morals and values are absolute, and that because we believe we are right, others must be wrong.

When we focus on winning arguments or getting our way, we miss the vital questions:

- _How are we relating to and treating one another?_
    
- _Are we caring, forgiving, tolerant, and helpful?_
    
- _Are we fair, honest, and open-minded?_
    

When personal priority centers entirely on “getting our way,” we lose the collective will to address major societal needs that often get ignored—like poverty and hunger, the affects of war, and disease.

## The Three Defects of a Listener

To change direction, we must look inward and ask: **Do we even listen to ourselves?**

Traditional Buddhist teachings describe three classic “defects of a listener”:

1. **The Inattentive Listener:** Listening with a distracted mind, focused on other thoughts.
    
2. **The Forgetful Listener:** Listening without retaining or integrating what was said.
    
3. **The Poisoned Listener:** Listening with bias or hostility, where there is no genuine desire to hear.
    

This internal process of listening deeply to ourselves is essential if we care about personal growth. Without it, external attitudes like entitlement, selfishness, and indifference naturally take over.

Everything in the practice points toward this inner listening. If we do not purify our internal dialogue, everything we project outward remains contrived and conditioned. We are capable of something higher. It starts by pausing and truly listening.
