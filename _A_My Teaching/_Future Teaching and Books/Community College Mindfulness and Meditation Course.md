## **Course Syllabus: MINDF 101 – Secular Mindfulness & Contemplative Science**

**Course Description:** An interdisciplinary introduction to the theory and practice of mindfulness. Students will explore the physiological effects of meditation on the brain, the psychological foundations of emotional regulation, and the philosophical roots of contemplation. This course is strictly secular, focusing on evidence-based techniques to reduce stress and enhance cognitive clarity.

---

### **I. Learning Objectives**

By the end of this term, students will be able to:

* Define mindfulness and contemplation within a scientific, non-religious framework.
* Explain the "Default Mode Network" (DMN) and how meditation affects neuroplasticity.
* Demonstrate proficiency in at least four distinct contemplative modalities (Breath, Body Scan, Open Monitoring, and Compassion-based).
* Critically evaluate current peer-reviewed research on mindfulness in clinical settings.

---

### **II. Course Modules**

#### **Module 1: Foundations & The Focused Mind**

* **Theory:** The history of secularization (from MBSR to modern psychology).
* **Science:** The stress-response cycle and the Autonomic Nervous System.
* **Practice:** Diaphragmatic breathing and "Single-Point" concentration.
* **Key Concept:** The difference between "ruminating" and "observing."

#### **Module 2: Embodiment & Neurobiology**

* **Theory:** The Mind-Body connection; Interoception vs. Proprioception.
* **Science:** Changes in the Prefrontal Cortex and Amygdala through consistent practice.
* **Practice:** The Progressive Body Scan and Mindful Movement (Yoga/Tai Chi movements for focus).

#### **Module 3: Cognitive Reframing & Emotional Intelligence**

* **Theory:** Meta-awareness (thinking about thinking).
* **Science:** Emotional regulation and the "Gap" between stimulus and response.
* **Practice:** Labeling thoughts and "Open Monitoring" (choiceless awareness).

#### **Module 4: Interpersonal Mindfulness & Ethics**

* **Theory:** Secular compassion (Metta) and prosocial behavior.
* **Science:** The role of Oxytocin and the Vagus Nerve in social connection.
* **Practice:** Active listening exercises and Perspective-Taking meditations.

## PSYC 110: Introduction to Secular Mindfulness and Contemplative Practice
## 1.0 Course Overview
### 1.1 Course Description
In an age of 24/7 connectivity, it is one of the great ironies of our time that we may find it more difficult than ever to actually be in touch with ourselves. This course is a practical and theoretical exploration of secular mindfulness as a remedy to this modern paradox. We will explore tools for cultivating focused attention, emotional balance, and greater self-awareness, grounding our work in both ancient contemplative traditions and contemporary scientific research.
At its core, mindfulness is what Jon Kabat-Zinn, a pioneer in this field, defines as "a particular way of paying attention: on purpose, in the present moment, and non-judgmentally." This course approaches mindfulness as a skill that can be learned, one that stands on its own as a powerful vehicle for self-understanding and healing, not dependent on any belief system or ideology. Accessible to individuals of any background, it is designed to help us navigate the "full catastrophe" of daily life with greater wisdom and compassion. This course aims to equip you with these practical life skills, supported by a deep conceptual understanding of how and why they work.
### 1.2 Learning Objectives
Upon successful completion of this course, you will have developed both the conceptual understanding and the practical skills to integrate mindfulness into your life, enhancing your capacity to navigate academic, professional, and personal challenges.
	• Define and Differentiate: Articulate the core principles of mindfulness, attention, and contemplation, and distinguish between formal meditation (e.g., sitting practice) and informal practice (bringing awareness to daily activities).
	• Apply Core Practices: Demonstrate foundational proficiency in core mindfulness meditation techniques, including the Body Scan, Sitting Meditation (with focus on breath), Mindful Walking, and Loving-Kindness Meditation.
	• Analyze Scientific Foundations: Explain key findings from neuroscience, such as the Harvard study showing that eight weeks of MBSR can physically thicken brain regions associated with emotion regulation and shrink the amygdala, the brain's threat-appraisal center, demonstrating the principle of neuroplasticity.
	• Evaluate Personal Experience: Utilize a practice journal and reflection to critically evaluate personal patterns of thought, emotion, and behavior (such as autopilot, the "Doing" mode, and emotional reactivity).
	• Develop Coping Strategies: Formulate and apply mindful strategies for responding skillfully to stress, difficult emotions, and physical pain by distinguishing between primary suffering (the unavoidable pain of life) and secondary suffering (the mental and emotional reaction we add to it).
	• Integrate into Daily Life: Identify specific opportunities to apply mindfulness in everyday situations, such as communication, eating, and academic or professional work, to enhance well-being and effectiveness.

The following course materials will support you on this learning journey.

## 3.0 Assignments and Grading
The course evaluation is designed to measure both your conceptual understanding of mindfulness principles and your personal engagement with the practices. The grading of your practice is based on your consistent effort and thoughtful reflection, not on achieving a particular internal state or having a "good" meditation. The aim is to cultivate curiosity about your own experience, whatever it may be.
### 3.1 Grading Breakdown
> • Weekly Practice Journal (30%): You are required to maintain a weekly journal to document and reflect upon your mindfulness practice. Each week, you will log your formal meditation practice (e.g., Body Scan, 45 minutes) and at least three instances of informal practice (e.g., mindful dishwashing). You will also write a brief reflection (200-300 words) on your experience, noting any challenges, insights, or observations. This journal is a tool for making your progress visible and for identifying personal patterns that might otherwise go unnoticed. In your reflections, you are encouraged to connect your experience to course concepts, such as the "Five Hindrances" or the "Doing vs. Being" modes of mind.
> • Reflection Papers (30%): There will be two short papers (2-3 pages each). The first will analyze a personal experience of working with a difficult emotion or physical sensation using mindfulness, connecting your direct experience to concepts from our readings on stress and suffering. This is an opportunity to approach your experience with curiosity and kindness, supported by the practices we are learning together. The second paper will analyze the practice of Loving-Kindness meditation and its potential application in strengthening compassion and navigating interpersonal relationships.
> • Final Integration Project (30%): For your final project, you will propose a detailed plan to integrate mindfulness into a specific area of your life or future career (e.g., managing academic stress, improving focus as an athlete, practicing mindful communication as a healthcare provider). The project must include a clear rationale grounded in course readings and scientific findings, and a proposed schedule of formal and informal practices to support your goal.
> • Class Participation (10%): Participation includes your active and respectful engagement in class discussions, small-group activities, and the inquiry process that follows our in-class experiential exercises. Mindful listening to your peers is a key component of participation.
> This grading structure supports the weekly schedule, which will lay out our path for the semester.
> 4.0 Course Schedule: An Eight-Week Journey into Mindfulness
> 
> This course is structured as an eight-module journey, mirroring the empirically validated format of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR). Each week will introduce a core theme, a new formal practice, and informal exercises designed to help you weave awareness into the fabric of your daily life.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
## Module 1: Waking Up from Autopilot
• Topics: Introduce the concept of "autopilot" versus mindful presence. Discuss the costs of a distracted life and the rationale for training attention. Define mindfulness and its secular, evidence-based approach.
• In-Class Practice: Guided "raisin exercise" to demonstrate mindful eating and sensory awareness. Introduction to the Body Scan meditation as the foundational formal practice.
• Home Practice: Practice the 45-minute Body Scan daily. Choose one routine daily activity (e.g., brushing teeth, drinking coffee) to perform with full, non-judgmental awareness.
• Reading: Full Catastrophe Living, Part I.
## Module 2: The Body as a Place to Be
• Topics: After learning to recognize the state of 'autopilot,' we now turn our attention inward, exploring the body as a primary source of present-moment information. We will discuss how the body communicates stress and emotion through sensation and introduce the breath as a reliable "anchor" for our awareness.
• In-Class Practice: Introduction to formal Sitting Meditation, focusing on the sensations of the breath.
• Home Practice: Alternate between the Body Scan and a 20-minute Sitting Meditation daily. Practice a 3-minute "Breathing Space" meditation during transitions in the day.
• Reading: Fully Present, Chapters 1-3.
## Module 3: Mindful Movement
• Topics: Building on our practice of anchoring in the body and breath, we now explore the difference between the mind's "Doing" mode (goal-oriented, problem-solving) and "Being" mode (allowing, accepting). We will explore how gentle, mindful movement can cultivate the Being mode and release physical tension.
• In-Class Practice: Guided Mindful Walking and introductory mindful yoga stretches suitable for a classroom setting.
• Home Practice: Alternate daily between Sitting Meditation and a 45-minute Mindful Yoga or Mindful Walking practice. Informally, practice mindful awareness while walking between classes.
• Reading: Full Catastrophe Living, section on Mindful Yoga.
## Module 4: Responding to Stress
• Topics: Now that we have a foundation in mindful presence, we will examine the psychobiology of the stress response. A gazelle escaping a lion returns to quiet grazing once the threat passes; humans, by contrast, can replay threats in their minds and "drive ourselves crazy in the process." We will differentiate between reacting to stress and responding mindfully, and introduce the concepts of primary suffering (unavoidable pain) and secondary suffering (the reaction we add).
• In-Class Practice: "Sitting with Difficulty," where attention is brought to a region of physical or emotional discomfort with attitudes of curiosity and kindness.
• Home Practice: Daily 30-minute Sitting Meditation. Use the "Breathing Space" practice when you notice a stress reaction during your day.
• Reading: You Are Not Your Pain, Chapters 1-3.
## Module 5: Befriending Thoughts and Emotions
• Topics: Having explored our response to external stressors, we now turn to the internal landscape of the mind. We will investigate the nature of thoughts as "events in the mind" rather than facts, and discuss how mindfulness changes our relationship to thoughts and emotions, not eliminate them. We introduce the "Five Hindrances" (e.g., restlessness, doubt) as normal and workable parts of practice.
• In-Class Practice: "Thoughts as Clouds" meditation, observing the arising and passing of thoughts without judgment or engagement.
• Home Practice: Continue daily Sitting Meditation, with an emphasis on gently labeling thoughts ("thinking") and returning awareness to the anchor of the breath.
• Reading: The Mindfulness and Acceptance Workbook for Anxiety, Chapters 4-5.
## Module 6: Cultivating a Compassionate Mind
• Topics: As we learn to relate to our thoughts and emotions with more awareness, we can introduce the powerful qualities of compassion. We will define compassion and self-compassion as trainable skills, not fixed traits, and differentiate them from the "near enemy" of pity or burnout. We will discuss the healing power of kindness in meeting our own suffering and that of others.
• In-Class Practice: Introduction to the formal Loving-Kindness (Metta) meditation, starting with offering kindness to oneself and a benefactor.
• Home Practice: Practice a 20-minute Loving-Kindness meditation 3-4 times this week, alternating with Sitting Meditation on other days. Informally, offer a silent kind wish to a stranger or someone you encounter during your day.
• Reading: Real Change, section on compassion and equanimity.
## Module 7: Mindful Communication and Connection
• Topics: Cultivating inner compassion naturally extends outward into our relationships. This week we analyze the components of mindful communication: deep listening with full presence and thoughtful, non-reactive speech. We explore how mindfulness can transform difficult conversations and strengthen interpersonal connection.
• In-Class Practice: Paired mindful listening exercise, where one person speaks for 3 minutes without interruption while the other listens with full, non-judgmental attention.
• Home Practice: Daily 20-minute Sitting Meditation. Practice mindful listening in at least one conversation per day, noticing the internal urge to interrupt, judge, or formulate a response while the other person is speaking.
• Reading: Mindfulness at Work For Dummies, section on mindful communication.
## Module 8: Weaving Mindfulness into the Fabric of Life
• Topics: In our final module, we bring together all the threads of our practice to synthesize the course material. We will discuss strategies for establishing and maintaining a consistent practice after the course ends, explore the concept of "awakened awareness" as a path beyond foundational mindfulness, and discuss applying these skills for positive social change.
• In-Class Practice: A 30-minute silent meditation period, allowing you to choose your preferred practice (Sitting, Body Scan, Loving-Kindness). Conclude with a group discussion on intentions for future practice.
• Home Practice: Formulate and begin your personal practice plan as part of your Final Integration Project.
• Reading: Beyond Mindfulness, Foreword and Chapter 1.

## 5.0 Course Policies
### 5.1 A Note on Self-Care and Safety
This course invites you to turn toward your own inner experience with curiosity and kindness. This process can sometimes bring up difficult or unexpected emotions, thoughts, and memories. Please know that this is a normal and often integral part of the process of self-discovery and healing. 
I strongly encourage you to practice self-compassion, allowing yourself to be with what arises without pushing yourself into overwhelming experiences. 
You are always in charge of your practice; please modify any exercise as needed for your physical and psychological well-being.
If you find that difficult material is arising consistently, please make use of campus resources, including the College Counseling Center. 
I am also available during office hours to consult with you about your practice and to help you find appropriate support. 
Our classroom is intended to be a safe, confidential, and supportive space for this shared exploration.
