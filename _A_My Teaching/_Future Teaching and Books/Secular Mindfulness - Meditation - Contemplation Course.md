SYLLABUS: PHIL 110 - Introduction to Secular Mindfulness and Contemplative Practice


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


1.0 Course Information

1.1 Course Title & Number

PHIL 110: Introduction to Secular Mindfulness and Contemplative Practice

1.2 Course Description

This course serves as a rigorous introduction to the theory and practice of secular mindfulness, meditation, and contemplation as disciplined tools for investigating human experience. We will explore foundational techniques not as a means to adopt a belief system, but as a method for direct inquiry into the nature of the mind. The primary aim is to learn how to "awaken to the present moment" and "investigate the process that we can actually witness to." Through guided practices, readings, and reflective journaling, we will focus on developing inner stability and clarity. Students will learn to observe their own patterns of thought, emotion, and perception, thereby gaining a deeper understanding of the nature of dissatisfaction, its origins in habitual reactions, and the potential for liberation from these conditioned patterns.

1.3 Course Learning Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

* Define and differentiate core concepts such as conditioned phenomena (sankhara), awareness (sati), and the constructed self (sakaditthi) using secular, experience-based terminology.
* Apply foundational mindfulness techniques, including mindfulness of breathing and contemplation of the body, to cultivate present-moment awareness and inner stability.
* Analyze personal patterns of thought and emotion by adopting the "witness" or "knower" perspective, distinguishing between direct experience and subsequent mental proliferation.
* Articulate the universal nature of dissatisfaction (dukkha) as a condition to be understood rather than a personal failing, tracing its origins to patterns of craving and aversion.
* Evaluate the practical difference between suppression and "letting go" as a method for allowing transient mental and emotional states to cease naturally.
* Synthesize course principles to deconstruct the "personality view," recognizing the self as a conditioned, impermanent process rather than a solid, unchanging entity.

1.4 Required Texts & Materials

All required readings will be provided as digital excerpts from the talks of contemporary contemplative teacher Ajahn Sumedho. No textbook purchase is necessary. Students are required to maintain a weekly reflection journal, which can be in either a digital or a physical format.

2.0 Course Schedule & Weekly Modules

Module 1: Foundations of Awareness - Beyond the Thinking Mind

This first week establishes the most critical distinction in contemplative practice: the difference between thinking and awareness. Our objective is to move beyond the habit of identifying solely with our cognitive processes and to recognize the silent, receptive quality of consciousness that underlies all experience. This shift is the gateway to the entire course, providing the foundational stability needed for deeper investigation. We are not trying to stop our thoughts, but to recognize "that which is behind thought," because as we will explore, "awareness, heedfulness, being attentive is the deathless itself." A primary obstacle to this direct knowing is the fetter of skeptical doubt (vicikiccha), the mental habit of trying to "figure it all out" intellectually. As Ajahn Sumedho notes, "Doubt always is a result of thinking." The practice, therefore, is to learn how to "get beyond the thinking" and trust in direct, non-conceptual awareness. The initial practice is to simply observe the breath for 15 minutes each day, gently noting when the mind has wandered without judgment and returning to the present. This simple act builds the fundamental muscle of attention, which we will strengthen in the weeks to come.

Module 2: The Body as an Anchor - Grounding in the Present

Having established awareness as our primary tool, this week we turn to the body as our primary anchor. The first foundation of mindfulness involves grounding our attention in physical sensation. In the whirlwind of thoughts, memories, and future plans, the body is a constant, reliable reference point that exists only in the here and now. As Ajahn Sumedho notes, "wherever we are, our body's with you. It's something that you can always refer to." By deliberately bringing attention to posture, the feeling of the breath, and the simple reality of physical presence, we learn to stabilize the mind and interrupt cycles of distraction. The practice this week is to intentionally recognize the reality of the body through its four main postures: sitting, standing, walking, and lying down, using them as opportunities to return to the present moment. This grounding practice prepares us to observe more subtle mental phenomena.

Module 3: The Nature of Experience - Witnessing Phenomena

This week, we shift our focus from grounding awareness to using it as a tool for observation. We will introduce the concept of sankharas—compounded, conditioned things—using secular language. This framework helps us recognize that all our experiences, including thoughts, emotions, memories, and even complex concepts like "England" or "America," are transient phenomena that arise and cease. The core practice is to shift our relationship to these phenomena from being an experiencer (who gets entangled) to being the witness or the knower (who observes with clarity). This shift moves us from personal identification to what Ajahn Sumedho calls "impersonal knowing," clarifying the relationship between the observer and the observed. As he puts it, "The relationship of Buddha to Dhamma, it's BuDdha that knows dhamma, not the personality." We learn to watch the flow of experience without being swept away by it, setting the stage for understanding the universal experience of dissatisfaction.

Module 4: Understanding Dissatisfaction - A Universal Condition

With the ability to witness phenomena, we now turn to one of the most fundamental human experiences: suffering, or dukkha. This will be framed not as a personal failure or a sign that something is wrong with us, but as a "noble truth" to be understood. Suffering, in this context, refers to the entire spectrum of dissatisfaction, from subtle stress and anxiety to profound grief and pain. We will investigate how this dissatisfaction arises from our tendency to identify with changing phenomena like aging, sickness, loss, and unfulfilled desires. As Ajahn Sumedho reflects from his own experience with a serious illness, "when we see it as I'm sick, then there's suffering... the suffering really wasn't the malaria, but the desire to get rid of it." By observing suffering as a universal condition, we depersonalize it and can begin to examine its origins more clearly.

Module 5: The Origin of Stress - The Habits of Craving & Aversion

Building on our understanding of dissatisfaction, this week we investigate its direct cause: the habit of desire, often translated as "craving" or "clinging." We will analyze the three primary forms of desire that generate stress and suffering in our lives. A key insight is recognizing how deeply these patterns are embedded in our minds and reinforced by our environment. Modern society, through mechanisms like advertising, is often "determined to create discontentment in the human mind," constantly telling us we need something more to be happy. Understanding these three distinct drivers of our behavior is a crucial step toward recognizing them in our own experience and loosening their grip.

Type of Desire	Definition	Examples
Sensual Desire (Kama-tanha)	Seeking pleasure and gratification through the five senses and the mind.	Craving a specific food, wanting to hear pleasant music, seeking entertainment, fantasizing about a positive outcome.
Desire for Becoming (Bhava-tanha)	The drive to achieve, to become something, to attain a certain status or state of being.	Wanting to be successful, trying to become enlightened, striving to be seen as a "good meditator," desiring to be a better person.
Desire to Get Rid Of (Vibhava-tanha)	The drive to annihilate, destroy, or escape from unpleasant feelings, people, or situations.	Wanting to get rid of anxiety or anger, avoiding a difficult conversation, pushing away feelings of boredom or frustration.

This systematic analysis of the causes of stress prepares us for the practical application of its cessation: the practice of letting go.

Module 6: The Practice of "Letting Go" - Realizing Cessation

This week, we explore the core contemplative practice of "letting go" as the direct path to the cessation of suffering. It is crucial to understand that this is not an act of suppression, denial, or forcefully "getting rid of" unwanted experiences. That approach is a form of aversion, which only strengthens the pattern of suffering. Instead, letting go is an act of allowing. As Ajahn Sumedho clarifies, "letting go is not getting rid of throwing away. It's leaving things let allowing them to go." By mindfully accepting and observing a difficult emotion or a painful thought without resistance, we allow it to follow its natural course of arising and ceasing. The result of this non-interference is peace. This practice requires trust and patience, turning our understanding of impermanence into a lived experience. This experiential understanding forms the basis for your Midterm Essay.

Module 7: Midterm Reflective Essay

This week is dedicated to integrating the concepts from the first half of the course through a structured reflective essay.

Prompt: Write a 3-page reflective essay analyzing a difficult emotion you have experienced recently (e.g., anger, fear, anxiety, frustration). First, describe the raw experience of the emotion: what were the physical sensations in your body? What thoughts, images, and narratives accompanied it? Second, analyze this experience through the lens of the "witness" practice. Reflect on what happens when you intentionally shift from identifying with the emotion ("I am angry") to simply observing it as a transient, impersonal condition ("There is anger"). What insights arise from this shift in perspective regarding the nature of the emotion and the nature of the self?

Module 8: Deconstructing the Self - Personality as a Created Habit

In the second half of the course, we use our established mindfulness skills to investigate the very structure of the self. We begin with the concept of the personality view (Sakaditthi), which is the deeply ingrained assumption that we are a solid, continuous, independent entity. From a contemplative perspective, the personality is not an essential self but "a habit one acquires" based on memory, cultural conditioning, past experiences, and social roles. As Ajahn Sumedho notes, "My personality is merely of what it is. It's a habit... this personality is not where it's at." The practice this week is to observe the arising and ceasing of the "personality" in daily interactions—the moods, opinions, and roles we adopt—without solidifying it into a fixed identity. Recognizing the constructed nature of personality is a key step toward greater freedom and flexibility.

Module 9: The Nature of Consciousness - An Unspoken Awareness

Having investigated the constructed nature of personality, we now turn our attention to that which is aware of it: consciousness itself. This is not the consciousness of specific thoughts or feelings, but the background awareness in which all experiences appear. In the texts, this foundational consciousness is described as "natural," "impersonal," "uncreated," and having "no boundary." To access this directly, we will use the meditative tool of the "sound of silence"—a subtle, inner resonance that can be perceived when the thinking mind quiets down. Resting attention here is a way of experiencing a state where the "self is gone." As Ajahn Sumedho describes it, "pure conscious awareness is not personal. It is silence behind all the noise." This module points toward a direct recognition of a non-personal, expansive awareness that is our "real home."

Module 10: The Middle Way of Effort - Alert Relaxation

A common pitfall in contemplative practice is applying the wrong kind of effort—a tense, willful striving that creates agitation and frustration. This week, we explore "Right Effort," which is a skillful "middle path" between the extreme of too much effort (which leads to restlessness) and too little effort (which leads to dullness and torpor). Right Effort is not about trying harder, but about applying energy wisely in a state of "alert relaxation." The primary work is twofold: gently "remembering to pay attention" when the mind has wandered, and making "the effort required to stop making the wrong kind of effort" when we notice ourselves striving. Paradoxically, when perfected, Right Effort becomes "no effort. It's effortless. It's natural." This reorients our practice from a struggle for attainment to a state of relaxed, vigilant presence.

Module 11: Cultivating Wholesome States - Kindness, Compassion & Equanimity

While much of our practice has been observational, this week we explore active methods for cultivating positive, or wholesome, mental qualities known as the Brahma Viharas or "Divine Abodes." These are not sentimental feelings but robust, stable states of mind that support our journey and improve our interactions with others. We will frame them in secular terms as universal human capacities:

* Metta: Unconditional Kindness, an attitude of goodwill that begins with oneself and extends outward.
* Karuna: Compassion, the ability to be present with the suffering of oneself and others without being overwhelmed.
* Mudita: Sympathetic Joy, the capacity to rejoice in the happiness and good fortune of others. This serves as a powerful antidote to envy and resentment; as a practice, it is the conscious decision "not to dwell on what I don't like about people."
* Upekkha: Equanimity, a balanced and unshakable state of mind that remains stable in the face of life's "eight worldly dharmas": praise and blame, success and failure, pleasure and pain, gain and loss. These practices demonstrate how we can live in society "not through personal preferences or views, but through compassion."

Module 12: Living with Integrity - A Foundation for Self-Respect

This week, we examine the role of morality (sila), not as a restrictive set of external rules, but as a "liberating convention" that creates the necessary foundation for genuine self-respect. In this framework, self-respect is not sought through external validation or modern therapeutic models that encourage "endlessly thinking about yourself and figuring yourself out." Instead, it arises naturally from "living in a way that you respect"—primarily by committing to refraining from actions of body and speech that cause harm to ourselves and others. This ethical foundation simplifies our lives, reduces inner conflict, and provides the stability and peace of mind required for deeper contemplative inquiry.

However, a subtle trap lies within this very convention. The fetter of silabbata-paramasa is the tendency to cling to the convention itself as the goal. This is the attachment to the rules, the rituals, or the identity of being a "good meditator" as a means of salvation, rather than using them as skillful tools for liberation. This module encourages us to use morality as a practical foundation, not a source of rigid identity.

Module 13: Contemplation on Impermanence & Death

This module addresses the practice of contemplating impermanence (anicca) and, by extension, our own mortality. This is not presented as a morbid or frightening exercise, but as a profound wisdom practice designed to help us see reality clearly and reduce fear. The instruction is to "constantly recall the caducity of all things to meditate on death day in and day out" in order to be "free and prepared to face ultimate reality." By reflecting on the inevitability of change and death, we begin to loosen our attachments to transient conditions, live with a greater sense of urgency and appreciation for the present, and face the future with less anxiety. This practice is a powerful catalyst for letting go of what is ultimately beyond our control.

Module 14: Final Integration & Application

In our final week, we synthesize the central themes of the course, focusing on the integration of contemplative practice into the fabric of everyday life. The ultimate goal is not to achieve a special, tranquil state on a meditation cushion, but to carry this "awakened attention" into our work, our relationships, and all our activities. The practice becomes about using every situation—loving something, hating something, feeling bored or excited—as an opportunity "for awareness to discern." We will conclude by reiterating that this path is not about a final destination but is a continuous, moment-to-moment process of remembering, investigating, and applying these principles of awareness and wisdom in the here and now.

3.0 Assignments & Grading

3.1 Grading Breakdown

* Weekly Journal Reflections: 30%
* Midterm Reflective Essay: 30%
* Final Integration Paper: 40%

3.2 Final Integration Paper

For your final paper (5-7 pages), select one of the "three fetters" discussed in our course: the personality view (sakaditthi), attachment to conventions as a means of salvation (silabbata-paramasa), or skeptical doubt (vicikiccha).

Prompt: In your paper, you must:

1. Explain: Define and explain your chosen fetter in your own words, using at least two direct quotes from the provided texts to support your explanation.
2. Illustrate: Provide two concrete, personal examples from your own experience that illustrate how this fetter manifests in your thoughts, emotions, or behaviors.
3. Analyze: Describe how specific contemplative practices from this course (e.g., mindfulness of the body, being the "witness," letting go) can be used to "see through" this fetter. Analyze the practical impact this shift in perspective can have on one's relationship to oneself and the world.
