# Deep Work on Forgiving Others: Releasing the Stone in the Shoe
*Adapted by Andy McGuire from teachings and guided meditations by Ajahn Amaro*
*(From Chapter 18: When the Last Breath Comes & The Living Well Dying Well Retreat)*

---

### Contemplative Domain & Teaching Notes
* **Contemplative Domain**: Emotional & Relational Cultivation (*Brahmavihāras*) & Working with Hindrances (*Vyāpāda* / Ill-Will).
* **Core Teaching**: True forgiveness is not condoning harm, approving of cruelty, or minimizing injustice. Rather, it is an act of *Abhaya-Dāna*—the "giving of fearlessness"—recognizing that carrying resentment is like walking through life with a jagged stone in our shoe. We examine the confused, fearful world from which others acted, and we choose not to carry that burden into our future.
* **Tone**: Gentle, unhurried, inviting, and observational. Using the inclusive "we."

---

## Guided Meditation Script

### 1. Somatic Grounding & Establishing the Vessel

[Gentle Bell]

We can begin by finding a posture that feels supportive, stable, and genuinely comfortable. Whether we are sitting upright on a cushion or a chair, or choosing to lie down flat on a mat, we can let our physical base settle into the earth.

Allowing our eyes to gently close, or softening our gaze downward.

Taking a slow, deep breath in... and as we breathe out, feeling the pull of gravity. We can invite the shoulders to drop away from the ears. Softening the brow, letting any tightness around the eyes dissolve. 

If our jaw is clenched, letting it unhinge slightly. If our stomach is tight or knotted up, we can invite the belly to expand naturally with the breath, making space for whatever sensations are present.

[Pause 30s]

For a few moments, simply resting with the natural rhythm of the body breathing. There is nothing to fix or manipulate. The breath comes in on its own; the breath goes out on its own. We are simply arriving here, grounded in our own skin.

[Silence 1 min]

---

### 2. The Frame of the Sacred Hour

In his teachings on dying and living well, Ajahn Amaro invites us into a deep contemplation: *What if we only had one hour left to live?*

We can bring that gentle perspective right into this quiet space. Without any alarm or morbidity, we can simply recognize the finite nature of this human life. We do not know how many days or breaths remain to us. 

When we step into this spacious perspective, we can ask ourselves honestly:
*What are we still carrying around in our hearts?*
*Who are we carrying from ten, twenty, or fifty years ago?*
*Do we really want to carry these old wounds and grievances into our final breath?*

[Pause 30s]

---

### 3. Bringing the Person to Mind

Now, we can gently invite to mind someone with whom we have carried unresolved resentment, hurt, or a long-standing grudge. 

It might be someone from our early childhood—a parent, an older sibling, a relative, or a schoolteacher who singled us out. Or it might be a bully from our school days, an employer who took advantage of us, a friend who betrayed our trust, or a partner who wounded us deeply.

Bringing their image or their name into awareness. 

As we call them to mind, we can notice what happens in the physical body right now. 
Perhaps the chest tightens. Perhaps our stomach knots up, or our throat constricts. There is no need to push that reaction away. We can simply notice: *Aversion feels like this. The residue of that injury feels like this.*

[Pause 45s]

---

### 4. Investigating the World They Lived In

Looking at this person who hurt us, we can begin to inquire with gentle curiosity:

*When they spoke those words, or acted in that cruel, thoughtless, or selfish way—were they happy?*
*Were they acting from deep wisdom, clarity, and peace?*

Or were they driven by their own unresolved pain, their own deep insecurities, fears, and restlessness? Were they caught in the grip of anger, greed, or confusion?

[Pause 30s]

Isn't it true that human beings who cause harm are almost always living in a miserable, contracted world? They are reacting blindly to their own internal storms, striking out because their own hearts are full of fear and turmoil.

We can see the universal truth: *Unawakened beings make mistakes.* They act from conditioned habit and confusion. We can recognize that their cruelty did not bring them true happiness; it only deepened their own suffering.

Without excusing what happened, can we begin to sense the tragedy of that confusion? 

[Pause 45s]

---

### 5. The Critical Distinction: Acceptance vs. Approval

Here, we can remind ourselves of something essential: **Forgiveness is never approval.**

There is no need to pretend that what they did was right, or fair, or acceptable. We do not have to say, *"It didn't matter,"* or *"I'm glad it happened."* An injury occurred. The scar may still be there. 

Acceptance simply means: *This is what happened.*
It means recognizing that people dominated by ignorance and fear will act in ignorant and fearful ways. 

Our continued hatred cannot change the past. It cannot undo the words spoken or the actions taken. Nursing our wrath only keeps the fire burning in our own living room. 

As the Buddha reminded us in the *Dhammapada*: 
*"Hatred is never conquered by hatred; hatred is only conquered by love. This is a law ancient and inexhaustible."*

[Silence 1 min]

---

### 6. Meeting the Resistance ("I Can't Forgive")

As we hold this reflection, we might notice an inner voice that resists. We might feel the jaw tighten or the hands clench, saying: *"I can't forgive this. It was too unfair. They don't deserve it."*

If that resistance arises, we don't fight it. We can simply wrap awareness around the resistance itself. 

We can notice: *The feeling of "I can't forgive" is like this.* 
We can ask: *Who does this clenched feeling belong to? Is this clenching serving my peace?*

Can we be patient even with our own lack of forgiveness? Can we forgive ourselves for feeling protective and hurt? 

We don't have to force an artificial resolution. We can just ask our heart: 
*Can I loosen the grip just five percent?*
*Can I give this person space to have been foolish, wounded, and imperfect?*

[Pause 45s]

---

### 7. Releasing the Burden (*Abhaya-Dāna*)

The English word *forgive* holds the word **give** right at its center. 
Forgiveness is an act of giving. It is *Abhaya-Dāna*—the gift of fearlessness. 

We give the other person permission to be human, with all their conditioning and flaws. And more than anything, we give ourselves the gift of freedom. We decide that we are no longer willing to walk around carrying them like a heavy stone in our shoe.

With the next few out-breaths, we can consciously invite the intention of release:

*I acknowledge the pain that occurred.*
*I see the confusion and suffering that drove it.*
*I choose not to carry this grudge into the rest of my life.*
*I let you go.*
*May you be free from the confusion and fear that caused you to harm others.*
*May my own heart be at peace.*

[Pause 1 min]

Feeling the physical weight of that resentment lifting from our chest, from our shoulders, from our belly. Breathing in spaciousness; breathing out release.

[Silence 2 min]

---

### 8. Closing Recollection & Dedication

Gently bringing awareness back to the physical form resting here.
Noticing the rhythm of the breathing.
Noticing the contact with the floor or the chair.

Whatever degree of letting go took place today—whether a total release or just a hairline fracture in an old wall of resentment—we can acknowledge it with gratitude. This is the noble work of untying the knots in our own consciousness.

May any peace, clarity, or goodwill cultivated in this practice be shared with all beings, without exception. 
May all beings everywhere be safe.
May all beings be free from ill-will, enmity, and suffering.
May all beings dwell with ease.

[Three Long Bells]
