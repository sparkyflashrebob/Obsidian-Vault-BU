A Buddhist monk who set himself alight as an act of protest has died of his injuries in Sri Lanka.

Bowatte Indarathana was protesting against the slaughter of cattle and the alleged conversion of Buddhists by the country's Muslim and Christian minority faiths.

He set himself on fire on Friday outside the country's holiest shrine - the Temple of the Tooth in the central city of Kandy.

The government condemned media outlets showing shocking video of the incident.

'Drawing the line'

The monk - believed to be aged 30 - belonged to a Buddhist revivalist group which has been campaigning against the Muslim halal method of slaughtering animals.

Buddhism forbids its own believers from killing animals but many Buddhists in Sri Lanka do eat meat, including beef, the BBC's Charles Haviland says.

Venerable Indarathana had also been calling for an end to proselytising by Christians and followers of other faiths in the Buddhist-majority nation of about 20m people.

Dozens of monks from the same group and their supporters later staged a demonstration in the capital Colombo after the authorities rejected their demand for the monk to be given a state funeral.

Self-immolation by Buddhist monks in Sri Lanka is exceptionally rare, although many Tibetan monks have recently committed suicide in this way for political reasons, our correspondent adds.

Meanwhile, senior government official Charith Herath told the BBC the monk had forewarned a reporter from a private TV station of his plan and the journalist then filmed the suicide instead of preventing it.

The official said the media should "draw the line" and stop certain things happening.

IMO, not at all. Non-involvement was the original intent.

But then Buddhism is pragmatic about life, so even if ‘non involvement’ is the policy, pragmatic involvements to some degree may be seen as just plain inevitable.

Consider the example of young child crying for the moon, the moon is not to be attained by the mere wishful desire. In the same way ‘active non involvement’ aka liberation from society ( aka Nirvana) , is not to be had by wishing. It is an end to be strived for, via the eightfold path.

‘The non-committing of any and all evil, the completing of all that is good/skillful, and the purification of mind’ is the usual framework/guideline of all Buddhist thought regardless of sect.

Yes, Buddhists can. Buddhism does not teach unilateral passiveness. If you are attacked, or the Dharma is attacked, you have the right to defend yourself.

Let’s consider the position of the Allies in WW2. What is those people had refused to fight? An evil ideology would have taken over the world. The Nazis not only intended to kill all the Jews. They intended to kill all the Slavs and all the Africans. Buddhists considered that it was their duty to fight.

Many Thai Buddhists, before doing their army service, give back the precepts to the monk from whom they received them. In Buddhism, you may give back something unbroken. They know that, as soldiers, they may break the First Precept against killing. Then when they have completed their service, they ask for the Precepts again.

I like this. It shows a proper awareness of the value of life and of Buddhist teaching, although in fact soldier is not a prohibited profession in Buddhism.

Contrariwise, at the start of the Second World War, the Japanese government - which was officially Shinto - asked various Buddhist traditions, of which there are many in Japan, whether they supported the war. All except Rinzai Zen and Risho Kosekai replied that they were against the war. This led to them being persecuted throughout the war.

What does it mean to be ordained as a Buddhist monk? What are the benefits of doing so? How is it done?

](https://www.quora.com/What-does-it-mean-to-be-ordained-as-a-Buddhist-monk-What-are-the-benefits-of-doing-so-How-is-it-done)

Basically, you take the vows of that particular Buddhist tradition WITH your teacher (who is a monk) and start to live AS a monk. Some traditions require that you live your normal life, taking in-person lessons with a monk, learning to DO the practices .. and giving yourself time to see if you have sustained interest in Buddhism and doing the mental discipline. Many people who come to Buddhism all excited drop out between the 1st and 3rd year .. and many who stick with it do NOT want to live a monk’s life.

Buddhism is work, and a monk works 24/7.

There are over 200 monks’ vows, and they include chastity, poverty and not working for money (in some traditions, not even handling money). So the monk joins a monastery, wears the robes of that tradition, lives with the other monks, and when not sleeping (they all get up at dawn), is doing group chanting or group meditations, or is reading/memorizing/being tested .. and all the rest of the time they do whatever chores the Abbot has assigned them to do. They serve the needs of the monastery, rather than their own needs.

In return, the monastery provides shelter, food, a new set of robes once a year, and provides whatever eye/dental/medical care it can afford to provide. Which may not be much since the monastery is a non-profit organization that relies on donations from the public. Because the monks have renounced a worldly life and material possession, they need little OTHER than shelter, food and clothing.

If you are interested in becoming a monk, FIRST see if you stay interested in being a Buddhist layperson (living your regular life, but daily doing the practices that your teacher has given you … whether or not you feel like it that day). If you are still interested after 5 years of this, ask your teacher if they think you are ready to take the vows of a monk.

It is NOT an easy life. Enlightenment is said to take countless lifetimes to fully achieve, and going into the monastery does not instantly make you an enlightened person. Basically, you are living with a bunch of other non-enlightened people and there are cliques, factions … and disputes (with the Abbot, or the Disciplinarian, will listen to and order how you will handle yourself). You do not have any of the distracting pleasures you currently have, including your cell phone, the internet, TV, the stimulation of change in our days, personal relationships (beyond friendships), tasty food, sex, etc.

The rewards? Buddhism is a system of mind-training where the goal is to become and then remain aware. As you gain skill at this over the years/decades, you increasingly start to observe, to “catch yourself” in the process OF making yourself unhappy or anxious or fearful or whatever. Even your desires come under this scrutiny. And as you start to observe in real-time how you are the cause of your discontent and unhappiness, slowly you let go of urgency and instead of thinking you “must” react/pursue something, instead you learn to accept, relax .. and to focus on the well-being of other rather than on yourself.

Even modern psychology understands that:

- The more we focus on ourselves, the more neurotic and unhappy we become and that learning to care about people creates inner happiness.
- Also that external things might distract us from inner unhappiness, but once the newness wears off, the discontent returns.
- And that 95% of how we feel arises automatically from whatever kinds of patterns we have fed into our brain. Change your input and gradually your change what comes out of your brain.

Buddha realized all of these truths long before psychology discovered them. Buddhism is a system of mind-training … of addressing all of these human truths, and the benefit is that you slowly become more at peace and delighted .. regardless of external situations. The following quote would be from someone who had become fully enlightened, but even a LITTLE bit in this direction creates great relief:

“Buddha was asked, ‘What have you gained from meditation?’ He replied: ‘Nothing! However, let me tell you what I have lost: anger, anxiety, depression, insecurity, fear of old age and death.’ “ Source: found on the website of a Buddhist temple. What is not mentioned in this quote is the delight and acceptance and also the sense of connection with others than comes from both insight and compassion.

The statement “Buddhist monks protested against American involvement in Vietnam” points to an important truth, but is poorly stated.

We do not protest, we live and demonstrate by how we live and how we speak.

We are not against anyone. If we have an “enemy,” it is not a person or a nation, but the greed, fear, and delusion that exist in us all. But our relationship with greed, fear, and delusion is not opposition, but all-embracing love.

Our goal during the Vietnam war was not to end American involvement, but to end all killing and harming.

> That is the Buddha’s way of looking. Man is not our enemy. The things we need to transform are fanaticism, rivalry, discrimination, fear, and attachment. We share a mindful, loving, truthful way of living

To be loving means to wish everyone and everything well. May each person and each living thing and our whole world be happy, safe, and free; safe from harm and free from suffering; growing in healthy vitality, joy, and happiness; and we know our own wishes, alone, are not enough.

We offer all we do - and our lives, when appropriate, to these goals. We stand for peace and, when appropriate, speak truth to power.

More often though, we care for others. We care for victims of war and wounded warriors. What began in Vietnam as a movement to care for people in bombed out villages and find medicine, food, and homes for orphans of the war grew to embrace, after the war, American Vietnam veterans who suffered PTSD after the war. We all need care.

Living this way in the midst of a war or any partisan conflict is likely to be dangerous. The Order of Interbeing was slated for assasination by the North Vietnamese, who called us CIA plants. Our work did not end when the war ended. It goes on today and lives for the present and the future.

If you know the term mindfulness meditation, you learned it through books written by Thich Nhat Hanh, or works drawn from those. We seek health and peace at all levels: in families, in communities, in nations, and around the world. We work to develop sustainable lifestyles that protect all species and help Mother Earth become more in balance in spite of the effects of human activities.

Why?

We have found that being for love, we remain in love. Being against hate or against evil brings an embattled, ultimately warlike mentality that opens doors to anger, fear, and violence.

Whatever is in our hearts and minds, we will, one way or another, make real. May we become aware of what is inside us, nourish healthy vitality, joy, and peace, and gently embrace the roots of suffering - in ourselves and others - in harmless love.

How did the first Buddhist monks spread the teaching of Buddha?

](https://www.quora.com/How-did-the-first-Buddhist-monks-spread-the-teaching-of-Buddha)

It was taught orally, and only by word-of-mouth for the first 800 to 900 years after Buddha died.

Understand that it is NOT the ideas, concepts, philosophy that matter. Nor is Buddhism a religion. It is mind-training, and the techniques were passed down from teacher to student, in-person, for centuries. Buddhism is mind-training, with the goal of developing awareness/introspection through a meditation practice, and then learning to recapture and retain that level of awareness when NOT meditating. THIS is when you start to gain wisdom that slowly produces inner peace.

“For those who have no introspection, though they hear the teachings, ponder them, or meditate, like water seeping from a leaking jar, their learning will not settle in their memories.” Shantideva (“The Way of the Bodhisattva”, ~700 C.E./A.D.)

So the teachings are NOT the ideas .. they are the techniques. Yes, many ideas have been written down over the past centuries .. but they don’t give you insight or inner change. They are meant to motivate you TO meditated and work on mindfulness … and to help confirm what YOU are starting to recognize as you become and remain mindful/aware/introspective.
