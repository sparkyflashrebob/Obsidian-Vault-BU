## Highlights
- ***Catuṣkoṭi*** ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit); [Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari): चतुष्कोटि, [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script): མུ་བཞི, [Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wylie_transliteration): *mu bzhi*, [Sinhalese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinhala_language):චතුස්කෝටිකය) is a logical argument(s) of a 'suite of four discrete functions' or 'an indivisible quaternity' that has multiple applications and has been important in the [Dharmic traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmic_traditions) of [Indian logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_logic), the [Buddhist logico-epistemological traditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_logic), particularly those of the [Madhyamaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhyamaka) school.
  In particular, the catuṣkoṭi is a "four-cornered" system of argumentation that involves the systematic examination of each of the 4 possibilities of a proposition, *P*: ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gs6w1p7vtwks4ctvhdr9dspf))
- • *P*; that is being.
  • not *P*; that is not being.
  • *P* and not *P*; that is being and that is not being.
  • not (*P* or not *P*); that is neither not being nor is that being. ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gs6w1fgve1vgjn5x8qaqp3gs))
- These four statements hold the following properties: (1) each alternative is mutually exclusive (that is, one of, but no more than one of, the four statements is true) and (2) that all the alternatives are together exhaustive (that is, at least one of them must necessarily be true).[[_A_My Research/_Buddhism/_Dharma Subjects and Themes/Logic and Philosophy 1/Catuskoti]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJayatilleke1967-1) This system of logic not only provides a novel method of classifying propositions into logical alternatives, but also because it does so in such a manner that the alternatives are not dependent on the number of truth-values assumed in the system of logic.[[_A_My Research/_Buddhism/_Dharma Subjects and Themes/Logic and Philosophy 1/Catuskoti]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-FOOTNOTEJayatilleke1967-1)
  To understand it for example if we put [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems) in Catuskoti format look as follows,
  Given any consistent formal system F within which a certain amount of elementary arithmetic can be carried out and given the logical statement constructed inside such system can always be:
  1. Proved
  2. Disproved
  3. Proved and Disproved
  4. Neither Proved nor Disproved ([View Highlight](https://read.readwise.io/read/01gs6w21t07w240y1rse1r7gj8))

- Alternate Four Limits/Four Extremes[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi&action=edit&section=11)]
  A Mantrayana enumeration of the Four Limits or the Four Extremes within the Buddhadharma is also common. These four 'limits' are evident in the earliest sutras of the Theravadin of the First Turning, through the Second Turning philosophy of Nagarjuna and his disciples and commentators and also evident in the Third Turning as evidenced in the presentation of Padmasambhava. Padmasambhava in his 'Secret Instruction in a Garland of Vision' [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script): མན་ངག་ལྟ་བའི་ཕྲེང་བ, [Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wylie_transliteration): *man ngag lta ba'i phreng ba* lists them as follows with the English rendering following [Dowman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Dowman) (2003)[[_A_My Research/_Buddhism/_Dharma Subjects and Themes/Suttas 1/Brahmajala Sutta]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-27) and Wylie following [Norbu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namkhai_Norbu) *et al.* (2001):[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catu%E1%B9%A3ko%E1%B9%ADi#cite_note-28)
  • the ['Hedonist' or 'Chalpas'](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%BD%95%E0%BE%B1%E0%BD%A3%E0%BC%8B%E0%BD%94%27) [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script): ཕྱལ་པ, [Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wylie_transliteration): *phyal pa*: does not perceive, ascribe to the view or realize that all events, dharmas, etc. have a cause and an effect;
  • the ['Atheist' or 'Gyangphenpas'](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%BD%A2%E0%BE%92%E0%BE%B1%E0%BD%84%E0%BC%8B%E0%BD%A0%E0%BD%95%E0%BD%BA%E0%BD%93%E0%BC%8B%E0%BD%94) [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script): རྒྱང་འཕེན་པ, [Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wylie_transliteration): *rgyang 'phen pa*: unable to see or perceive past and future lives, the atheist toils for wealth and power in this lifetime alone. They engage in intrigue;
  • the ['Nihilist' or 'Murthugpas'](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%BD%98%E0%BD%B4%E0%BD%A2%E0%BC%8B%E0%BD%90%E0%BD%B4%E0%BD%82%E0%BC%8B%E0%BD%94) [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script): མུར་ཐུག་པ, [Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wylie_transliteration): *mur thug pa*: holds that there is no causality or causal relationship between events and dharmas. They are of the view that everything is adventitiously arisen due to chance and events and that dharmas dissipate and vanish into the void. Death is the ultimate cessation and there is no continuity between lives; and
  • the ['Eternalist' or 'Mutegpas'](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%BD%98%E0%BD%B4%E0%BC%8B%E0%BD%A6%E0%BE%9F%E0%BD%BA%E0%BD%82%E0%BD%A6%E0%BC%8B%E0%BD%94) [Tibetan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script): མུ་སྟེགས་པ, [Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wylie_transliteration): *mu stegs pa*: holds to the view of an eternal, unchanging '[atman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atman_(Buddhism))', where atman is often rendered as 'soul' in English. There is considerable diversity of the mechanics of causality with proponents of this view. Some perceive the atman as having a cause but not effect, an effect but no cause, or indeed a complex causality or causal relationship.
