In Buddhism, **name-and-form** (Pali: **[[namarupa]]**; Sanskrit: **namarupa**) is a compound term that refers to the <u>psycho-physical constituents of a living being</u>. The term is made up of two words: **nama**, which means <u>"name" or "mental factors</u>," and **rupa**, which means "form" or <u>"physical body."</u>
   
   In the Pali Canon, the Buddha describes name-and-form as follows:
   
   > "And what [monks] is name-&-form? Feeling, perception, intention, contact, & attention: This is called name. The four great elements, and the form dependent on the four great elements: This is called form. This name & this form are, [monks], called name-&-form."
   
   (**Samyutta Nikaya 22.54**)

[[Nama]]
   The five mental factors that are included in name are:
   
   - Feeling (vedana)
   - Perception (sanna)
   - Intention (cetanā)
   - Contact (phassa)
   - Attention (manasikara)
   
   The four physical elements that are included in form are:
   
   - Earth (pathavi)
   - Water (ap)
   - Fire (tejo)
   - Air (vayu)
   
   Name-and-form is a key concept in the Buddhist understanding of the nature of reality. It is seen as a <u>transient and impermanent phenomenon that is subject to change</u>. The Buddha taught that the goal of the Buddhist path is to <u>overcome attachment to name-and-form</u> and to achieve liberation from the cycle of rebirth.
   
   The concept of name-and-form is also found in other Indian religious traditions, such as Hinduism and Jainism. However, in Buddhism, it has a specific meaning that is related to the Buddha's teaching on dependent arising ([[paticca-samuppada]]). According to this teaching, all phenomena arise in dependence on other phenomena. Name-and-form is seen as one of the twelve links in the chain of dependent arising, and it is said to <u>arise from ignorance</u> (avijja).
   
   The concept of name-and-form can be a helpful tool for understanding the nature of reality and for developing insight into the workings of the mind. By contemplating the impermanence and interconnectedness of name-and-form, we can begin to see through the illusion of self and to cultivate a deeper understanding of the Buddha's teachings.
