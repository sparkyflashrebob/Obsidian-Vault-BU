Forgiveness is considered essential to spiritual practice especially in Western Buddhism. But it is not exclusively practiced in buddhist communities and, in fact, there are some who say it is not a buddhist practice at all.

The Webster Third New International Dictionary defines forgiveness as “to cease to feel
resentment against on account of a wrong committed”. So part of this shedding is letting go of resentment and forgiving.

This definition is illustrated in a well-known Tibetan Buddhist story about two monks who encounter each other some years after being released from prison where they had been tortured by their captors.

> “Have you forgiven them?” asks the first. 
> “I will never forgive them! Never!” replies the second.
> “Well, I guess they still have you in prison, don’t they?” the first says.

At the many Monastic Retreats that I have attended one of the highlights of the typical closing ceremony is the simple practice of forgiveness. The Monastics actually perform two ceremonies that support each other. One, what they call the Pavarana Or Invitation is held at the end of their yearly three month retreat - the Rains Retreat in Southeast Asia and the Winter Retreat in the Northern Hemisphere - where they gather and invite feedback from the other monastics, where they intentionally ask for correction from, as he puts it, from “your friends in the Holy life.”

There is a story of the Buddha who said “if you were offered a choice between a pile of gold - a huge pile of gold - a vast fortune - or to ask someone to give you feedback about your conduct, you would be wise to ask for the feedback. That's more valuable to you than a big pile of gold.

So the buddha gets our attention in this very skillful way. It's more valuable for us to get
feedback. Because if your friends won't tell you that what you're saying that is upsetting, or the way that you relate to others or the way you see yourself - if your friends won't tell you where you are off balance or where you're acting in a way that is unkind or disharmonious - how are you gonna know? 

Ignorance is the last Fetter to go on the path to Enlightenment so it is very deep in us. This delusion is not so easy to see and so this kind of invitation for feedback is because we can't see for ourselves where we are making mistakes where we are say upsetting others or our behavior is out of balance and so that it's helping us to see through our own delusions our own distorted perceptions. 

So that's where kalyanamitta - spiritual friendship - is so important.

The Monastics have another little ceremony that is maybe more on-point to this discussion which is called “Asking for Forgiveness”. Okay so here is the list:
1. Understanding what forgiveness is and what it is not.
2. Sense the suffering that comes with the inability to forgive.
3. Reflect on the benefits of a loving heart.
4. Discover (in relation to your identity), you don't have to be loyal to your suffering.
(stories/identity)
5. Understand that forgiveness is a process, not a single action.
6. You have to set your intention for forgiveness.
7. Learn the inner and outer forms of forgiveness.
8. Start with the easiest thing that can open your heart.
9. Be willing to grieve and let go.
10. Sometimes trauma is stored in our physical bodies.
11. Shift of identity (from me to other).
12. Perspective (from me to we) - Bigger than us.

This 1st Principal: Understanding what forgiveness is and what it is not means that we are not condoning or papering over our transgressions or the transgressions of others. And it is not for the other person. 
Each one of us is the owner of our own karma and will inherit the results of our actions, be they skillful or unskillful. We may end up offering them metta and come to
understand the suffering that they have endured but that does not necessarily absolve them of their actions.

The 2nd Principal: To Sense the suffering that comes with the inability to forgive asks us to consider our own suffering. To sense the suffering in ourselves when we hold on to this lack of forgiveness for yourself or another.
	● This comes with the understanding that it's not compassionate that you have this great suffering.
	● That it's not in our best interest to keep running with the thoughts and feeling the feelings. Stress is damaging to our heart and body.
	● In understanding this principle we actually sense the weight of not forgiving

For the 11th Principle: 
We understand that with forgiveness there comes a shift of  identity. 
- There is in us an undying capacity for love and freedom that is untouched by what happens to you. 
- To come back to this true nature is the work of forgiveness.

And finally for Principle Number 12: 
Jack says this Forgiveness involves perspective. 
We are in this drama in life that is so much bigger than our ‘little stories.’ When we can open this perspective, we see it is not just your hurt, but the hurt of humanity. Everyone who loves is hurt in some way. Everyone who enters the marketplace gets betrayed. The loss is not just your pain, it is the pain of being alive. Then you feel connected to everyone in this vastness that is life.
