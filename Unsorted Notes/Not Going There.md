“Choosing not to go there” has a flavor that Buddhism would nod at.

- **Restraint (saṃvara):** The Buddha often praised knowing when not to pursue a line of thought, word, or action that would add more agitation. Stepping back is its own kind of wisdom.
    
- **Right effort:** The teaching doesn’t say “never think,” but points to abandoning unwholesome states and guarding the mind against ones not yet arisen. Not going there is effort in the direction of peace.
    
- **Dependent arising:** By not feeding the link, the chain doesn’t roll forward. Choosing silence or non-engagement breaks a cycle before it grows.
    
- **Noble silence:** There are suttas where the Buddha stays quiet instead of entertaining fruitless debates. “Not going there” echoes that same stance.
    

So yes, the connections are real: in Buddhism, sometimes wisdom is shown not by entering, but by stepping aside.

Here’s how the gesture of “not going there” resonates across traditions:

---

### **Theravāda**

- **Focus:** Guarding the sense doors and right effort.
    
- To decline chasing a thought, word, or impulse is exactly the practice of restraining the mind before it builds momentum.
    
- The commentaries talk about _yoniso manasikāra_ (wise attention) vs. _ayoniso_ (unwise). “Not going there” is like refusing to water the unwise seeds.
    

---

### **Zen**

- **Focus:** Directness and cutting off entanglement.
    
- Zen teachers often answer with silence, a shout, or turning away instead of entering a conceptual debate.
    
- “Not going there” could be seen as a koan in action—showing the futility of entering the maze of thought when presence is enough.
    

---

### **Mahāyāna (Madhyamaka & beyond)**

- **Focus:** Emptiness and compassion.
    
- In Nāgārjuna’s style, “going there” often means getting trapped in one side of a view. Choosing not to go is declining to reify extremes—existence vs. nonexistence, self vs. no-self.
    
- In the Bodhisattva path, not going into unwholesome states also preserves energy for compassionate action.
    

---

### **Tibetan (Vajrayāna)**

- **Focus:** Working skillfully with appearances.
    
- Sometimes restraint looks like stepping back (_shamatha_). Other times, it means transforming what arises through visualization or mantra.
    
- “Not going there” can mean refusing to be captured by the solidity of appearances—remembering they’re dreamlike.
    

---

### **Common Thread**

Across the board, the point isn’t repression. It’s clarity: knowing when engaging would deepen delusion versus when pausing opens space for wisdom.

Alright, let’s pull it into the modern room:

---

### **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) & Clinical Settings**

- “Not going there” is often taught as _choosing where to place attention._
    
- Instead of replaying the stressful story, you feel your feet, the breath, or the body. That’s restraint without judgment—attention shifted, not suppressed.
    

---

### **Trauma-Sensitive Practice**

- When the nervous system is raw, “not going there” means respecting limits.
    
- A teacher might guide someone away from revisiting traumatic material in meditation, because retraumatization helps no one. The skill is titration: allowing a little bit, then stepping back.
    
- Here, it’s compassion in action—safety first, awakening second.
    

---

### **Everyday Mindfulness (the secular flavor)**

- Often phrased as “not feeding the wolf.” You notice anger bubbling, but you don’t spin the rant.
    
- Apps and workplace mindfulness use the line “pause, notice, choose.” That’s the same principle—deciding not to wander into mental loops.
    

---

### **Somatic & Neuroscience Angle**

- Brain science calls this _response inhibition_ and _prefrontal regulation._
    
- Not going there isn’t about clamping down; it’s about redirecting networks before they spiral into fight/flight.
    
- In mindfulness terms: you’re training neural pathways of choice rather than compulsion.
    

---

**Thread through all of it:**  
“Not going there” is freedom at the micro-level. It’s not denial—it’s the art of recognizing: _this path will bind me tighter,_ and then stepping aside.

Here’s a simple way to work with it when the mind is tugging:

---

### **A 4-Step “Not Going There” Sequence**

1. **Spot the hook.**  
    Notice the first flash—an image, a tone of voice in your head, the rise of irritation. Catching it early is key.
    
2. **Name it lightly.**  
    Acknowledge: _“worrying,” “judging,” “planning,”_ whatever it is. This gives it shape without feeding it.
    
3. **Choose your redirect.**  
    Bring attention to a safe anchor: breath, the ground under your feet, or the feel of your hands. If emotions are strong, choose body sensation over thought.
    
4. **Release without fanfare.**  
    No need for a struggle. Just don’t add wood to the fire. If the thought returns (it will), repeat steps 1–3 with the same casual clarity.
    

---

### **Alternative Angles**

- **Visual metaphor:** Imagine the thought as a bus pulling up. You see it, nod, but don’t get on.
    
- **Body-based:** When tension rises, exhale slowly and feel the shoulders drop—that signals “not boarding the train.”
    
- **Compassion lens:** Ask, _“What would ease look like here?”_ Often the answer is simply not diving deeper.
    

---

### **Practical Action Plan**

- Practice the sequence once or twice a day with small irritations (emails, traffic).
    
- Use the same anchor each time (breath, feet, hands) so it becomes a reliable “home base.”
    
- Notice not just what you _avoid_, but the space that opens when you step aside.
