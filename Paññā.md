[cite_start] **Paññā** (Pali) or **Prajna** (Sanskrit) refers to **Wisdom, Understanding, or Discernment**[cite: 23, 27, 33]. [cite_start]It is one of the three foundational divisions of the Noble Eightfold Path, alongside **Sila** (Ethical Conduct) and **Samadhi** (Concentration)[cite: 23, 29, 33].

### Core Definitions and Components
* [cite_start]**Fundamental Meaning**: Paññā is defined as the understanding of the true nature of reality, specifically gaining insight into the **Four Noble Truths** and the **Three Characteristics of Existence** (Impermanence, Suffering, and Non-self)[cite: 23, 33].
* **Path Factors**: In the Eightfold Path, the Paññā group consists of:
    * [cite_start]**Right View (Samma-ditthi)**: Knowledge of the Four Noble Truths and understanding that actions have consequences[cite: 23, 33].
    * [cite_start]**Right Intention/Resolve (Samma-sankappa)**: Wholesome motivations such as selfless renunciation, loving-kindness, and non-violence[cite: 23, 33].

### The Three Types of Paññā
Your documents categorize wisdom into three levels of development:
1.  [cite_start]**Learned Wisdom**: Acquired by listening to others or reading books[cite: 23].
2.  [cite_start]**Reflective Wisdom**: Developed through logical thinking, reasoning, and intellectual analysis[cite: 23].
3.  [cite_start]**Spiritual/Insight Wisdom**: Derived from direct meditative experience, such as **Vipassana** (insight) or **Jhana** (absorption)[cite: 23, 25].

### Functional Role in Practice
* [cite_start]**The "Weapon"**: In the "Warrior Model" of the Thai Forest Tradition, Paññā is described as the weapon used to destroy the "enemy" of mental defilements (greed, hatred, and delusion)[cite: 21].
* [cite_start]**Interdependence**: Wisdom is seen as one of the "two wings of a bird" (the other being compassion) or as emerging naturally once the mind is made pure and calm through concentration[cite: 23, 33].
* [cite_start]**One of the Five Faculties**: It acts as a controlling faculty that must be balanced with **Faith** (Saddha) to prevent practice from becoming overly intellectual or purely ritualistic[cite: 23, 24].
