MMR#64 - Perspectives on the Heart
Sutra
In the upcoming July edition of Lion’s Roar - a bi-monthly buddhist magazine formally known as
Shambala Sun there is a feature on The Heart Sutra that I found to be very moving. We have
not discussed emptiness here so far so much because it comes more from the Mahayana/Zen
tradition. However the concept is a part of all buddhist practice. In Theravada they normally
discuss this as impermanence - which we refer here to all the time. Today we will have a more in
depth discussion on impermanence and emptiness thru on of the core teachings in the
Mahayana path.
Norman Fisher, a well known local Zen Buddhist Teacher, explains in the featured segment that
many of us have heard of the word bodhisattva - it is defined as a compassionate spiritual being
being completely dedicated to the support of others. This compassion depends on prajna
parimita - which is the sanskrit word for penetrating compassion or more accurately “the
perfection of wisdom”. This perfection of wisdom is contained in the Mahayana Buddhist
teaching called “The Heart Sutra”. This perfection of wisdom is in emptiness and, in the
Mahayana tradition, emptiness and compassion are complementary: they absolutely depend on
each other.
There are actually many teachings on emptiness in Mahayana Buddhism but the greatest and
most succinct is “The Heart Sutra”. It is chanted daily in many buddhist traditions, particularly
Zen buddhism, as it is one the foremost teachings. In Mahayana Buddhism compassion is
different than ordinary compassion. Ordinary compassion involves there being a me here who is
compassionate for you over there. It is conditional and dependent on how much I am willing to
sacrifice. The compassion of the bodhisattva is empty of both a me who gives and another
who receives.
The Heart Sutra, as we will hear in a few minutes, has a list of phenomena, each of which we
have heard before as the aggregates. As you may remember, the aggregates of form, feeling,
perception, mental formations, and consciousness are subjects for mindfulness being contained
in the 4th Foundation of Mindfulness - Mindful of Dhammas or Mind Object.
Each of these phenomena are said to be empty. So, in what sense are they empty? Norman
Fisher points out that they are empty of inherent existence - they exist in some way, but
not in the way that we experience them - as separate, solid, completely real entities. They
are sankhara’s. “This means that there are no helpers or no one being helped. There are
no separate beings; there is only the unceasing coming and going of being.”
Compassion is based on this vision of reality and is therefore “sustainable, joyful, and wide,
devoid of self-sacrifice. No one is compassionate to anyone. Life is compassion.”

Before we go any further I’d like to read “The Heart Sutra”. It is one of my favorite texts, and just
like when I contemplate the Metta Sutta it can evoke some emotion in me.
Here is one of the short versions.

The Heart Sutra
Ava-lok-ite-shvara, the Bodhisattva of Compassion, (otherwise known as Guanyin or
Padma-pani) was meditating deeply on the Perfection of Wisdom, and saw clearly that the five
aspects of human existence are empty*, and so released one from suffering.
Answering the monk Sariputra, one said this:
Body is nothing more than emptiness, emptiness is nothing more than body. The body is exactly
empty, and emptiness is exactly body. The other four aspects of human existence -- feeling,
thought, will, and consciousness -- are likewise nothing more than emptiness, and emptiness
nothing more than they.
All things are empty: Nothing is born, nothing dies, nothing is pure, nothing is stained, nothing
increases and nothing decreases. So, in emptiness, there is no body, no feeling, no thought, no
will, no consciousness. There are no eyes, no ears, no nose, no tongue, no body, no mind.
There is no seeing, no hearing, no smelling, no tasting, no touching, no imagining. There is
nothing seen, nor heard, nor smelled, nor tasted, nor touched, nor imagined. There is no
ignorance, and no end to ignorance. There is no old age and death, and no end to old age and
death. There is no suffering, no cause of suffering, no end to suffering, no path to follow. There
is no attainment of wisdom, and no wisdom to attain.
The Bodhisattvas rely on the Perfection of Wisdom, and so with no delusions, they feel no fear,
and have Nirvana here and now. All the Buddhas, past, present, and future, rely on the
Perfection of Wisdom, and live in full enlightenment. The Perfection of Wisdom is the greatest
mantra. It is the clearest mantra, the highest mantra, the mantra that removes all suffering. This
is truth that cannot be doubted.
Say it so:
Gaté, gaté, paragaté, parasamgaté. Bodhi! Svaha!
Which means... Gone, gone, gone over, gone fully over. Awakened! So be it!
We really have not discussed the view of emptiness in great detail. You may have noticed how
the concept Suññatā and sankharas relate. The Theravada tradition generally takes the view
that emptiness is merely the not-self nature of the five aggregates. The Mahayana tradition - of
which The Heart Sutra teaches that all entities, including dhammas, are empty of self, or
essential core, or of intrinsic nature - being only conceptual constructs.

Following Norman Fischer's introduction in the magazine article, many other well known
teachers featured different takes on this sutra. Language can be tricky. These definitions or
labels can “limit” our views or perspectives on something or it can be a means for expanding our
understanding. One of the beautiful things about Buddhism and its emphasis on awakening to
the true nature of reality, I believe, is seeing our experience of life through this lens of dhamma
Finding it be both freeing and therefore liberating. Having a different take on things can open the
door to our limited view of self and other and, as a result, greatly benefit our practice and the
way we see the world. Actually, this is further expansion what we've already been talking about
in terms of our attitude, our views and the positions we can take for our own practice.
One of the supporting pieces was done by Joan Halifax, who is a well-known Zen Buddhist
teacher, anthropologist, ecologist, civil rights activist, hospice caregiver and author of several
books on buddhism and spirituality - who also wrote her own book on the Heart Sutra. She
mentioned that typically before the Heart Sutra (which was created somewhere around 650 AD
or thereafter) buddhist practitioners typically relied on the presence of an external teacher - a
particular Buddha - to show them the way. But that “The Heart Sutra” pointed to an approach
that was revolutionary - one that directed us towards the Mind as a teacher now. This a
refreshing approach that we can consider. Instead of just trying to figure out what the Buddha
was pointing to, we can connect with that innate intuitive awareness in us to free ourselves.
As I mentioned earlier - the term for emptiness - shunyata or sunnata, in Theravada Buddhism
refers to the non-self (anatta) nature of the aggregates and also as a meditative state or
experience. In Mahayana Buddhism it refers to the tenant that “all things are empty of intrinsic
existence and nature” as Norman Fischer defined earlier.
Emptiness is defined as where one cannot identify anything we experience as one's own self or
having anything pertaining to one's own self ... Emptiness - as a mental state - in the early teachings
is said to be a mode of perception in which one neither adds anything to nor takes anything away
from what is present, noting simply, "There is this." or as Ajahn Sumedho states, “it’s like this”.
This is not the position of a person having a personal perspective who clings to experience that is
not really self. But the selfless perspective of the way it is.
This the article Joan chooses to translate shunyata as “boundlessness.” This points to the
mind that cannot be captured, described or limited - a mind that's embedded in the
boundlessness of the present.
Even though space, emptiness, or boundlessness may not seem to have any qualities, it has
some characteristics that form the basis for meditation practice, allowing or encouraging us to
let go - to transform from our grasping into open-handedness. This comes into play in a few
different ways.
This first characteristic that she mentions- lets us meet our practice in a way of not knowing.
This “boundless mind” free of ideas, opinions and conceptions. It's fresh, open and

unmediated”. Instead of coming from a mind of knowing, perceiving, and conceiving- releasing
that and coming to knowing things as they are free from our limiting perspective.
The second aspect she mentions is this groundlessness. The Wisdom beyond wisdom is not
grounded in anything at all. There is no need to define it or try to pin it down. This is not
necessary. Any way of defining actually missed the mark anyway. All the senses are
impermanent, ever changing, not fixed in time or space. Enlightenment is a mystery that cannot
be arrived at through reasoning and the discursive mind reference points and preferences are
discovered to be rooted in fear - of the need for the small self to become more solid and more
grounded.
So then there's this third aspect that she mentions of boundlessness which is immediacy - our
present situation. We cannot explain it. No scientists can unfold it for us yet each moment is the
repository of the past, present, and future and all the sutras or teachings are contained herein.
This experience of immediacy is our boundless original dwelling place, the realization of wisdom
beyond wisdom.
She even mentions a fourth characteristic of boundlessness as an expression of
interconnectedness, the endless connections that flow through our lives. In this present
moment, nothing is missing. We are all here. Our tendency to discriminate, cut and parse out
experience might seem to make us more secure but we cut ourselves away from who we really
are, a self that coexists with all that's cohesive with all beings and things.
So it's in this practice in the heart sutra that this boundless mind is like Buddha, none other than
Buddha. It is mind that is everywhere and yet nowhere. It is a mind that is both timeless and in
embrace of this moment- a mind that has realized life as eternal. It belongs to no one and to
everyone and is a bond of vastness that brings all beings into a unity. Joan remind us: “From
this perspective then how could a historical personage be our ultimate teacher? Our ultimate
teacher is this boundless mind embedded in all beings and things.”
So how can we use these teachings? We can look at it as a meditative dwelling. As an attribute
of objects and as a type of awareness-release.
We can meditate on the substance of what we are able to sense -to get a feel for their qualities -
what is it like to be in a body - to have form? . If we take everything as real and of substance
from a personal perspective we get a perspective that it is like this - even if it is rooted in
delusion and ignorance.
Looking back at our experience - There is a world full of sensation and energies. The Isness of
our experience. Coming from nowhere to express itself and then going back into the void.
Sometimes seemingly extremely busy and at other times calm, silent and still. This is our
playground. This is also as it is. All that we know comes from how we perceive things but
ultimately is not the thing being perceived. Nature is not bound by nor defined by human
understanding. We call this conventional reality.
And when we open our eyes and experience the senses of seeing what has changed? Is
anything in this world solid, persistent and unchanging? This is what is meant as being empty. It
has no real substance on its own. Yet we can know this too.

When we know it as a personality then we experience it from that relationship. Its relationship
to me as a person. I give it value. And, on the other hand, there is the knowing before there is
an “I” that knows it. That knows it directly. Boundlessness - not limited. Groundlessness - not
bound to time and place.. Immediate - knowing without conception & interconnected to all.
Knowing this for ourselves is freedom. Knowing this brings peace. Knowing this brings great
joy that is free from conditions - is not deluded.
I offer this for our consideration.
