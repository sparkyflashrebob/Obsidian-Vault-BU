The Hindrances are five mental states that hijack the mind -make it hard, if not impossible, for
us to stay focused on meditation, and, when not in meditation, prevent us from achieving any
personal growth as human beings.
This evening we venture into a discussion of the last of the Hindrances - known as vicikicchā in
Pali, otherwise known as Skeptical Doubt. .
Joseph Goldstein distinguishes between two general usages of the word doubt.
1. It is an aspect of inquiry and investigation. It motivates us to examine things carefully. In
a well known saying the Buddha urges us to not believe what he says, anything you read
or hear from others (no matter who they are), and to NOT rely on our own logic or
speculation and not to infer or be deceived by appearances - but to find out for ourselves
what is true and virtuous. He encourages what he calls appropriate attention.
Sampajanna. He uses this term in the very beginning of the Satipatthana Sutta to
describe what he deems to define meditation the essential preliminaries to practice and
“Mindfulness”. We are to be ardent, alert, mindful, without greed or distress in the world.
Just as we dont want to gobble up things, we also do not want to dogmatically disbelieve
something because it doesn’t fit into our current views. This is what bias is. Skeptical
Doubt has some benefit to us because when used wisely it is our discernment of what is
beneficial or not.
2. And then there is the 2nd kind of doubt that is considered a hindrance because it is a
mind-state of uncertainty, wavering and indecision.
Vicikicchā
'sceptical doubt', is one of the 5 mental hindrances and one of the 3 fetters. The fetters, or fetters of suffering, are mental chains or shackles that bond us to continuous cycles of dukkha/suffering.* There are the “three fetters”; belief in a self; attachment to rites and rituals; and doubt, and then there are the 10 fetters which are shed during what is called the path of liberation beginning with stream-entry. I have and will continue to talk about what it means to be a stream-enterer because of how much I believe an understanding of three fetters seen together can be a great aid for us to realizing freedom from suffering.

Doubt, as a fetter, refers to sceptical doubt about the the Buddha, the Teachings, the Sangha,
and the training; about things past and future, and conditionality (Dhs.1004; cf. A.X.71).
It also applies to uncertainty whether things are wholesome or not, to be practiced or not, or of
high or low value.
According to the Visuddhimagga, a manual of the teachings known as :”Tthe Path of
Purification”, vicikicchā is the lack of desire to think things out, to come to some conclusion; it
has the nature of wavering, and it shows itself thru indecision and a divided attitude; its
proximate cause is unwise attention to matters of doubt. Learning to recognize doubt is critical
to being about to deal with it wisely.
Doubt is also associated with one of the 2 classes of unwholesome consciousness rooted in
delusion (Tab. I, No. 32).
So there is a lot to do with it.
As I mentioned above, the hindrance of doubt (vicikicchā) refers to doubt about the teachings
but also to one's ability to understand and implement the meditation instructions, as well what I
mentioned as about the teacher and Buddhist teachings in general.
● Ajahn Brahma-vamso states: "Doubt refers to the disturbing inner questions at a time
when one should be silently moving deeper. Doubt can question one's own ability
'Can I do This?,' or question the method 'Is this the right way?,' or even question the
meaning 'What is this?.' It should be remembered that such questions are obstacles
to meditation because they are asked at the wrong time and thus become an
intrusion, obscuring one's clarity."
● Traleg Kyab-gon said : "When we meditate in the presence of this hindrance, we
have a constant nagging feeling: 'How do I know what I am doing is right? How do I
know if this thing really works and if I am not just wasting my time? How do I know
what the Buddhist teachings say is true? How do I know if what the meditation
teachers have taught me is right and that they are not deluded?'

So,in simple terms, Doubt is disgruntlement, disengagement, indecision or confusion, and, at its
most severe, depression.
One has a tendency to make generalized statements when one is with doubt as a Hindrance
such as “I can’t do this” or “this isn’t fair, life sucks.”
We are unable to remember any counter examples - like when we have succeeded or did
something similar that went well, or when we overcame obstacles.
It refers to a lack of confidence and clarity.
There is a collapse of trust, a lack of confidence in ourselves, in the practice, in others, and
whether they have anything to offer us.
All of these above are signs of when skeptical doubt is present.
This is our first step in order to overcome this Hindrance -- to see it.

Analogy
Doubt is compared to being lost in a desert, not recognising any landmarks.

Antidote
● It is said that Doubt is overcome by gathering clear instructions, having a good map,
so that one can recognise the subtle landmarks in the unfamiliar territory of deep
meditation and so know which way to go. Doubt in one's ability is overcome by
nurturing self-confidence with a good teacher.
● The end of doubt, in meditation, is described by a mind which has full trust in the
silence, and so doesn't interfere with any inner speech. Like having a good chauffeur,
one sits silently on the journey out of trust in the driver.

So how might we know when Doubt is present or absent?
First a warning. Just as our thinking and logic can lead us astray at any time, doubt can
masquerade as wisdom. Listening to the voices in our head and not necessarily believing them
is an important understanding of wisdom. I am learning to question my thoughts and my
thinking. Just as I question my moods and emotions. Not to deny them but to know if they are
appropriate to the situation or not. Are they skillful? Moods and emotions themselves are not a
problem. Nothing we experience is. We are simply to notice the way it is, however that may be.
It is our relationship to them that can create suffering.
So back knowing when doubt is present or not - Joseph suggests a couple of ways.
The first step is to see thru this masquerade and recognize when doubt arises. Being able to
see thru own tapes and to note them. Labeling them can be helpful when we recognize them.
As the Buddha said, “Mara, I see you!”
Another thing we can do is purposely and constantly reflect on our state of mind. What is the
state of our mind? Not only that, we can pay attention and notice when it changes from one

Noticing these changes should be a part of our Mindfulness. When we are aware of their
transitory nature we can bow to this as a moment of recognition. This is another aspect of
seeing the impermanent nature of things - that things change. The more we see this truth of
impermanence the more we see its is not something we can or should hold on to as ours.

Summary
Sooner or later all meditators practice with the hindrances. For most, it will mean learning to
deal with them over and over again and at all levels of practice. These obstructing mind states
should not be seen as unfortunate occurrences. Rather, they are opportunities for strengthening
practice -for developing mindfulness, concentration, understanding, and non-clinging.
This from the Dalai Lama:
“So our intelligence makes us very smart, but at the same time, precisely because of
that fact, we also have more doubts and suspicions, and hence more fears. I think the
imagination of fear is much more developed in humans than in other animals. In addition,
the many conflicts within the human family and within one's own family, not to mention
the conflicts within the community and between nations, as well as the internal conflicts
within the individual - all conflicts and contradictions arise from the different ideas
and views our intelligence brings. So unfortunately, intelligence can sometimes create a
quite unhappy state of mind. In this sense, it becomes another source of human misery.

Yet, at the same time, I think that ultimately intelligence is the tool with which we can
overcome all these conflicts and differences.
From this point of view, of all the various species of animal on the planet, human beings
are the biggest troublemakers. That is clear. I imagine that if there were no longer any
humans on the planet, the planet itself would be safer! Certainly millions of fish, chicken
and other small animals might enjoy some sort of genuine liberation!
It is therefore important that human intelligence be utilized in a constructive way.
That is the key. If we utilize its capacity properly, then not only human beings would
become less harmful to each other, and to the planet, but also individual human beings
would be happier in themselves. It is in our hands. Whether we utilize our intelligence in
the right way or the wrong way is up to us. Nobody can impose their values on us. How
can we learn to use our capacity constructively? First, we need to recognize our nature
and then, if we have the determination, there is a real possibility of transforming the human
heart.”
