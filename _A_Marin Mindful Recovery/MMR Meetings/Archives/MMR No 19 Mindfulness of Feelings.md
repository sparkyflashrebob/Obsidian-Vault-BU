MMR#19 Mindfulness of Feelings (notes)
Read Section from Sattipitthana
The Pāli term for “feeling” is vedanā, derived from the verb vedeti, which means both “to feel”
and “to know”.1 In its usage in the discourses, vedanā comprises both bodily and mental
feelings. Feeling is the mental factor that feels the object, the bare underlying quality of an
experience, which may be pleasant, painful or neutral.
It is one of the seven universal mental factors in the Theravada tradition, five in the Mahayana
tradition, one of the 12 links of dependent origination, one of the five khandas or aggregates of
clinging, and, as we know now, the second of the four foundations of Mindfulness.
Vedanā does not include “emotion” in its range of meaning. Although emotions arise depending
on the initial input provided by feeling, they are more complex mental phenomena than bare
feeling itself and are therefore the domain of the next satipaṭṭhāna in the Sattipatthana Sutta,
contemplation of states of mind.

Feelings vs Emotions (from Psychology Today)
Many people use the terms “feeling” and “emotion” as synonyms, but they are not
interchangeable. While they have similar elements, there is a marked difference between
feelings and emotions.
Feelings. Both emotional experiences and physical sensations — such as hunger or pain —
bring about feelings. Feelings are a conscious experience, although not every conscious
experience, such as seeing or believing, is a feeling. The subject of contact is important though.
And we will explore this somewhat when we discuss Dependant Origination.
Withing the Buddhist Teachings 'feeling' is not quote our ordinary notion of feeling. It is not the
feeling we take so seriously as, for instance, when we say, He hurt my feelings'. This is more in
line with emotions which are complexities of concept and consciousness.
Emotions. According to Psychology Today, an emotion “can only ever be felt through the
emotional experiences it gives rise to, even though it might be discovered through its associated
thoughts, beliefs, desires and actions.”
Whether or not we can distinguish between feelings and emotions we can be conscious of how
we feel. All feelings have the function of experiencing the sight, taste, touch, sensation or
mental aspect of an object.
It is the mental factor which feels the object.

As stated in the sutta, there are three basic ways to categorized types of worldly feelings that
arise:
We can say they are Pleasant
We can say they are Painful
We can say they are neither pleasant or unpleasant, otherwise Neutral
All three types of worldly feelings can cause suffering.

Pleasant feelings (either physical or mental) are associated with greed (wanting more
enjoyment and desire).
Unpleasant feelings are associated with aversion, pain, or anything that you want to resist or get
rid of.
Neutral feelings are neither pleasant nor painful. Neutral feelings have the tendency to allow
delusion (confusion) to emerge. This is because the mind may either be ignoring or not aware of
feelings.
You can observe your own feelings (direct insight) and you can compare your feelings with
those of others (inferential understanding).

Part of the Buddhas teaching is not taking for granted anything but exploring. In terms of feeling,
this starts with how we feel.
The Sixfold Base.
Sense organs (internal)
eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind (interacts with other organs)
Sense object (external)
form (sight), sound (ear), odor (nose), flavor (tongue), tangile (body), mind-object (mind).
Buddhist explanation of how this works is that for example with sound.
There is the Ear organ, the sound object and from contact of the two - sound consciousness
arises. This is the same for all of the sixfold bases. The result of this is our being aware of
another feeling, whether it is pleasant, unpleasant or neutral feeling to arise.
It is from this feeling that "craving" arises, clinging and suffering arises.
The Goal is to be aware of this craving and turn it towards deliverance or freedom.
The way the suttas describe the means to "understanding" is to use noting to bring some
awareness to their affect.
So we look at the 6 sense bases to their being pleasant, unpleasant or neutral. Or similarly in
terms of joy, grief and equanimity.
Again the teachings ask us to consider how we look at our experience. We look at these in
terms of someone who is not on a spiritual path and in terms of one who aspires to growing
spiritually.
For a householder , Joy is defined as things that are wished for, desirable, agreeable, gratifying
and associated with worldiness - or when one recalls what was formerly gained or obtained that
has passed, ceased or changed and as a result that joy arises.
For the spiritual seeker, joy is defined as by knowing the impermanance, change, fading away
and cessation of any of the six bases and as a result that joy arises. That joy is called joy based
on renunciation.
Grief based on the household life is defined as the loss of things that are wished for, desirable,
agreeable, gratifying and associated with worldiness- or when one recalls what was formerly

obtained that has passed, ceased and changed and as a result that grief arises.
Grief based on renunciation is further defined as when, by knowing the impermanence, change,
fading away, and cessation of the six-sense bases, one sees it as it actually is with proper
wisdom that these are all impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, one generates a
longing for liberation.
With regards to the Six Kinds of Equanimity (or neutral feelings) - based on the householder life,
it is defined as when one experiences feelings and does not overcome their limitations, nor the
results of their actions, and who is blind to their danger (karma). Such equanimity does not
transcend their experience of the sense bases.
Based on the spiritual aspirant's life, when, by knowing the impermanence, change, fading
away, and cessation of forms (or any other object), one sees as it actually is with proper wisdom
that forms both formerly (those that have changed) and now (in the present) are all
impermanent, suffering, and subject to change, equanimity arises. Such equanimity as this
transcends the form.

It is said that knowing about the sense bases is part of the "soil" of liberating wisdom. Other
components of this "soil" include the aggregates, the faculties, the Four Noble Truths and
Dependent Origination. We will be touching on all of these in the weeks to come.
The basic point introduced here is awareness of whether a particular feeling is related to
progress or regress on the path.
Householder (no motivation to change)
Spiritual Seeker (motivation to seek deeper spiritual understanding)
Accomplished One (realized a higher path: Equanimity)
The Buddha did not categorically reject all pleasant feelings, nor did he categorically
recommend unpleasant experiences for their supposedly purifying effect. Instead, he placed
emphasis on the mental and ethical consequences of all types of feeling.
With the help of the above sixfold classification, this ethical dimension becomes apparent,
uncovering in particular the relation of feelings to the activation of a latent mental tendency
(anusaya) towards lust, irritation, or ignorance. These are synonyms for greed, hatred and
delusion.
This and other discourses point out that, developing understanding and detachment (or
equanimity) in regard to these three feelings has the potential to lead to freedom from dukkha.
This potential is based on the simple method of directing awareness to the very first stages of
the arising of our likes and dislikes, by clearly noting whether the present moment’s experience
is felt as “pleasant”, or “unpleasant”, or neither.
To contemplate feelings means quite literally to know how one feels, and this with such
immediacy that the light of awareness is present before the onset of reactions, projections, or
justifications in regard to how one feels. Undertaken in this way, contemplation of feelings will
reveal the surprising degree to which one’s attitudes and reactions are based on this initial
affective input provided by feelings.

The conditional relation between feelings and our mental tendencies is of central importance,
since by activating these latent tendencies, feelings can lead to the arising of unwholesome
mental reactions. This is one of the ways that Buddhism comes to an understanding of the
foundation of our addictive and destructive tendancies, the other being the twelve links of
dependent co-arising. In that teaching feelings form the condition that can lead to the arising of
craving (taṇhā).
Even our reasoning or thinking can began from our feelings. All that we do depends on our
feelings. Our reaction to any situation depends on how we feel. After reacting to the situation we
may rationalize our reaction. All our emotional reactions depend on how we feel about a
situation. Repeated emotional reactions to feelings gradually become the inclination of our
minds. When emotional reaction becomes a habit we might rationalize our emotional reaction
and defend ourselves way of thinking.
A prominent characteristic of most feelings (but not all) is their ephemeral nature, they are here
and then gone. Sustained contemplation of this ephemeral and impermanent nature of feelings
can then become a powerful tool for developing disenchantment with them.
In many cases, once the conditioned nature of feelings is fully apprehended, detachment,
balance or equanimity arises naturally and our identification with feelings starts to dissolve.
A mindful observer of feelings, by the very fact of observation, no longer fully identifies with
them and thereby begins to move beyond the conditioning and controlling power of the
pleasure–pain dichotomy.
One of my favorite reminders is the saying "If you can see it you cannot be it".
A balanced attitude towards feelings, owing to awareness of their impermanent nature, is
characteristic of the experiences of one who has gone beyond suffering.

HERE ARE A FEW INSTRUCTIONS FROM BHANTE GUNARATANA
Suppose one day you feel very peaceful, joyful and happy. Look at that feeling as it is and try to
pay total attention to it. As long as you feel peaceful, joyful and happy, try to pay total attention
to it and let it fade away when it fades away. Don’t try to make it permanent. If that feeling
disappears, don’t get upset; simply accept the disappearance. Welcome it as it is. By accepting
it you allow yourself to recreate it in your mind at another time. If you worry about its
disappearance you won’t permit it to come back. What you are really doing by accepting the
disappearance of your pleasant feeling is learning to relax and be comfortable with the change
in your own feelings. You cannot force any feeling to stay with you as you wish. It slips away
from your grip. The harder you try to keep it with you the quicker it disappears. If you simply
accept it as it comes and let it go as it goes away, you maintain your equilibrium and this permits
you to relax.

By the same token, if an unpleasant feeling arises in you, don’t try to reject it or push it away
prematurely. It takes time for any feeling to go away. You have to cultivate patience with
unpleasant feelings as well. If you lose your patience with it, you lose the pleasantness that can
follow the unpleasantness, and even magnify it. When you “take it easy”, you make things
simple and more comfortable for yourself. Simply pay total attention to your unpleasant feeling.
You may have certain unpleasant feelings due to a chemical imbalance in your brain. You must
admit that whether you like it or not, things in your body and mind change all the time. If you
experience certain unpleasant sensations due to a change in hormone balance, you may
prolong the imbalance by worrying or by being impatient. If you relax and pay total attention to
the hormone imbalance your mind generates better and more positive hormones to transcend
the imbalanced state.
Inadvertently, you cultivate a certain mental attitude towards numerous things and persons. This
attitude can cause you pleasant or unpleasant feelings. When you mindfully look at your own
state of mind, you will see that it is your own attitude that has created that state of mind which
results in one feeling or another. Feeling does not come from the object that you perceive but
from your own state or mental attitude. This is why when several people look at the same object
they can have several different feelings, several different opinions about the object.
If you mindfully watch your own mind and feelings, you can see very clearly and unequivocally
that what you feel is your own creation and that you are totally responsible for it. Mindfully
watching the continuous change of your own feelings can make you abstain from emotional
reactions and make you see the truth of your own feelings. Mindfulness of feelings will not
cause you to think obsessive thoughts or abusive thoughts or harmful thoughts. By unmindful
thinking you abuse your mind. The abused mind always generates abusive feelings, which
always is painful.

Additional Instructions as it be: if one experiences a pleasant feeling, one should develop

This is how we might be mindful extermally.
In summary, each mode of vedana is accompanied by a corresponding underlying tendency or
obsession, pleasant for lust, unpleasant for aversion, and for eight pleasant nor unpleasant
toward ignorance or delusion. Meditating with concentration on vedana and lead to deep
mindfulness and clear comprehension. With this one can experience directly within oneself the
reality of impermanence and the nature of attachment. This, in turn can lead ultimately to
liberation of the mind. Even if we do not achieve this in this lifetime it is of great benefit.
