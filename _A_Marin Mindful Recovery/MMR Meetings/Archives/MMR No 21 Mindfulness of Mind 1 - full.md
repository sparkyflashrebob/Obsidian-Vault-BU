(CONTEMPLATION OF MIND)
34. “And how, bhikkhus, does a bhikkhu abide contemplating mind as mind? Here a bhikkhu
understands mind affected by lust as mind affected by lust, and mind unaffected by lust as mind
unaffected by lust. He understands mind affected by hate as mind affected by hate, and mind
unaffected by hate as mind unaffected by hate. He understands mind affected by delusion as
mind affected by delusion, and mind unaffected by delusion as mind unaffected by delusion. He
understands contracted mind as contracted mind, and distracted mind as distracted mind. He
understands exalted mind as exalted mind, and unexalted mind as unexalted mind. He
understands surpassed mind as surpassed mind, and unsurpassed mind as unsurpassed mind.
He understands concentrated mind as concentrated mind, and unconcentrated mind as
unconcentrated mind. He understands liberated mind as liberated mind, and unliberated mind
as unliberated mind.
There are three words for "mind" in Buddhism that overlap but are used distinctly.
#1. Is "means" which indicates the general thinking faculty. It is associated with volition, intention
or willfulness. Willing is defined as deliberate thinking. Undeliberate though is thought to be an
expression of latent tendencies (anusaya) (past volition). You might call this as the habitual
mind.
The 2nd "vinnana" is known as the mental force that animates an otherwise inert material body.
Otherwise known as consciousness. In Buddhism there are 3 types of consciousness, A) those
that derive from our senses, B) one of the 5 aggregates, form, feeling, perception,
consciousness and volitional formations or fabrications, and C))one of the twelve causes of
Dependant Origination which provide for the buddhist notions of karma, rebirth and release.
The 3rd "citta" is what is being referred to in this sutta refers to our state of mind or mindset. It is

neither an entity or a process.
Citta is considered to be referred to as "heart-mind" because it is a consciousness of both
thoughts and emotions.
It is the "citta" mind that is liberated at enlightenment.
"In this list of mind states, the Buddha emphasizes knowing the presence or absence of the
three unwholesome roots of mind and how they color or condition our minds. These three roots
are GREED or lust; HATRED, which includes ill will and anger; and DELUSION or ignorance,
which encompasses bewilderment and confusion."
The sutta emphasizes not only knowing the presence of these unwholesome states of mind or
their ABSENCE but also it is also beneficial to note when the mind is free from greed, hared or
delusion.
Another way of saying this is it is also beneficial to note when the mind is not positively inclined
but that we can still recognize when the mind is at ease, peaceful and open.
Since consciousness itself, in its own nature, is the bare knowing or cognizing of an object,
(meaning it is not colored by what it is perceiving) the quality of any state of mind is determined
by its associated mental factors, such as lust, hate, and delusion or their opposites, as
mentioned by the sutta.

What Is Skillful and What Is Not
By focusing this section on the recognition and knowing of unwholesome roots and the
wholesome ones in their absence - what is emphasized again is wise discernment of what is
skillful and what is unskillful. This just like what we discussed with feelings.
As we have already noted, the Buddha did not categorically reject all pleasant feelings, nor did
he categorically recommend unpleasant experiences for their supposedly purifying effect.
Instead, he placed emphasis on the mental and ethical consequences of all types of feeling.
Householder (no motivation to change)
Spiritual Seeker (motivation to seek deeper spiritual understanding)
Accomplished One (realized a higher path: Equanimity)
Likewise with the mind. Knowing whether what is present is beneficial or not beneficial.
This discerning is the knowing of what leads to happiness and liberation or to what leads to
suffering. This is basic knowledge in Buddhism but in Western Culture it can be a delicate
process.
For many people, it is easy to recognize a mind state like greed or hatred and knowing it being
unwholesome. But then identifying with the mind state as who we are, which leads to more
self-judgment, more aversion, and more suffering.
Again pointing to this idea of Sakkaya Ditthi.
Again the two darts or arrows come into play. The first being the event in itself, the Eight Worldly
winds, and then the second, which is our taking the dart out and stabbing ourselves with it.
It is important to understand which mind states are skillful or unskillful, not to judge ourselves,
but to see which lead to happiness and be cultivated in our lives, or, on the other hand, see

which lead to suffering and should be abandoned.
These states not only arise in our minds but they also are what motivates our actions.
"Whatever we think or ponder upon will be the inclination of our minds".
It is said that the Dalai Lama emphasized the importance of measuring our actions not by their
success or failure but by the motivations behind them. We cannot often control outcomes but we
can train our minds and hearts. This training is the 3rd foundation of mindfulness - Mindfulness
of Mind.
We can learn and improve our actions by being perceptive, attentive and by setting intentions.
This is the training we put into practice. By knowing ourselves whether a lustful mind is lustful or
a mind without lust, an angry mind is with or without anger, or a confused mind as confused or
clear.
With the practice of mindfulness, we see directly as we experience these mind states. These are
not abstract concepts or principle we are using, it is our direct experience. We feel the
excitability of wanting or greed - the tightness, contraction and alienation of anger and hatred.
We might feel the confused entanglement of delusion. These have also been described as
"pulling-in, pushing away, and running around in circles".
In meditation we might ask the question, "What's the attitude in the mind right now?"
This can shine the light on whether the mind is holding on or wanting some other state of mind
to occur and is a direct application of mindfulness of mind.
It can be more difficult to know when these states are absent. It is easier in some ways to see
the difficulties and overlook wholesome states.
We are being asked to be equally mindful of each.
One suggestion to play with this is when you notice the wanting mind to pay attention to how it
plays out in the body and to notice when the wanting disappears. Before rushing back to the
breath - pay attention to a mind free of wanting, experiencing the coolness and peace of that
state.
This clear comprehension can become the framework for experiencing and knowing a deeper
experience of the mind -- itself free of any words or concepts. This is actually why we
emphasize is the Knower, the observer who knows what is present or not. This is mindfulness
being fully immersed in the object and being known. This is what mindfulness is all about,
knowing the body, the breath, knowing feelings and the mind itself.
This knowing if wholesome roots of mind are present or not is something that is something that
is essential for our freedom. The defilements, called kileasas are not always present. This
should be emphasized. DEFILEMENTS are not inherent or innate to the mind - It is not the
nature of the mind itself. They are visitors who come by when the conditions are right.
Instead, as we realize this, we can free ourselves from unnecessary suffering
and to find a place of Inner refuge, for healing and for comfort.
Tenzin Wangyal says," The moment you feel the unlimited spaciousness of your own mind, you
feel incredible protection."

Instead of drowning in our defilements through identification with them, or judging them or
denying them, the Buddha reminds us to simply be mindful of when they are present or not.
Although this is simple it is not always easy to do. Again, this is why we call it practice. There
will be times when we do not see, are not mindful, are caught in our view, beliefs, ideas, and
judgements. But, with this tool of mindfulness, we can awaken - to snap out of our clinging. At
some point we can begin to see that difficult mind-states are themselves not a problem or a
mistake. They are simply a part of the path, impermanent, selfless, just like everything else.
They become no big deal thru wisdom. The ability to see and be aware of our defilements is the
first step. It is better to see them and explore them than not - and simply act them out.
For me the key is to see the suffering in them.
