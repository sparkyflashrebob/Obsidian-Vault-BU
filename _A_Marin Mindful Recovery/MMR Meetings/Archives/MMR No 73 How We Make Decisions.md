● Reading a book “Why Buddhism is True” by Robert Wright.
○ Evolutionary Psychology. Evolutionary psychology which is a theoretical
approach to psychology that attempts to explain useful mental and
psychological traits—such as memory, perception, or language—as
adaptations, i.e., see them as the functional products of natural selection.
○ Understanding that Natural Selection “cares” only about one thing - getting genes
into the next generation.
○ Sees that Buddhism in many ways is in alignment with natural selection - by
science and philosophy.
○ Not in the “supernatural” or metaphysical part of buddhism - such as
reincarnation, but more with the naturalistic parts.
○ In that -- if we ask the question“What kinds of perceptions and thoughts and
feelings guide us through life each day?” the answer, at the most basic level, isn’t
“The kinds of thoughts and feelings and perceptions that give us an accurate
picture of reality.” But is “The kinds of thoughts and feelings and perceptions that
helped our ancestors get genes into the next generation.” Whether those
thoughts and feelings and perceptions give us a true view of reality is beside the
point. As a result, they sometimes don’t. Our brains are designed to, among other
things, delude us.
○ As a result - human beings are hard-wired to be perpetually dissatisfied.
○ He says "We are condemned to always want things to be a little different,
always want a little more."
○ In Why Buddhism Is True, he suggests that Buddhist practices can rewire
the brain to overcome a host of anxieties and emotional pain that afflict so
many people.
○ "I think of mindfulness meditation as almost a rebellion against natural
selection," says Wright. "And Buddhism says, 'We don't have to play this
game.'"

Yongey Mingyur Rinpoche, a meditation teacher in the Tibetan Buddhist tradition, has
said, “Ultimately, happiness comes down to choosing between the discomfort of
becoming aware of your mental afflictions and the discomfort of being ruled by them.”

you from realizing true happiness, you have to first become aware of them, which can be
unpleasant.
