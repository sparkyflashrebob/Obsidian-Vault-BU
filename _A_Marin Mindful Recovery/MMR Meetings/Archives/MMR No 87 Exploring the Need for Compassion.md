MMR#83 Exploration of Compassion
Last week I brought up the idea that, in Buddhism, the mind and heart are primarily spoken of
together as one word - the pali word “citta” which emphasises the emotive side of the mind. This
is opposed that which refers to the mind in terms of the intellect in the sense of what grasps
mental objects. ‘Citta’ primarily represents one’s mindset or state of mind. Taking this one step
further is another word in buddhism - the pali word “bodhi-citta” which is the awakening of this
heart-mind. This is the goal of our practice.
The Dalai Lama, when he was in DC some years back, said, “I don't know why people like me
so much. It must be because I care about bodhi-citta. I can't claim to practice all the time, but I
care about it.”
That’s a wonderful way that he put that, that it's that — we care about caring. — We know our
hearts aren't always wide open and tender, and we're often caught in all sorts of more
small-minded places, right? But something in us cares about caring.
Tara Brach tells a story about a prisoner in San Quentin who was also a meditator. This guy
was in the prison yard one day and he stopped another prisoner from throwing rocks at a
seagull, which is apparently a real no-no in prison etiquette - telling the man that “that bird has
my wings". That he was connected to that bird.
And that is how it is - whenever we pay close attention to another life - when we get to know it -
that being begins to feel like a part of us. We begin to care about them. It’s why we don’t feed
the animals or why I don’t like to window shop. We become attached. We become connected to
everything.

Yet most of the time we act as if in a Trance - in our conditioning and habits, preoccupations,
and judgments. We can feel threatened. So, in order to protect ourselves, we distance
ourselves from them. We depersonalize them. Tara calls this making them “unreal others”.
There are many ways we protect ourselves. We can be desensitized by the endless news of
human suffering, natural disasters, refugees being rejected, drug overdoses, suicide,
health-related disease, and deaths. This is painful – so we turn away, distract ourselves, or
become numb to it.
We can also become caught in the daily stress and reactivity of our lives, and, as a result, even
those we love and cherish can become an “unreal other”. We can block our basic sense of
goodness, our hearts can close to defend our vulnerability. We can become numb to their hurts
and fears. We can lose our ability to respond with warmth and tenderness.

Yet, even with all of this, there is this potential to bring the spirit of compassion and
connectedness to all beings. To turn our heart-mind towards compassion for all beings.
This “Unreal” conditioning, that is prevalent in the world, has come about due to how we have
evolved. For millions of years, we found it to be safer in groups. To become close-knit
communities as a means of protection. Those not in your circle were seen as enemies to be
hated and/or feared as they threatened the survival of “the people” who were close to us - those
whom we relied upon for survival.
As we evolved, at least at one point, we made a dramatic leap, in language, in how we
communicate, and in how we collaborate. We developed the capacity for mindfulness and
reason, empathy and compassion – allowing for calming and regulating the reactions of our
survival brain.
Despite this, in the most primitive parts of our brain, the Unreal Other has persisted - threatening
our survival as a species.
And yet, there are other aspects of our development that continue to evolve and give us the
tools to undo this - the ability to recognize our unconscious biases - those ways of thinking that
are below the line. The biases - more specifically “Implicit Bias” is a term for the unconscious or
semiconscious ways we stereotype individuals or groups - depending on our social conditioning.
As we bring these biases into conscious we are more able to respond from a wakeful and
inclusive heart.
There is a wonderful mindfulness exercise where two people are asked to sit directly across
from each other and to look in the other eyes of the other person. Frequently the result of this is,
as we look into each other’s eyes, we cannot help but come to care for the other - they become
real to us.
If not, we find, that as we look into the eyes of others and instead feel judgment coming up
within us- then this mindful non-judging awareness that we use as our practice can offer us a
glimpse into how these thoughts arise within us. This mindful awareness during investigation
reveals the vulnerability that naturally arises in us. Eventually we begin to start to see them as
just thoughts. This clears the way – allowing us to investigate our experience more deeply and
loosen our judgments.
Compassion means to suffer together. It is a feeling that arises when we encounter suffering
and we feel the motivation to relieve it. We can be aware our own suffering and we can be
aware of this in others. As we get to understand the suffering in us we can begin to have
empathy for that suffering in others. Empathy is the ability to take the perspective of another and
to feel the emotions another is feeling.

So during our contemplation of the feelings of others if we can ask ourselves “Where does it
hurt?” or more broadly, “What’s it like being you?” this quiets our reactive survival brain and
awakens empathy, understanding, and care.
The Dalai Lama says that he believes “that every human being has an innate desire for
happiness and does not want to suffer. That the very purpose of life is to experience this
happiness. I believe that each of us has the same potential to develop inner peace and thereby
achieve happiness and joy.”
If we have a positive mental attitude, then even when surrounded by hostility, we will not lack
inner peace. On the other hand, if our mental attitude is more negative, influenced by fear,
suspicion, helplessness, or self-loathing, then even when surrounded by our best friends, in a
nice atmosphere, and in comfortable surroundings, we will not be happy. So, mental attitude is
very important: it makes a real difference to our state of happiness.
It is unrealistic to think that something positive can come about merely from something external.
Our material situation is important and helpful to us, and it is also true that our inner, mental
attitudes are equally important.
Since our very existence and well-being are a result of the cooperation and contributions of
countless others, we must develop a proper attitude about the way we relate to them. We often
tend to forget this basic fact. Today, in our modern global economy, national boundaries are
irrelevant. Not only do countries depend upon one another, but so do continents. We are heavily
interdependent. When we look closely at the many problems facing humanity today, we can see
that they have been created by us. We are not talking of natural disasters. However, conflicts,
bloodshed, problems arising out of nationalism and national boundaries, are all man-made.
Yet the truth of the matter is today, in a sense the concept of “us” and “them” is almost no longer
relevant, as our neighbors’ interests are ours as well. Caring for our neighbors’ interests is
essentially caring for our own future. Today the reality is simple. In harming our enemy, we are
harmed.”

Love is often hard work, and when we’re in conflict, we can regress into our survival brain in a
flash. We can feel that being honest with ourselves, we are pretty much caught up in our own
problems - that we just don’t seem to have much authentic caring for others. Having
compassion for others can be seen as widening the circle of inclusion. Compassion for our own
life is at the center of this. We can trust that this quality of caring is intrinsic to who we are.
It’s important to remember and realize that, especially when we’re stressed, our habits of Unreal
Othering apply even to those closest to us—our brother, child, mother, friend, and work
colleague. We are trying to “get through the day,” and they are making things worse or better or
playing bit parts, irrelevant to our concerns. We’re just not there; our hearts are closed.

If we want to relate more reliably with presence and compassion, we need to bring the habitual
trance of Unreal Other above the line. Our starting place is to pay closer attention to our
everyday encounters. This means being mindful as a start. Remember, mindfulness is the
non-judging awareness that is always there to some degree and it gives rise to compassion.
The path to our liberation is through this awareness.
Usually, when we face our problems, we look at them from our own point of view and may even
deliberately ignore other aspects of the situation. It is important for us to have a broader
perspective.
It is easy for us to come from the perspective of thought, to be identified with them, and being in
our heads - thinking how things are up in there as being the way it is. It can be helpful in many
ways to drop down and center ourselves on the area of the heart and the solar plexus, in the
torso. Be mindful of the body in this way. Taking the energy away from the head area - away
from thinking.
Getting in touch with feeling is very much different than with our thoughts. We cling to our
thoughts because identify them as who we are. When we can get the sense that once we have
an idea of what is going on that we agree with - that we have it figured out - that that is reality.
We can have seemingly rational thoughts and altruistic views and ideals and all that. One can
get very inspired with them. We can get quite high over our thoughts and beliefs and ideas. We
can get attached to them.
But when we have highs we also have lows, we can become bored or depressed. We can
become discontented, threatened.
So we bring our attention to the way that life feels - to this direct knowing. When you bring our
attention to the way that life feels - versus thinking about it - you are no longer interpreting that
the feeling is personal - making it and judging it from the perspective of thought. Not coming
from the position of the way it should be but from the way it is.
Using this Mindfulness of the body and the way things feel. This demands honesty and the
willingness to accept the way things are even if it is not in alignment with our ideals or the way
we want things to be. This will allow us to develop patience and compassion for ourselves.
There is no way that you can make feelings other than what they are.
When you are able to accept feelings as they are then you are no longer a victim of desire.
When you can accept the feelings of - say fear - we can let fear go - these fearful feelings can
cease. The same with pain. That is because you are willing to accept them. Even if they don’t
cease - we learn from them - get to understand them – become willing to be patient with them -
willing to forbear.

And if not, compassion for our own suffering can be another avenue to help us with this.
Understanding feeling in ourselves also helps us to relate to others because when we recognize
the suffering in ourselves we also can recognize that in others. This recognition of suffering
elicits the feeling of empathy and compassion in us. Compassion feels good. It is in our biology
to evolve to be more compassionate.
It is just simple attention that allows us to truly listen to the sound of the bird, to see
deeply the glory of an autumn leaf, to touch the heart of another and be touched.
This is the power of our practice - to be mindful. It is not a practice in thought. It is something
that has to be experienced - to be felt - to be known. We learn to pay attention to our actual
experience, instead of our interpretations of it.
When we plant and water the seeds of compassion in us, then this frees us to be with the
experience of others in a different way. To allow our caring that we develop within us to expand
into wider and wider circles of inclusion.
