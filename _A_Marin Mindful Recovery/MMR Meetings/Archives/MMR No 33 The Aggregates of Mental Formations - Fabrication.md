One of my principal teachers, Thanissaro Bhikkhu starts out one of his dhamma talks that he gave a few years back by stating that “The mind has a basic habit, which is to create things. In fact, when the Buddha describes causality, how experiences come about, he says that the power of creation or sankhara—the mental tendency to put things together—actually comes prior to our sensory experience. It is because the mind is active, actively putting things together, that it knows things.” This is what the mind does.

Many of his talks go something similar to this:
He asks us to place our awareness and Minds on the Breath - Everything is happening here so he recommends that we be as still as possible so you catch site of all these important things happening. Most of the time we just see the breath goes in and the breath goes out. We are on automatic pilot and so we miss the great number of details that are currently out of our awareness. But when we meditate - we stop to look at the process in detail.

When you have the mind on the breath you have all 4 foundations here. Body, feeling,
mind-state and MENTAL QUALITIES. We are now looking at these mental qualities specifically here in the 4th Satipattanna, which looks at mental qualities, such as the hinderances, the aggregates, the sense spheres, the seven factors of awakening, and the 4 noble truths.

What we are watching for are how the mind shapes things. If you want to look for ignorance you do not have to look very far. Look at the fabrications of the mind.
The Principle of Dependant Co-arising says fabrications are conditioned by ignorance. It is right here where the mind fashions things. Fashioning body sensations and mental events. From Fabrication comes consciousness, and consciousness leads to name and form: physical and mental phenomena. The other Aggregates.

This fashioning or fabrication is the 4th of the Aggregates that I introduced here last week and that we today explore. This buddhist concept of Saṅkhāra (Pali; सङ्खार; Sanskrit: सस्ं कार or saṃskāra) it is a term that figures prominently in Buddhism. The word means 'formations'[1] or 'that which has been put together' and 'that which puts together'. 

It is used in two senses in the Present. In the first (passive) sense, saṅkhāra refers to
conditioned phenomena generally but specifically to all mental "dispositions". These are called 'volitional formations' both because they are formed as a result of volition and because they are causes for the arising of future volitional actions.

English translations for saṅkhāra in the first sense of the word include 'conditioned things,'' determinations,' 'fabrications' and 'formations' (or, particularly when referring to mental processes, 'volitional formations').

In the second (active) sense of the word, saṅkhāra refers to karma (sankhara-khandha) that which leads to conditioned arising, dependent origination.

Although we give it definition here, actually, the pali word “Saṅkhāra” is a complex concept, with no single-word English translation.

It fuses "object and subject" as interdependent parts of each human's consciousness and the philosophical study of the nature, origin, and limits of human knowledge. It implies "our impressions, dispositions, conditioning, forming, perfecting in one's mind, influencing one's sensory and conceptual faculty" as well as any "preparation" that "impresses, disposes, influences or conditions" how one thinks, conceives or feels.

### Conditioned things

As I mentioned, in the first (passive) sense, saṅkhāra refers to "conditioned things" or
"dispositions” or “mental imprints". Early Buddhist texts state that all aggregates in the world – physical or mental concomitants, and all phenomena are conditioned things. It can refer to any compound form in the universe whether a tree, a cloud, a human being, a thought, or a molecule. All these are saṅkhāras, as well as everything that is physical and visible in the phenomenal world. They are conditioned things, or aggregates of mental conditions.

The Buddha taught that all saṅkhāras are impermanent and essenceless.
Why? Since conditioned things and dispositions are perceptions and do not have real essence, they are not reliable sources of pleasure.  And they are impermanent. Understanding the significance of this reality is wisdom. This "conditioned things" sense of the word Saṅkhāra appears in the Four Noble Truths that we talk about in the coming weeks and in the Buddhist theory of dependent origination, that is how ignorance or misconceptions about impermanence and non-self leads to Taṇhā (craving) and rebirths.

### Sankhara-khandha[edit]

[[Dependent Origination.png]]
[[The Five Aggregates.png]]

In the second (or active) sense, saṅkhāra (or saṅkhāra-khandha) refers to the form-creating faculty of mind. It is part of the doctrine of conditioned arising or dependent origination. In this sense, the term Sankhara is karmically active volition or intention, which generates rebirth and influences the realm of rebirth (how we are reborn). Sankhara, here, is synonymous with karma, and includes actions of the body, speech and mind.

In terms of rebirth and karma, I would like again to recall how I believe it is helpful to view karma and rebirth apply to our lives - to view them here in the present. This process of karma and rebirth are happening in each and every moment .The most important aspect of this process is that it unfolds and is experienced in the here and now. To be honest though, this is not an Orthodox view. We can view rebirth and karma both in terms of the present and in terms of past and future.

The saṅkhāra-khandha, or “aggregate” that we are exploring, says that living beings are reborn (this word in Pali called “bhava”, or becoming) by means of actions of body and speech (kamma). 

In this process of Dependant Origination, the Buddha states that all volitional constructs are conditioned by ignorance (avijja) of impermanence and non-self. 

It is this ignorance that leads to the origination of the sankharas/aggregates and ultimately causes human suffering. 

There is this propensity for MInd of not knowing what is going on yet at the same time has this task of creation.

The cessation (dissolution, disappearance, or ending) of all such sankharas
(sabba-saṅkhāra-nirodha) is synonymous with both Awakening (bodhi), the attainment of the unconditioned phenomenon of nirvana which is the end of conditioned arising or dependent origination in the karmic sense.

As the ignorance conditions the volitional formations, these formations condition, in turn, the consciousness (viññāna). 
The Buddha elaborated:
'What one intends, what one arranges, and what one obsesses about: This is a support for the stationing of consciousness. There being a support, there is a landing [or: an establishing] of consciousness. When that consciousness lands and grows, there is the production of renewed becoming in the future. When there is the production of renewed becoming in the future, there is future birth, aging & death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, & despair. Such is the origination
of this entire mass of suffering & stress.' 

This is what is know as Dependant Origination or Dependant Co-Arising.

I believe that this description by the Buddha makes the process somewhat understandable as a causal process.

Thanissaro Bhikkhu points out that “ the problem is that most of (of the Minds) actions, most of its creations, come out of ignorance, so the kind of knowledge that comes from those creations can be misleading.” He suggests that “for this reason, what you want to do in the process of meditation is to back up, to get down as close to this process of creation as you can, to see if there’s a way to do it skillfully that leads to knowledge, that leads you to a point that breaks through ignorance. And this means, instead of building up a lot of things, you let things fall apart so you can get down to exactly where these basic forces in the mind are putting things together.”

That is what this whole process, this path, as we call it, is designed to help us to accomplish. It is that still forest pond that is aware of everything that touches it. The One who knows.

Thanissaro Bhikkhu further clarifies how these sankhara’s operate. There are three forms of fabrication; “kaya-sankhara” or bodily fabrication; “vaci-sankhara” or verbal fabrication, and “citta-sankhara” or mental fabrication.

When I mentioned when we began, when we think of the breath, all of the basic fabrications can be seen right there in their most elemental form. The breath is what puts life together in thebody. Without breath we would die pretty quickly. That is Bodily fabrication.

A few weeks ago when talking about deepening meditation I introduced us to two words, vitaka and vichara, which together mean directed thought and evaluation. This is verbal fabrication, our process of talking internally to ourselves. You direct your thoughts to the breath and then evaluate it. 

His simple teaching on this is to ask ourselves. “How does the breath feel? Does it
feel good? If it does, stay with it. If it doesn’t feel good, can you change it? This is about the most basic level of conversation you can have with yourself: “Does this feel good or not? Comfortable or not? Yes. No.”

So when we are directing our thoughts and evaluating - what provides us with feedback? It is through feeling and perception, two other sankharas. So now what is in play is mental fabrication.

Feeling provides the raw data of whether it is pleasant, unpleasant or neutral, and perception provides us with the label “this is pleasant” or “this is painful”.

When you’ve got the mind with the breath, you’ve got all of these things brought together: the feelings that come with the breath, the perceptions that label the breath: “Now the breath is coming in. Now the breath is going out. Now the mind is like this. Now it’s like that.” 
The directed thought and the evaluation are there as well, keeping you focused on the breath and on evaluating the breath.

This is why the teachings use the breath as an object of meditation. All three aspects of
fabrication are here for us to observe. 

So what do we do when we stray away from our object of meditation. If you stray away into distraction, into another train of experience in which you lose your frame of reference, this is what is called becoming, bhava. This is the creation of another world.

Mindfulness is like being a sentry at the gate of your awareness. When you are aware of the breath and aspects of the breath you let that come in.

This is the most basic idea of the teachings, our training, and our practice. To notice that we have gone into another world, followed the cloud as it passed by, or jumped on the train of thoughts or feelings or moods or emotions - we simply keep coming back to our object of meditation.

Mindfulness is primarily this aspect of remembering, remembering what you are doing, and remembering the teachings so you can draw upon their wisdom to bring you back into present awareness. We want to recognize what pulls us into these others worlds so that we teach ourselves so we can awaken.

In order to bring us back we have some other options too. At this point something interesting can happen. We can skillfully “fabricate a fabrication”.

Using Vitakka and vichara, directed thought and evaluation, and mindfulness, we can simply realize we left our object of mediation, or frame of reference, and come back. Most of the time this works. Other times is might not be that easy.

As we learned in our discussion on the removal of distracting thoughts we can:
- Actively reflection on the drawbacks of that other world - especially if it has lust,
aversion, delusion or is harming
- Other times you can simply ignore the distraction - this happens often when I am not
retreat and I notice that I am replaying a song in my head over and over. You can just
leave it be. This is also effective for staying on the breath in general. Notice that this is
an active process of knowing and letting go.
- The 4th way of pulling yourself back is to notice that when there is a process of creation, an element of tension goes with them. For me, this is noticing suffering at its mostfundamental. What I do when I am aware of this - is to relax. It will pass away as long as I do not feed it. This is the two wolves of the mind - which one and I feeding.
- There is another way, that I have mentioned before, that I really do not recommend
because it means to crush distraction with our force of will. Mind crushing mind.
- There are other skillful ways to use our memory and imagination to bring us back to the present that we can use to explore. Maybe those will come up in our discussion.

However, one way to stay connected, to stay with our object of reference, is to create levels of concentration in the mind. Give it something to do. This is what we are doing when we stay with our frame of reference. 

This idea of fabricating a fabrication. Fabrication of the path. Use our form, our breath, to make it attractive. You make if feel real good. Feeling. When you do this you create a sense of pleasure that more attractive, gets more intense and becomes more
solid. 

Actually the act of sitting here breathing is really refreshing, we use it for healing, and energy and vitality. It feels good right here. This sense of pleasure is rapture. This interest can build. This is a good form of desire. This is a good use of fabrication.

Instead of pleasure, you might experience pain. Again we can take what is and put it to good use. In the 4 Noble Truths the Buddha states that the duty of seeing suffering is to understand it. 

When we get the mind solid and stable enough, through our practice, it can get to a point where it is not so threatened by pain. You might even notice the proliferation of thoughts and feelings related to your experience of pain. 

If you identify with them then you risk going into other worlds of becoming. So you learn to let them go.

I have had various experiences with pain and have explored them in many ways. On retreat the pain for me gets unbearable most of the time. As I stay with it though, I am aware of its impermanent and changing nature. Likewise with my attitudes and views.
Directed thought and evaluation is stressful. Pleasure likewise is stressful. Actually any “action” is stressful. 

As we meditate, these conditioned things can fall away until we reach a state of
equanimity. That has neither pleasure nor pain. This is where the Buddha said he went in meditation, his place of rest. He called it a pleasant abiding in the here and now.

However all of these aspects, even equanimity, even the sense of space, up to the level of nothingness - all of these are perception attainments.

These have an element of stress. Thanisarro Bhikkhu says that “The path is a process of taking things that are stressful—these perceptions, these feelings, these processes of creation—and using them in a skillful way. 

So you use fabrication to undo fabrication and then finally reach a point where everything opens up to the unfabricated.”

So here is this teaching on Fabrication in General. There are other elements of Fabrication and the path that we will be discussing, especially when we get to the Noble Eightfold Path, which is the path that leads to the end of suffering. So we will have opportunities to understand aspects of Fabrication in other meaningful ways.
