MMR#132 Equanimity and Compassion
The origins of Equanimity or equipoise, the pali wordupek(k)hā, suggests a mental attitude of “looking upon” or “to look over”, and not an indifferent “looking away”. 
The term thus conveys an awareness of whatever is happening combined with mental balance and the absence of favoring or opposing.
It arises from the power of observation and the ability to see without being caught by what we see.
As a result, when well-developed, such power gives rise to a great sense of peace.
It can also refer to the ease that comes from seeing the big picture.
In the Indian culture upekkha is sometimes used to mean ‘to see with patience”.
We might see this as “seeing with understanding”
Another take on equanimity is to “stand in the middle of all this”.  A balanced strength or stability in the face of whatever is happening.
The strong presence of inner calm, well-being, confidence, vitality or integrity that can keep us upright, like the ballast that keeps a ship upright in strong winds.
It is a form of protection from the “eight worldly winds” 
praise and blame, 
success and failure, 
pleasure and pain, 
fame and disrepute. 
Becoming attached to or excessively elated with success, praise, fame or pleasure can be a set-up for suffering when the winds of life change direction.

There are a few ways of Transitioning from Compassion to Equanimity that the Buddha practiced
In 1, he was confronted by the refusal of a Monk to take only 1 meal per day
In the deliberations the Buddha compromised with the Monk (I assume that the could have two meals a day)
But the Monk further declined to follow the rule
So in this case the Buddha remained equanimous
Apparently and eventually the Monk came to realize the inappropriateness of his behavior and followed the rules that the Buddha put forth
In another the Buddha approached a group of monks quarreling
He attempted to engage in compassionate intervention
As was the case with the Monk above, they did not stop
So the Buddha remained equanimous and took his robes and bowl and went off on his own
 So these are cases where his attitude shifted from compassion to equanimity
With Equanimity as a Divine Abode
This shows that equanimity at times can be the appropriate attitude on its own

The idea of Compassion leading to equanimity does not need to be attached to it bearing fruit and being successful

So just a couple of Comments and Reminders About Compassion and other Divine Abodes
Basic thrust of compassion is the wish for others to be free from affliction
This generally means One is often concerned about those who are less well of than oneself
There is the Potential pitfall of compassion
To perceive others to be in a lower and inferior position
So this Requires counter-balancing this to avoid it becoming a habit
As it could give rise to conceit and a feeling of superiority
In order to protect against this - we will want to cultivate other brahmaviharas
Metta
The basic attitude of kindness and well-wishing
Treating everyone equally as one's friend
This too can function as the appropriate attitude when one is faced with various forms of aggression
And then there is the ability of sympathetic joy to complement the Cultivation of Compassion
Sympathetic Joy ensures that, after what is negative has received due attention, what is positive will equally be taken into account.
With Equanimity, a further complement comes into play with the other brahmaviharas
The orientation continues but there is no need to be active in reaching out at all times - It doesn't need to  be turned into a task
Equanimity rounds off compassion by freeing it from an trace of compulsion and obsession.
Equanimity completes the 4 divine abodes
The Brahmaviharas themselves offer those of us on the path a number of choices for the appropriate mental attitude for any situation
They are mentioned frequently in the early discourses and are considered of upmost importance for the systematic cultivation of the mind
They were also a regular form of practice, in those early times, due to the benefits that they provide
So what we find in early Buddhist thought is that compassion or metta by way of body, speech, and mind was its foundation
It played out in many ways, from kindness in daily life situations to facing the most dramatic forms of aggression.
Based on metta, compassion in turn finds its complement in sympathetic joy and equanimity.
The practice is actually cumulative.
By taking all of the brahmaviharas to heart we enrich our actual practice and avoid the possible pitfalls that could result from taking on compassion without the support of the others.
