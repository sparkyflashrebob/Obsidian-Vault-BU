So far, in our exploration of the Satipatthana Sutta, we have covered: the First Foundation of
Mindfulness, being Mindful of the Body. We understand being Mindful of the body, the body
breathing, being aware of the full body, the position of the body (sitting, standing, & lying down), the
activities of the body (walking, eating, and such), the composition of the body (the elements of
solidity, fluidity, heat and wind), contemplation of the passing away of the body,, and full awareness
of the body. We are asked to notice the body both internally and externally, and to notice the
impermanence of the body conditions.
Likewise with the 2nd Satipatthana, being Mindful of Feeling, we are aware of sensations in the body
and mind, whether they be pleasant feelings or unpleasant feelings or aware of neither pleasant nor
unpleasant feelings. This tendency for us to want or crave pleasant feelings, to want to push away
or get rid of unpleasant feelings, and being both ambivalent about neither pleasant nor unpleasant
feelings or, in the case of us addicts or alcoholics, who are used to having one feeling or another,
find non-feeling to be unpleasant in general. We are also reminded to reflect on impermanence of
feelings and on the feelings of others.
In the 3rd Satipatthana we were asked to be mindful of our state of mind as a whole, primarily where
it comes to greed, hatred and delusion. The word for mind being Citta. As is the case with all of
these, being mindful meaning this knowing quality of whether these states of mind were present or
not. We were also to include the knowing of other types of mind states such as Contracted Mind,
Exalted Mind, Samadhi Mind or a Mind that is Released. Here we are also reminded to reflect on
impermanence of mind states and on the mind states both internally and externally - the mind state
of others.
In the 4th Satipatthana we are looking at Mental Factors, also known as Dhammas, that have the
ability to color the mind or to apprehend a particular quality of an object. These can be considered
mental states, or mental events. Here again we are asked to be aware of, be mindful of when these
factors are present or not. We looked at the Five Hindrances; sense-desire (kamachanda) where
one latches onto thoughts based on the pleasure of the five senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, and
touch); ill-will (vyapada) - latching onto thoughts and feeling based on anger, resentment or similar;
sloth and torpor (Thina-middha) which is a dull state of mind, where one lacks energy or motivation,
when we are opposed to wholesome activity; restlessness and worry (uddhacca-kukkucca) where
the mind is in a state of agitation and is unable to settle down; and Doubt (vicikiccha) which is doubt
in our ability to understand and implement the teachings, or Buddha or the teacher.
In this 4th Satipatthana, we are currently looking at the second series of mental factors known as the
Khandas or Aggregates. Also known as the Five Aggregates of clinging. These being Form,
Feeling, Perception, Mental Formations and Consciousness. Together these are thought of as the
five material and mental factors that take part in the rise of craving and clinging. They are also
explained as the five processes that we think of in terms of our person or personality. In the
Theravada tradition, suffering arises when one identifies with or clings to the aggregates and
suffering is extinguished by relinquishing our attachments to the aggregates. In the Mahayana
tradition the emphasis is that the nature of all aggregates is that they are intrinsically empty of
independent existence.
I find it helpful to review this over and over to reinforce the teachings and to provide continuity to
what we have covered. It is my hope that, in doing so, we can get a full picture of these teachings,
how they are connected, and their importance to our understanding of the nature of things from a
buddhist perspective - one that I find so helpful to leading a life of purpose.

Although in this tradition the goal is release, or nibanna, there are many benefits that we gain by
being on the path. It helps us to reduce stress, can help control anxiety, it greatly promotes
emotional health, enhances self-awareness, improves our attention and attention span, is an aid to
helping us deal with addictive tendencies, helps generate compassion for ourselves and others,
among many other benefits. There is not guarantee that you will experience any or all of these but
based on my own experience, if you practice regular meditation, you will experience beneficial
results over time.
Last week I presented an overview of the 4th Khandha or Aggregate - “Mental Formations” - which is
the word saṅkārā in the Pali language. The word sankhara has many meanings but is prominently
defined as “formations”, or “that which has been put together” and “that which puts together”.
It is thought of passively as conditioned phenomena generally but specifically to all mental qualities
and characteristics. They are referred to as volitional formations because they are formed of volition
and because they are causes for arising of future volitional formations - such as anger stoking
violence. Additional translations include ‘conditioned things, determinations, and fabrications.
In the second (active) sense, sankhara refers to “karma” that leads to conditioned arising/dependant
origination. We will focus a little more both on Fabrication and on Dependant Origination in a bit.
As far as these mental factors are concerned, Joseph Goldenstein, in his book on Mindfulness: A
Practical Guide to Awakening, points out that in Buddhist psychology “mental factors are the building
blocks of all of our mental activity”. This includes our thoughts, emotions, moods, and mind states.
Buddhism is known for its lists and as far as mental factors, this is no exception.
The Abhidhamma, which are the teachings that were elaborated upon some 500 years after the
Buddha's passing, breaks down these mental factors into four basic categories.
In the Theravada tradition there are 52 of these mental factors.
The first category is known as the “universals”. They are common to all moments of consciousness.
These are contact, feeling, perception, attention, one-pointedness, volition, and something called
“life faculty” or vitality. It is the word jivitindryia. Indryia mean physical strength and jivit mean “life”.
The 2nd category of mental factors are known as the “occasionals” - they are sometimes present
and sometimes not. These are factors such as initial and sustained application of mind (vitacca and
vicara), decision, mental energy, rapture, and the “desire to do” - the word Chunda, which is different
than Tanha which is a type of desire which is craving or thirst - unskillful qualities.
The 3rd category is for the 14 unwholesome factors of mind: greed, hatred, delusion,
shamelessness, fearlessness of wrongdoing, restlessness, worry, wrong view, conceit, envy, greed,
sloth, torpor, and doubt.
Of all these unwholesome mind states—those that bring suffering to others or ourselves—four of
them are always present in every unwholesome consciousness: delusion, shamelessness,
fearlessness of wrongdoing, and restlessness.”

And finally we have the 4th category of mental factors known as the beautiful factors of mind - of
which there are 25. Of these 25 beautiful mental factors, 19 are considered to be common or
Universal to every skillful state of mind.
These include such factors as faith, mindfulness, shame at doing evil, lack of greed, lack of hatred,
having a balanced mind, tranquility of our body and of our consciousness, lightness of our body and
consciousness, malleability or softness of our body and consciousness, wieldiness of our body and
consciousness, proficiency of ours body and our consciousness, and straightness/rectitude of our
body and consciousness.
This word ‘kaya’ comes up in the 6 of the last 12 I mentioned here. I have used the translation
“body”. InBuddhism the word kaya is translated into “that which is accumulated”. I believe this is a
direct reference to “form” in terms of the Aggregates. The Aggregates being form, feeling,
perception, mental formations and consciousness.
After the universal 19 beautiful states we have the three abstinences which are right speech vs
wrong speech, right bodily action vs wrong bodily actions, and right livelihood vs wrong livelihood.
Then we have the two immeasurables which are karuna or compassion and mudita or sympathetic
joy.
And then finally one faculty of wisdom which is known as panna.
This list is not exhaustive. You may be able to come up with a few more, and, in fact, there are many
other mental factors listed in the teachings. There are also variations between the traditions. For
example In the Mahayana tradition there are 51 mental factors listed vs the 52 in the Theravada
tradition.
I will not list those here but I would like to point out in that, in the Mahayana, they delineate the
universal factors based on how we experience an object. Contact, Feeling or sensation, perception,
volition and attention. If any of these factors are missing then the experience of an object is
incomplete.
For example:
If there is no contact, then there would be no basis for perception.
If there is no feeling or sensation, there is no relishing of the object.
If there is no perception, then the specific characteristic of the object is not perceived.
If there is no volition, then there is no movement towards and settling on the object.
If there is no attention, then there is not holding onto the object.
In our contemplation of the Mental Factors it is important for us to see if we and how we identify with
any of them. Regardless of whether they are wholesome or unwholesome, it is this identification that
creates a sense of I, me and mine. This is when we do so we take on the perspective of “I am
thinking” or “ I am angry” or “I am happy” or I” I want to be happy”.
If we pay attention closely we begin to notice with wisdom and insight that these mental factors arise
from constantly changing conditions. We can notice how moods and emotions and feelings trigger
other moods, emotions, and feelings. As we experience these mental states we can notice how they

will have an affect on the body as well. Likewise, we can notice how a feelings in the body can have
an affect on what mind-states or mental factors arising.

Last week I provided a statement from the Buddha on Dependant Origination. I’d like to reiterate
that again here to remind us of this important principle.
The Buddha simply states that Dependant Origination is like this:
'What one intends, what one arranges, and what one obsesses about: This is a support
for the stationing of consciousness. There being a support, there is a landing [or: an
establishing] of consciousness. When that consciousness lands and grows, there is the
production of renewed becoming in the future. When there is the production of renewed
becoming in the future, there is future birth, aging & death, sorrow, lamentation, pain,
distress, & despair. Such is the origination of this entire mass of suffering & stress.'
Dependent Origination says that all dharmas arise in dependent upon other dharmas. It is often
phrased as "When this is, that is; This arising, that arises; When this is not, that is not; This
ceasing, that ceases."
There is another factor of the word sankhara that is of upmost importance and that is in terms of
Volition or intentionality. Joseph refers to this aspect as the “Chief of Staff” of the mind. If you
remember back, at one point we referred to our senses specifically as to different types of animals.
Well, mentally, we have a committee of mental factors at play all of the time. These are the
intentions we hold. Referred to as Volition - It is our intentions that motivate our actions so they are
karmically active. Intensions have the ability to bring results both in the present and in the future.
And these intentions can bring results in many ways - they can have an impact on our lives on many
levels.
Intention as a mental factor is neutral in itself. But it is the nature of or our motivation behind the act
that has karmic consequences. If we intentionally kill another sentient being is way different than if it
was accidental. On top of that, we can identify with these intentions. Being filled with anger we can
feel we are angry people, or if we are experiencing compassion we can feel we are compassionate.
I am not downplaying any skillful means here - just that what is being asked of us is to notice these
factors and our relationship with them. For instance, having the view that we are compassionate we
might get angry when someone doesn’t recognize this compassionate goodness in us. It is important
for us to be able to notice how intention factors into the movement through our lives. In order to stop
any intention would require that we make a decision in our minds to do so. Even then, we can notice
that the flow of intentions coming thru us seems to have a momentum of its own. It would be
beneficial for us to take an interest in what moves us through the day. What is it, that comes into our
minds, that is the impulse for us to take some action, for instance to move your hand, or to drink
some tea? This is what we are trying to see through our practice. These calming and concentration
practices of the satipatthanas.

And finally, I like to talk briefly about the 5th Aggregate of CONSCIOUSNESS. As we have noted,
consciouosness arises in relation to each of the sense doors, when we see, feel, taste, touch or
experience in the mind thoughts, feelings and emotions. Consciousness is the aspect of knowing.

Even though our consciousness is colored by the mental factors that we have been discussing, the
basic cognizing activity does not change in different circumstances. Consciousness is a mirrorlike
awareness as the nature of a mirror is simply to reflect what comes before it.
We can learn to look at the impermanent nature of consciousness. Seeing happens as a result of
contact between the eye, visible form, light and attention. If we explore this process, we can free
ourselves even from identification with the activity of knowing. We can explore this by knowing that
when we are concentrated on something that we blot out other parts of our consciousness. This
happens to me daily at my 9 to 5. I can have a pain here or there and get so absorbed in my work
that the pain can appear to disappear! I like to refer to what is known as our DEFAULT MODE in
psychology. This occurs when we are not doing a task or thinking of others. The default mode is
when we are alone with ourselves, our mind tends come into self reflection, to take an inventory of
our state of being - usually to reflect on the past with judgement or to the future with worry. A lot of
what we do is to avoid this because it can make us uncomfortable. Sitting with this dukkha - being
the knowing/the observer can enable us to reset this position we take with our own being.
Another way we can look at this is in terms of the senses themselves. If there is an absence of
attention to sound, hearing consciousness doesn’t arise. If that is the case can potentially unwanted
sounds bother us if we do not give them attention. What about anything else we experience in
consciousness? What about things we do not want to see? For to feel and so on. What about
thinking? What if we did not give attention to our thinking or a single thought?
What if instead of taking in the content of our experience we simply become aware of seeing in itself
or hearing, or thinking? This is what is commonly pointed out in meditation instructions. Noticing the
clouds of experience as they pass by and bringing our attention back into the body or to our
breathing or whatever we chose as our anchor. We can easily note this in our awareness as
“seeing” or “feeling” or “thinking” or “excited” or whatever you are knowing.
From this comes what is called Purification of View. For as we are able to see the dharma unfold in
front of us, to becoming aware of the impermanent, impersonal and stressful nature of our
experience we do not cling to it and not clinging we gain freedom from their hold on us.
Two quotes to close:
Joseph refers to two quotes in his book on this subject. The first is one by Ajahn Jumnien, a Thai
Forest Master to is still living. He says:
At some point the mind becomes so clear and balanced that whatever arises is seen and left
untouched, with no interference. One ceases to focus on any particular content and all is
seen as simply mind and matter, an empty process arising and passing away of its own . . . a
perfect balance of mind, with no reactions. . . . There is no longer any doing . . .
And another very important quote that points to this knowing quality of awareness that is beyond
conditioned existence. This one from Mahasi Sayadow:
[At times there is] nothing to note, with the body disappearing and the sense of touch lost.
However at this moment, knowing consciousness is still apparent. In the very clear open
space of the sky there remains only one very clear, blissful consciousness, which is very
clear beyond comparison and very blissful. The yogi tends to delight in such clear, blissful
consciousness. But, the consciousness is not going to stay permanent. . . . It has to be noted
as “knowing, knowing.”

And finally, it should be understood that even as we get to very refined states of awareness for us to
not hold onto them. A Ch’an Master said “As soon as there’s something considered important, it
becomes a nest.” Joseph reminds us, “If freedom is our aspiration, we don’t want to make even
consciousness our nest. As long as there is identification with anything, any sense of the knower, the
one knowing, then we are still bound by the conventional, conditioned mind.”
Being aware of and seeing the Aggregates with wisdom is extremely important in order to develop
detachment with them - to non-clinging. We can be aware of our clinging to form, clingint to feelings,
clinging to perceptions, clinging to mental formations (how we create the world and our intentions),
and clinging to our consciousness. We can also be aware of the impermanent nature of each.
Without this we will not be able to free ourselves from the stress and suffering that result from our
doing so. This is our practice. This is this understanding and use of this Foundation of Mindfulness.
