Exploring the Bahia Sutta
# The Story
%% This is one of my favorite teachings - on par with [[B_Book Markups/Readwise/Books/The Heart Sutra]]%%
Bahia was a well-respected practitioner 
lived in ancient India the middle of the country
who wondered if he was an [[arahant (enlightened)]]
As the story goes he was informed 
that he was not an arahant,
& that he wasn't even on the right path.
but that there is a real arahant living some distance away (The Buddha).
So Bahiya immediately travels to see the Buddha. 
It was a long trek but eventually he meets the him on alms round.
Immediately Implores him for a teaching. Saying he might die at any moment.
Finally, after asking him 3 times the Buddha then gives him the instruction
that he should remain conscious of just the [[base sense experience]] as a way to transcend [[Google Keep/Dukkha]]


#### THE BAHIYA INSTRUCTIONS
There are a few slighly different translations
I Prefer this one from Joseph Goldstein's book, "Mindfulness: A Practical Guide to Awakening".
It goes like this.

> In the seen there is only the seen, 
> in the heard, there is only the heard, 
> in the sensed [smell, taste, and touch], there is only the sensed, 
> in the cognized, there is only the cognized: 
> This, Bahiya, is how you should train yourself. 
> 
> When, Bahiya, there is for you 
> in the seen only the seen, 
> in the heard only the heard, 
> in the sensed only the sensed, 
> in the cognized only the cognized, 
> then, Bahiya, there is no “you” 
> in connection with all that.
> 
> When, Bahiya, there is no “you” 
> in connection with that, 
> there is no “you” there. 
> 
> When, Bahiya, there is no “you” there, 
> then, Bahiya, you are neither here 
> nor there 
> nor in between the two. 
> This, just this, is the end of suffering.

Goldstein, Joseph. Mindfulness (pp. 41-42). Sounds True. Kindle Edition. 

### Commentary
A few comments 
1st from Analayo
He believes that the main implication of these instructions
> are that one should remain aware of the [[bare experience]]
> and to limit one's mental processing to that - the bare act of cognizing. 
> If we were to stay [[in the present]] just with awareness of what we are experiencing, then the usual [[proliferations]] can be avoided. 

If we can remain aware at the "[[sense-doors]]"
we will not be carried away with them.

HOW WOULD THAT PLAY OUT IN PRACTICE?

By not being carried away (not identifying/grasping onto them/reacting/equanimous)
we are not in those senses nor in the sensed (empty, not the subject or the object)
this is the meaning of 
there is no "you" in connection with that.

neither here nor there as a result (again this refers to not in those senses nor in the senses, neither object or subject, seer or seen)
this is the connection to the idea of experience being empty of self

With this same logic we can understand neither being here nor there, nor in between the two. (empty, but when we think of it we take a position)

As a result, Analayo points out that we would tend to not take a stand on anything. That would be grasping onto them again.
But this is our habit.

In the Sutta itself 
it points to the practice of not attaching to anything
presence
staying present with awareness of experience
being aware of seeing forms without mental chatter
Staying with the bare experience

So 
Once we see there is just the seen, one realizes one is not that which is seen.
We will not be carried away our perception of experiences that are impermanent, not under our control, conditioned by our perceptual process.
In a sense, we will not identify with this process of experience that is taking place. 
Nor identify with any desire or aversion to what is conditioned. This is a key insight
We learn to stand in the middle ground. Not reacting or identifying any aspect of experience.
And one learns to avoid taking a stance on anything.
This is the perfection of equanimity, of seeing the emptiness of things.
We learn to not cling to experience and are free to act with wisdom and an open heart.



### Lead in to Zhuangzi
Teaching has an interesting connection to the philosopher Zhuangzi
who lived about 100 years later than the Buddha. 
He once stated that "listening stops at the ear" and that we should "hide the world in the world".  
Doesn't this sound familiar?
He thought that 
> language itself is the formost barrier in our search for our essential selves and our desire to live happy, fulfilled lives.
https://theconversation.com/finding-your-essential-self-the-ancient-philosophy-of-zhuangzi-explained-196215
And that Higher knowledge is beyond words. 
Being empty and for us  to go beyond the container of self 
Train our ability to be In and not OF this world

There is a certain level of scepticism in Buddhism, and a healty dose of it in philosophy.
But we shouldn't be overly [[skeptical]].
As "Words are not just blowing wind"
Words have something to say and language is only a repository from meanings, and not meaning itself.

We cannot deny we are social animals.
We have certain duties are inescapable features of the roles we find ourselves - being parents or sons and daughters, 
employers and employees, 
a part of society

To be in the world and not of it means we accept what is beyond our control with equanimity.
This from Zhuangzi:
> Once upon a time, I, Zhuangzi, dreamt I was a butterfly, fluttering hither and thither, to all intents and purposes a butterfly. I was conscious only of my happiness as a butterfly, unaware that I was Zhuangzi. Soon I awakened, and there I was, veritably myself again. Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly, dreaming I am a man.


In entertaining for a moment the idea that we might be dreaming we are a butterfly that is dreaming it is human, we engage in the very activity which allows us to break free of the normal and human way of understanding the world. This is a kind of therapeutic scepticism.

This practice is the adoption of a more universal perspective
With practice, our ordinary lives can become imbued with moments of spontaneous awe.
We can see the transformation of things
Notice how everything eventually becomes part of something else
With this mindfulness and scepticism -in questioning the world around us, we transcend our own limited perspective and ignite a boundless curiosity about everything we experience.  

Iris Murdock described the highest virtue necessary for living a fulfilled life as a kind of "unselfing"
She said:
> “Good is a transcendent reality,” ... unselfing, or engaging in this therapeutic act of scepticism, allows us to “pierce the veil of selfish consciousness and join the world as it really is”.
