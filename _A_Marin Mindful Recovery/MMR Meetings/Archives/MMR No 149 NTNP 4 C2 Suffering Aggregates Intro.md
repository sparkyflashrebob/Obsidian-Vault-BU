[[Khanda]]
Prior to the Buddha it was defined as 
- a mass (of firewood or water)
- thrunk of a tree
- torso of the body
- shoulder of an elephant  [simile of the elephants trunk]

Buddha gave it a psychological meaning
calling them clinging-aggregates
a way of describing his analysis of the truth of stress and suffering
- the constituents of experience itself
	- material form
	- feeling
	- perception
	- volitional activities ([[sankhara]])
	- consciousness
- each aggregate is actually are broad a number of factors sharing a particular quality or function

For instance, he defines Form broadly as an Aggregate in this way:
> - “Whatever form there is, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near: this is called the form aggregate.”
  
And this is the same with the other aggregates - feelings, perceptions, volitions, & consciousness

So each of these aggregates can be further distinguished in 11 ways
1. in the past
2. in the future
3. in the present
4. whether it is pertaining to oneself
5. or pertaining to others
6. quality of being gross (or as a whole)
7. quality of being subtle (or refined)
8. quality of being inferior (not important)
9. quality of being superior (or more valuable)
10. far (not close to us)
11. near (or close to us)

In terms of form:
- while I refer to each of these aspects (pay attention to what comes up inside of you)
- here referring to form as the body
	- in time thinking about
		- how was the body in the past
		- how will the body be in the future
		- how is the body in the present
	- the relative to oneself
		- referring to my body
		- referring to other bodies
	- referring to the body in gross or subtle terms
		- the body as a whole or aspects that might be refined or hidden
	- superior & inferior being 
		- what is more valuable or not
	- likewise with near and far 
		- with what is here (touching the body) or in the distance

Speaking of Form
The Aggregate of Form is what we call the material substance we experience ([[rupakkhandha]])
- Before the discovery of the periodical table of elements that we know today, in ancient Indian physics and other cultures 
	- Generally spoke of physical form of being comprised of "the four great elements"
		- earth element
		- water element
		- fire element
		- air element
	- each describe the four behavioral properties of matter
		- earth
			- solidity or hardness of something
			- provides a basis of support for the other elements
		- water
			- liquidity & fluidity and movement
			- function of binding material particles together
			- like making pancakes
		- fire
			- heat or coolness
			- considered to be a function of ripening matter
		- air
			- distension
				- expansion and contraction
	- there are qualities that are derived form the elements
		- most important ones are
			- space (the container)
				- not usually thought of
				- we usually think about the objects in space vs the space itself
			- and then there are the 5 sense faculties and sense objects
				- visible forms, sound, odor, and taste

When we think about forms, generally we do not think about the elements or the parts that make up objects, we talk about their overall function and purpose.  I would say this would be from the gross perspective.

Aggregate of Feeling ([[vedanakkhandha]])
- aspect of experience that arises out of contact with something
	- Feeling as a concept is an important link in the Buddha's teaching on [[_Book Markups/Readwise/Articles/Dependent Origination]] - on conditionality
	- feelings arise when we encounter the consciousness of an object thru a sense base
		- for instance there is a feeling thru sight consciousness of a ship thru the eye
		- or when see a loved one or someone we do not like
	-  this feeling occurs with the purely internal mind base which cognizes purely mental objects
		- thinking about a loved on or someone you might not like
	- there are considered six kinds of feeling
		- based on the type of contact as its condition
			- the feeling of seeing (eye-contact)
			- the feeling of hearing (ear-contact) 
			- the feeling of smelling (nose-contact) 
			- the feeling of taste (tongue-contact)
			- the feeling of touching (body-contact)
			- the feeling of mental contact (mind-contact)
	- The word for feeling in Pali is [[vedana]] and it refers to affective or feeling tone of something, whether it is 
		- pleasant
		- painful
		- neutral
		- one of the universal mental factors - factors that operate in the wake of every mind situation
	- In Buddhist terms this does not signify emotion - which does not have a specific word
		- Emotions are the "meaning" we give to our felt experience
		- in terms of the aggregates Emotions are considered a complex phenomena involving several aggregates

Aggregate of Perception ([[sannakkhanda]])
- also arises thru contact
	- six types of perception also based on the sense base
	- these are described in relation to the object being perceived (rather than the perspective of the sense door), that is
		- perception of visible forms
		- perception of sounds
		- perception of odors
		- perception of tastes
		- perception of tactile objects
		- perception of mental objects
	- Perception has  the function of singling out and grasping distinctive qualities of an object
	- it is also a basis for how we identify, categorize & recognize objects
	- (PAUSE) some teachings highlight the dangers inherent in raw perception
		- because there is the tendancy to create and posit inappropriate labels to things
			- which result in a distorted, biased, and deceptive picture of the world

Aggregate of volitional activities ([[sankharakkhanda]])
- six kinds of volition
	- volition regarding visible forms
	- volition regarding sounds
	- volition regarding odors
	- volition regarding tastes
	- volition regarding tactile objects
	- volition regarding mental objects
-  function is to instigate action
- it is thru this aggregate karma is created
> - “It is volition, monks, that I call kamma; for having willed, one acts through body, speech, and mind.”
  - in defining dukkha there are three general categories 

Later this aggregate was expanded  to serve a broad range of categories comprising all the varied mental functions in the suttas that do not fit the other aggregates.
- thought ([[Vitakka]])
- examination ([[vicara]])
- unwholesome states as greed, hatred & delusion
- wholesome states such as mindfulness, kindness, compassion, & wisdom

Aggregate of consciousness 
- six kinds
	- eye-consciousness
	- ear-consciousness
	- nose-consciousness
	- tongue-consciousness
	- body-consciousness
	- mind-consciousness
- distinction between consciousness and perception is hard to draw
- linked
- teachings do not differentiate them other than
	- perception by way of its objects
	- consciousness by way of it internal sense base
- Bhikkhu Bodhi figures that:
	-  consciousness is the factor that "illuminates" an entire sensory sphere making it accessible thru a sense faculty, while
	- perception is the factor that homes in on the sense object illuminated by consciousness

Referring back to this understanding of what is the Buddha calls dukkha - or stress and suffering, when the Budha spoke of dukkha he was refers to three kinds of dukkha.
		- 1. [[Dukkha Dukkha]] - which is our response to unpleasant physical or mental experiences - there is the feeling of it being unpleasant and there is aversion to it and wanting to experience something different. 
		- 2. [[Sankhara Dukkha]] - and this is when we take Dukkha Dukha a step further than our simple aversion to unpleasant physical or mental experience.  This is where thoughts arise, with phrases that include "should's" and "shouldn't", "if only", the judgements, and anxiety-filled thoughts that arise.  
		- 3. similarly  there is [[Viparinama Dukkha]] - which arises in response to pleasant experiences and is tied to its impermanence or changing. It is the suffering that occurs when we can something to continue. Like my grand daughter wanting us to stay with here awhile longer. It also lingers while we are experiencing pleasant experiences because underneath the experience we know that it will not last. So even in our joy there can be suffering.

In all three cases, the suffering arises due to craving or wanting for our circumstances to be different.  I call this [[banging our heads against the wall]]. 
We are bound to experience unpleasant feelings of separation or loss because this is the nature of human experience.
The only way to keep dukkha from arising is to change our response to unpleasant experience.  If we can acknowledge unpleasant feelings and sensations, be with them and let them run their course, dukkha will not arise.
This requires mindfulness. Being mindful of these aggregates, in all of its guises, past, present or future, within oneself or with others, whether crude or subtle, near or far. Knowing that they are temporary, can help us shift our perspective, can provide us with the vision to see what we are adding onto our experience of things. Whether pleasant, unpleasant or neither. And to stop that. To free ourselves from our own suffering. And to be with the experience of being alive, however it is unfolding. With its 10,000 joys and its 10,000 sorrows.
