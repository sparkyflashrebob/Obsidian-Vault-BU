We have been taking a detailed look at the Discourse on the Foundations of MIndfulness for the
last few months. This Discourse and its teachings are the cornerstone of the vipassana
tradition, which is also known here in the west as the insight meditation. It details what the
Buddha defines as the method and practice of mindfulness or what I like to call, a method of
Awakening.
The 1st Foundation, mindfulness of the body, has to do with bringing attention to breathing and
body sensations as well as establish ways of looking at the body as a body, it postures,
activities, seeing it as a whole both internally and externally, seeing it as components,
contemplation of the dissolution of the body, and awareness of its elements - according to the
understanding of nature at the time of the Buddha, which was over 2500 years ago.
The 2nd Foundation, Mindfulness of Feelings, has to do with noticing the sensations of the body
and how the mind perceives this affect, whether pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
The 3rd Foundation, Mindfulness of Mind, has to do with an evaluation of our state of mind in
terms of wanting or greed, not-wanting or hatred, or being deluded about what we are aware of -
ignorance.
The 4th Satipatthana, which is Contemplation of Mind-Objects, mental phenomena or
Dhammas. It investigates 108 mental objects and in the process manages to guide us through
the whole curriculum of Buddhist psychology: The Mind-Objects include The Five Hindrances,
The Five Aggregates, the Six Sense spheres, the Seven Factors of Awakening, and the Four
Noble Truths.
We are familiar somewhat with “The Hindrances” as these mind-objects have been the topic that
we have focused on the last few weeks. They include Sense-Desire, Ill-Will, Sloth & Torpor,
Restlessness & Worry, and Skeptical Doubt.
Now we are introduced to a new set of Mind-Objects, known together as “The Five Aggregates”.
“Again, bhikkhus, a bhikkhu abides contemplating mind-objects as mind-objects in terms of the
five aggregates affected by clinging. And how does a bhikkhu abide contemplating mind-objects
as mind-objects in terms of the five aggregates affected by clinging?
Here a bhikkhu understands: ‘Such is material form, such is its origin, such is its disappearance;
such is feeling, such is its origin, such is its disappearance; such is perception, such is its origin,
such is its disappearance; such are the formations, such their origin, such their disappearance;
such is consciousness, such is its origin, such is its disappearance.’
There is an added section note here that explains that “The five aggregates affected by clinging
(pañc’upādānakkhadha ) are “usually taught as “the five groups of factors comprising the
individual personality.”
As you can guess, this will get into very rich territory and I hope to cover this, and the related
topics it raises, with as much detail as possible and, yet, attempt to make it as simple as
possible. This week we will take on the overview of the aggregates themselves. Next week we
will follow up with a more detailed look at “formations” otherwise known as fabrications, and
then I believe a more detailed look at “consciousness”. At least that is what I am leaning

First some background.
The word for “The Aggregates” in Pali is khandas or skandhas in Sanskrit. It means “heaps,
aggregates, collections, groupings”. It refers to the five aggregates of clinging, “the five material
and mental factors that take part in the rise of craving and clinging. They are also explained as
the five factors that constitute and explain a sentient being’s person and personality”. Notice
that in referring to the Aggregates the Buddha places emphasis on the aspects of clinging. It is
our clinging to these aspects of categorizing the world of experience, to identifying with them
that causes us to suffer.
As an aggregate, this includes all of the possibilities within each, whether it is past, present or
future, internal or external, gross or subtle, inferior or superior, near or far.
Clinging refers to desire and attachment to the aggregates. This is important to understand as it
is this desire and attachment in relation to the aggregates that this the root cause for the arising
of dukkha, which is stress and suffering.
The first of the Aggregates is “form” or “matter” - material elements - the word rupa in Pali.
Defined as matter, body or “material form” of a being or any existence. It refers to what makes
up any person, sentient being and object to be composed of - referring to the four basic
elements known to Buddhism at the time earth (solidity), water (cohesion), fire (heat) and wind
(motion). It is explained that material form is what is affected by external conditions such as hot
and cold, hunger and thirst.
“Feeling” or “sensation” the word vedana in Pali is something we covered a bit already, as it is
the what is referred to in the 2nd Foundation of Mindfulness. It is the sensory experience of an
object. It is pleasant, unpleasant or neutral qualities.
“Perception” is the word sanna in Pali. “It is the sensory and mental process that registers,
recognizes and labels” objects of experience, such as tree, the color green or the emotion fear.
Both feeling and perception are closely related to the same aspect of experience. Feelings
provide the “how” and perceptions the “what”. Perception is related to the faculty of memory.
“Mental Formations” also known as sankhara are “constructing activities”, “constructing thing”,
“volition”, karmic activities”, all types of mental imprints and conditioning triggered by an object.
It includes any process that makes a person initiate action or act. It is our intention. This is the
reactive aspect of the mind.
“Consciousness” is the word vijnana in Pali and refers to discrimination or discernment of
something. It is the Awareness of an object and discrimination of its components and aspects
and it is of six types. Eye Consciousness, Ear Consciousness, Nose Consciousness, Tongue
Consciousness, Body Consciousness and Mind Consciousness. It is described as “that which
cognizes or discerns” and as “the base that supports all experience.”

Although The Aggregates are often interpreted and taught as the constituents of a person and
personality, that my teacher, Thanisarro Bhikkhu prefers that the skandhas, these Aggregates,
should be viewed as activities. In a Study Guide that he created a few years back he says that
“Form — which covers physical phenomena of all sorts, both within and without the body —
wears down or "de-forms." Feeling feels pleasure, pain, and neither pleasure nor pain.
Perception labels or identifies objects. Consciousness cognizes the six senses (counting the
intellect as the sixth) along with their objects.
The most difficult to understand is Mental-Formations otherwise known as Fabrications. We will
discuss this topic in detail next week.
In terms of being aggregates of personality. The idea is that, based on form and contact, mental
factors arise (feelings, perceptions and mental formations) which also forms of contact, and
these give rise to consciousness. It is this coming together or contact of an object, the
sense-organs and sense consciousness that are the factors of existence. We then take these
experiences, identify with them, and create a sense of self out of it, hence the term Aggregate or
heap - just like the rock aggregate, which is made up of other rocks and forms of matter
compressed together.

Teaching on Anatta-------
In this next section on explaining “Self”, I borrow liberally from Bhiikhu Analyo’s book on the
“Satipatthana: The Direct Path to Realization”.
There are many different schools of thought about the nature of self. At the time of the Buddha,
which was a time rich with varying schools of thought. The Ajivika teachings proposed a soul
having a particular color and considerable size as a true self. The Jains an infinite soul of
similar size and weight that survived death, and its pure state possessed infinite knowledge.
The Upanisads envisioned various conceptions of an eternal self. Then there were the
materialists schools which rejected all immaterial conceptions of a self or soul. In order to
account for causality they proposed a theory of inherent nature of material phenomena.
Humans individuals were automatons functioning according to the dictates of matter. Human
effor was of no avail and there was no such thing as ethical responsiblity.
The Buddhe took the middle approach to the belief of an eternal soul and the denial of anything
other than matter. The Buddha, in his enlightenment, affirmed karmic consequences and ethical
responsibility by explaining karmic retribution over several lifetimes with the help of dependent
co-arising. He explained that the five aggregates account for our subjective experience and
when looked at closely were found to be impermanent and not amenable to complete personal
control. As a result a permanent and self-sufficient self cannot be found within or apart from the
five aggregates.
Self, as an independent and permanent entity is related to notions of mastery and control. The
standard teachings on anatta suggest that each of the five aggregates should be considered of
“mine”, “I am”, and “myself”. This is importance of our exploration of the aggregates, to uncover
our various patterns of identification and attachment to a sense of self.

Most Buddhist Meditation Teachers explain the aggregates this way. It makes it easier to
explore and understand through our own direct experience. So let's contemplate how these
elements of experience might aggregate into that of a person.
When we look at who we are, how do we define that? Let first take a look at our bodies. Are we
our bodies?
There is a parable in the Udana about a group of blind men who came upon an elephant but
have never come upon one before. The story goes that they wanted to know what it was so
they each touched different parts of the elephant's body and reported back to the group.
“The first person, whose hand landed on the trunk, said, "This being is like a thick snake". For
another one whose hand reached its ear, it seemed like a kind of fan. As for another person,
whose hand was upon its leg, said, the elephant is a pillar like a tree-trunk. The blind man who
placed his hand upon its side said the elephant, "is a wall". Another who felt its tail, described it
as a rope. The last felt its tusk, stating the elephant is that which is hard, smooth and like a
spear.”
In some versions of the story, as they describe to each other what they think the elephant is,
they suspect that the others were dishonest and they come to blows. The moral of the story is
that humans have a tendency to claim absolute truth based on their limited subjective
experience and they ignore other people’s limited, subjective experience which may be equally
true or untrue.
I agree on this, but the point here for this discussion of the aggregates is that we have the
tendency to take what appears to us, what we can sense, as being what that object is, this
includes aspects of ourselves and the body.
Another way to look at it would be as if we were a cow. Now a cow when it is seen as a whole is
a cow. But if the cow is killed and cut up into parts then we call it steak, or hamburger or shoes
or a coat. If I were to lose my hand, if it was sitting on the table, would I be any less me? If I
take off a leg also would I be any less me? How far would you have to go before I would not be
me and still alive to be able to talk or interact with you?
There are studies that show that even when people have lost limbs that they still imagine that
they are part of them, that they have pain, or can experience sensation. There are even
experiments that you can view on Youtube showing experiments with people viewing a fake
hand and hiding their real hand, that are being pinched and probed. The get a feeling,they feel
sensations as if the fake hand that is not attached to their body is theirs!!!
There is a story of a man who lived his life normally with only 10% of what we would suggest as

which is below average but not considered a mental disability. He is just one of many similar

In terms of Consciousness, this is the cognizing part of the mind tha simply KNOWS.
Consciousness has one basic characteristic, whic is knowing. There are some interesting
aspects of consciousness. The first is that different circumstances do not flavor the knowing.

Joseph Goldstein refers to it as a “mirrorlike awareness, just as the nature of a mirror is simple
to reflect what comes before it.” In these teachings on the Aggregates and consciousness, the
Buddha provides special emphasis to their impermanent nature. For example, Eye
Consciousness arises when there is an eye, visible form, light and attention. If any of these are
absent then eye consciousness cannot arise. It is therefore dependent on cause and conditions.
So the question is, if consciousness is impermanent - only arising out of conditions and
therefore insubstantial and empty, should it be considered me and mine?
By the way, as I have mentioned before, the Buddha actually never answered if there is a self or
not. In fact there were quite a few questions that he did not answer for various reasons. But the
main reason is that they distracted us from the bigger question that he focused his teachings
about. This question of there being suffering and there is an end to suffering.
Throughout the forty-five years after his enlightenment, the Buddha had answered many
questions asked to him. Depending on the nature of the questions, the Buddha had largely
responded using one of the four ways: 1) He gave unequivocal answers to questions which
have direct answers; 2) He elaborated on answers to questions which require careful analysis;
3) He replied with counter-questions to guide the inquirer towards forming an answer; and 4) He
kept some questions aside. In particular the Buddha kept silent on ten questions.
These undeclared questions that were kept aside are:
(1) Is the universe eternal?
(2) Is the universe not eternal?
(3) Is the universe finite?
(4) Is the universe infinite?
(5) Are the soul and the body the same?
(6) Are the soul and the body not the same?
(7) Will the enlightened one be reborn after death?
(8) Will the enlightened one not be reborn after death?
(9) Will the enlightened one both be reborn and not be reborn after death?
(10) Will the enlightened one neither be reborn nor not be reborn after death?
