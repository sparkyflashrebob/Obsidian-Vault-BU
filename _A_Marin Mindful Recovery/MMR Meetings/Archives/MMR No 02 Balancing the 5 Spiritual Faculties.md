DHARMA TALK ON BALANCING THE 5 SPIRITUAL FACULTIES

In Buddhism there are many lists used as aids to memory, like the 5 Hinderances have already discussed.
This week I will introduce the [[5 spiritual faculties]] we can use to prop up our practice.

The way of Wisdom or Spiritual progress on the path depends on the emergence of five cardinal virtues —

-[[faith]] or conviction or belief ([[saddhā]])
-[[energy]] or persistence or perseverance ([[viriya]])
-[[_Book Markups/Readwise/Books/Mindfulness]] or memory ([[sati]])
-[[stillness of the mind]] ([[samādhi]])
-[[wisdom]] or understanding or comprehension ([[pañña]]).

The conduct of ordinary people (like us) is governed by our senses-based instincts and impulses. As we progress on this spiritual path, these new spiritual forces gradually take over, until in the end the five cardinal virtues dominate and shape everything we do feel and think.

These virtues are called [[indriya]] in Sanskrit and Pali, which can be translated to faculties, controlling faculties, or spiritual faculties.
These same five virtues are called powers ([[bala]]) if emphasis is on the fact that they are "unshakable by their opposites." lack of faith, low energy, mindlessness, an agitated mind and ignorance or lack of understanding or comprehension.

Together, this set of five faculties is one of the seven sets of qualities lauded by the Buddha as conducive to Enlightenment or awakkening.

[[ENERGY OR VIRYA]]

This brings us to ENERGY or VIRYA
Virya is a Buddhist term commonly translated as "energy", "diligence", "enthusiasm", or "effort". It can be defined as an attitude of gladly engaging in wholesome activities, and it functions to cause one to accomplish wholesome or virtuous actions.
Energy or persistence refers to exertion towards the Four Right Efforts, which will be a future topic that I offer to this group very soon. Just briefly they are 1) Restraint of the senses, 2) Abandonment of our defects of character, 3) Cultivation of the Enlightenment or Awakening Factors, and 4) Preservation of concentration. Which means to put effort into meditation.

Within the teachings, virya is identified as One of the six occasional mental factors, which means that they are not always present.
These are:
1) [[Vitakka]] - [[Application of thought]]
2) [[Vicāra]] - [[Examining]]
3) [[Adhimokkha]] - [[Decision]]
4) [[Viriya]] - [[Energy]]
5) [[Pīti]] - [[Rapture]]
6) [[Chanda]] - [[Desire (to act)]]

[[Virya]] is the mind intent on being ever active, devoted, unshaken, not turning back and or being indefatigable. These are ideals so they point us in the right direction in our practice. Virya perfects and realizes what is conducive to the positive.
Virya (the perfection of vigor) involves applying persistent energy and zeal in overcoming one's faults and cultivating good qualities, in studying the Dharma and the arts and sciences, and in doing good works for the welfare of others. The term is derived from vira (a noun for a hero). It corresponds to right memory, the sixth factor of the Noble Eightfold Path, but more explicitly it signifies a heroic endeavor or to benefit other living beings.

Viriya oe Energy is a critical component in many of the sets of qualities conducive to Enlightenment, including:
These sets include both the five spiritual faculties (indriya) which we are discussing today and the five powers (bala), in the ten "perfections" (pāramitās), and in the seven factors of enlightenment (bojjhaṅga).

It is also associated with "Right Effort" in the Noble Eightfold Path.

In the Kī ṭā giri Sutta (MN 70), the Buddha instructs his followers:
... For a faithful disciple who is intent on fathoming the Teacher's Dispensation, it is natural that he conducts himself thus: 'Willingly, let only my skin, sinews, and bones remain, and let the flesh and blood dry up on my body, but my energy shall not be relaxed so long as I have not attained what can be attained by human strength [purisa-tthāmena], human energy [purisa-viriyena], and human persistence [purisa-parakkamena]...."

If that were not enough, there are other characterizations of virya which I would like to briefly highlight.

Viya stands for strenuous and sustained effort to overcome unskillful ways (akusala dhamma), such as indulging in sensuality, ill will, and harmfulness. In the absence of sustained effort in practicing meditation, craving creeps in and the meditator comes under its influence. Right effort (vīryabala) is thus required to overcome unskillful mental factors.
Viriya can also be aroused by strong feelings of Saṃvega (or disgust with the non-spiritual way of life). This can lead us to a course correction in the way we live our lives and turn us further towards awakening and the path of peace.  
It also stands for the right endeavor to attain deep states of meditation, jhana.
Vīrya can also signify courage and physical strength and also signifies the strength of character and persistent effort for the well-being of others as well as the ability to defend the the path itself. This is like defending those of the benfits of AA by those do not know of those benefits. 

Interesting aside - in writing this talk and in my life. I am aware that my energy waxes and wains. Part of the skillsfulness around energy is to be aware of it and learning how to cope with these changing energy levels and the means to provide more energy. Sometimes to get energy you have to put forth more effort, sometimes you rest, sometimes nurture, sometimes you contemplate or remember (fear is a motivator).

So the lesson here is to have enough energy but not too much as it would exhaust eventually.

[[MINDFULNESS OR SATI]]

MINDFULNESS/SATI is a synonym for the whole of the path but primarily refers to focusing on the four satipatthana or foundations of mindfulness. SATI is defined as mindfulness or awareness but actually is translated as the ability to remember wholesome dhammas or teachings. Bhikkhu Bodhi, one of the foremost modern translators says the word derives from a verb, sarati, meaning “to remember,” and occasionally in Pali sati is still explained in a way that connects it with the idea of memory. But when it is used in relation to meditation practice, we have no word in English that precisely captures what it refers to. An early translator cleverly drew upon the word mindfulness, which is not even in my dictionary. This has served its role admirably, but it does not preserve the connection with memory, sometimes needed to make sense of a passage.

[sati] should be understood as what allows awareness of the full range and extent of dhammas; sati is an awareness of things in relation to things, and hence an awareness of their relative value. Applied to the satipațțhānas, presumably what this means is that sati is what causes the meditation practitioner to "remember" that any feeling he may experience exists in relation to a whole variety or world of feelings that may be skillful or unskillful, with faults or faultless, relatively inferior or refined, dark or pure."

Other have defined mindfulness as a way to liberation, "constantly watching sensory experience in order to prevent the arising of cravings which would power future experience into rebirths" 

It would wise to note that “Mindfulness” is the antidote to delusion (Moha).

Traditionally, mindfulness is thought to be applied to four satipattana or domains of meditation.  "Constantly watching sensory experience in order to prevent the arising of cravings which would power future experiences of suffering."

The four domains are:
1) mindfulness of the body (kaya);
2) mindfulness of feelings or sensations (vedanā);
3) mindfulness of mind or consciousness (citta); and
4) mindfulness of dhammās

For practicing Buddhists, references to "dharma" or dhamma in Pali, particularly as "the Dharma", generally means the teachings of the Buddha, commonly known throughout the East as Buddha-Dharma. It includes especially the discourses on the fundamental principles (such as the Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path), as opposed to the parables and to the poems.
We will talk a lot about the meaning of mindfulness here in this group as it applied to our meditation practice but also to it can aid us in our lives, moment-to-moment.

[[SAMADI OR STILLNESS OF MIND]]

> Stillness of the mind refers to achieving the four jhanas. The practice of the jhana’s is born from concentration. 
> The term 'Samadhi' derives from the root sam-a-dha, which means 'to collect' or 'bring together', and thus it is often translated as 'concentration' or 'unification of mind'. 
> Samadhi is also associated with the term samatha which is a calm abiding. 
> In the commentarial tradition (development/clarification of buddhist teachings after the buddha passed), samadhi is defined as ekaggata, or one-pointedness of mind (Cittass'ekaggatā).
> Buddhagosa, one of the foremost Theravada Buddhist commentator, translator, and philosophers of the 5th-Century, defines samadhi as "the centering of consciousness ... evenly and rightly on a single object [...] the state in virtue of which consciousness' remains evenly and rightly on a single object, undistracted and unscattered." 
> He also said that teaching mention four kinds of samadhi:
> Momentary concentration (khanikasamadhi): A mental stabilization which arises during vipassana.
> Preliminary concentration (parikammasamadhi): Arises out of the meditator's initial attempts to focus on a meditation object.
> Access concentration (upacarasamadhi): Arises when the five hindrances are dispelled, when jhana is present, and with the appearance the 'counterpart sign' (patibhaganimitta). This is also called the Nimita, which are colored lights in the mind that appear just before or while in deep meditative states.
> Absorption concentration (appanasamadhi): The total immersion of the mind on its meditation of object and stabilization of all four jhanas.

[[PANNA OR WISDOM]]
> 
> Finally we come to Wisdom or understanding. This primarily refers to discerning the Four Noble Truths, another subject which we will definitely cover a lot in this group.
> 
> There are three types of paññā.
> 
> Learned paññā (Pāli: suta-maya-paññā), or knowledge or wisdom that is acquired from books or listening to others.
> 
> Reflective paññā (Pāli: cinta-maya-paññā), or knowledge or wisdom that is acquired from thought or logic and reasoning.
> 
> And plain Paññā which comes from spiritual development (Pāli: bhāvanā-maya-paññā), or knowledge or wisdom that is acquired from direct spiritual experience. This category of knowledge is produced from higher meditative absorptions.
> 
> Thai Buddhist monk and meditation master Ajahn Lee, who is one of the primary teachers of Thanissaro Bhikkhu, classifies the first two types of paññā as Dhamma on the theory level and the last as Dhamma on the practice level. Ajahn Lee states that this results in two levels of paññā: mundane paññā which is the comprehension of worldly and Dhamma subjects, and transcendent paññā which is an awareness of the supramundane that is realized by enlightened beings.
> 
> In the commentaries it is described that there are seven ways to gain paññā.
> 1. Asking a wise person
> 2. Keeping things clean
> 3. Balancing the five faculties (faith, energy, mindfulness, concentration and wisdom)
> 4. Avoiding foolish people
> 5. Associating with wise people
> 6. Reflecting on and analyzing the Dhamma
> 7. Having the mind inclined towards developing wisdom

See also wise attention, samprajanna, 
NOTE: Of these five faculties, wisdom is the "chief" (agga).

A faculty is a noun which means 
A) an inherent mental or physical power.
Similar: power, capability, capacity, facility, potential, propensity, wherewithal,
means, preparedness, senses, wits, reason, intelligence

C) a group of university departments concerned with a major division of knowledge. Like "the Faculty of Arts and Sciences"

We can generate energy or use persistence or perseverance (viriya) to motivate ourselves  when our motivation is lack (also a hinderance)

stillness of the mind (samādhi) - can provide clarity - like a merky pond that is allowed to settle - to clear up. It is peaceful and can provide us with a sense of strength and enegy.  

With this stillness Wisdom will develop and when combined with mindfulness and faith and energy can steer us toward understanding the truth of the dhamma, to our suffering, to the ending of our suffering, to realizing the end of our suffering and to staying on the path.  We can be a light for ourselves and for others.
