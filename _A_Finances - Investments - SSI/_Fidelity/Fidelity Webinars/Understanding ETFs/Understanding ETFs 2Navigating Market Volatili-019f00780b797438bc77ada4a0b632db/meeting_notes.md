### Outline
### Minimum Volatility Strategies as a Risk Management Tool

- Designed to help investors stay allocated to equities while reducing the impact of market drawdowns, min-vol strategies are built by analyzing how stocks behave historically, including their individual movements and correlations to one another.

- Portfolio construction under this approach focuses on optimizing for stability rather than maximizing returns, with the goal of smoothing the investment experience by losing less during downturns.

- Limiting downside is emphasized as a key advantage, supported by the mathematical principle that a 50% loss requires a 100% gain to recover, making loss reduction critical to long-term performance.

- Real market stress scenarios are used to illustrate the approach, with one example showing USMV declining only 0.1% during the US-Iran Strait of Hormuz shock compared to a 2.4% decline in the S&P 500.

- USMV is highlighted as a rules-based ETF that starts with the broad market and optimizes for lower volatility, having delivered meaningfully lower volatility than the S&P 500 without eliminating risk entirely.

### Buffer ETFs for Defined Outcome Investing

- Buffer ETFs use options to provide a defined level of downside protection in exchange for capping upside participation over a set outcome period, making them suitable for risk-sensitive investors who still want equity exposure.

- A 10% buffer structure is designed to absorb the first 10% of losses, while deeper structures such as a 100% or max buffer are designed to protect against essentially all losses over the outcome period, net of fees.

- More protection comes with a lower upside cap, while less protection allows for greater upside participation, making the trade-off between protection and growth potential a central consideration.

- These products are designed for investors who plan to hold through the full outcome period in order to achieve the defined experience they are structured to provide.

- Outcome periods reset at specific dates, such as the end of June, at which point new caps are set and current cap levels can be reviewed on the iShares website.

### Dollar Cost Averaging as a Behavioral Investment Discipline

- Dollar cost averaging involves investing a consistent amount on a regular schedule, such as weekly or monthly, regardless of market conditions, removing the emotional burden of timing investment decisions.

- When markets rise, contributions participate in that growth, and when markets fall, the same contribution purchases more shares, which can improve the average entry point over time.

- A concrete example comparing a $1,200 lump sum investment versus $100 per month illustrates that systematic investing can generate a profit even in a flat market with a one-year return of 0%.

- Consistency of contribution is presented as more important than predicting market direction, with the principle that time in the market matters more than timing the market.

### Portfolio Rebalancing to Maintain Risk Alignment

- Over time, strong performance in certain asset classes can cause a portfolio to drift away from its original allocation, resulting in unintended increases in risk exposure without any conscious decision by the investor.

- Rebalancing involves trimming positions that have grown beyond their target weight and adding to positions that have lagged, in order to return the portfolio to its intended risk profile.

- Discipline during market downturns is equally important, as rebalancing after a decline requires selling assets that feel safe and buying assets that are falling, which is psychologically difficult but strategically valuable.

- Investors who rebalanced during the COVID-19 market decline in spring 2020 by trimming bonds and buying equities were better positioned to benefit from the recovery that followed in 2021.

- Rebalancing can be handled automatically through core allocation ETFs or managed manually by reviewing the portfolio allocation a few times per year and adjusting as needed.

### Common Mistakes Made by New Investors

- The most common mistake observed among new investors is concentrating their portfolio in assets that have recently performed well, rather than maintaining broad diversification.

- Historical data supports the risk of concentration, with only one of the top ten US companies by market value in the year 2000 remaining in the top ten today, illustrating how past leadership does not guarantee future relevance.

- Diversification across US equities, international stocks, and bonds is identified as one of the most important foundations for investors who are just getting started.

### International Exposure in Portfolio Construction

- International allocations have received increased attention over the past 18 months due to strong performance in international markets and emerging opportunities tied to physical AI infrastructure buildout in markets such as Taiwan and South Korea.

- Despite tactical reasons to consider international exposure, it is viewed primarily as a core long-term allocation rather than a short-term or opportunistic position.

- Client portfolios tend to underweight international equities, making it an area where increased allocation is generally recommended as part of a diversified long-term strategy.

### ETF Liquidity and Trading Volume Considerations

- ETF liquidity operates on two levels, the trading volume of the ETF itself and the liquidity of the underlying securities it holds, both of which are relevant when evaluating how easily a position can be entered or exited.

- Institutional investors trading large volumes have different liquidity requirements than individual retail investors, meaning the relevance of trading volume varies depending on the size and nature of the investment.

- Understanding equity order types, including market orders and limit orders, becomes increasingly important when investing in ETFs with relatively lower trading volume compared to similar funds with equivalent exposure.

### ETF Expense Ratios and Cost Structure

- Expense ratios are expressed as a percentage of the fund's assets and are accrued daily from the net asset value of the fund, requiring no direct action from the investor.

- Lower expense ratios allow investors to retain more of their returns, while higher expense ratios reduce net growth over time, making cost an important factor in fund selection.

- Broad market and core allocation ETFs are specifically designed with cost efficiency in mind, while more specialized strategies may carry higher expense ratios that reflect the complexity of the approach.

### Tax Considerations for International ETFs

- Holding an ETF that contains international stocks allows a professional management team to handle currency conversions and dividend processing on behalf of the investor.

- Dividends from international holdings within an ETF are distributed in US dollars in the same manner as dividends from domestic stocks, simplifying the investor experience.

- Consulting a tax professional is recommended for any specific tax-related questions to ensure that individual circumstances are properly addressed.

### Evaluating and Comparing Minimum Volatility ETFs

- Upside and downside capture ratios are among the most useful metrics when comparing min-vol funds, as they show how much of the broader market's gains and losses a fund captures relative to a benchmark.

- Min-vol ETFs are generally expected to capture more of the market's upside than its downside, which represents the core investment thesis behind this category of funds.

- Correlation to the broader market is another key metric, as it helps investors assess how much genuine diversification benefit a min-vol fund provides relative to a standard equity allocation.
