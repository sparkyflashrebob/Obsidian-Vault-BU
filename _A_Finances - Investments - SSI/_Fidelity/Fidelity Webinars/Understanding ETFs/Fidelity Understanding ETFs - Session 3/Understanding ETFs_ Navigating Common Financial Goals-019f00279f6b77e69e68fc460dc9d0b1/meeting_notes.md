### Outline
### Overview of ETFs as an Investment Vehicle

- Exchange-traded funds are increasingly becoming the investment vehicle of choice for a wide range of investors, functioning like mutual funds that trade on exchanges the way stocks do.

- Both ETFs and mutual funds are professionally managed baskets of securities, but unlike mutual funds, ETFs can be bought or sold throughout the trading day.

- Lower fees compared to comparable mutual funds and a more tax-efficient structure have contributed to ETFs being widely adopted, particularly in taxable accounts.

- More than $15 trillion in ETFs are listed in the US today, with over 5,000 options available for investors to choose from.

- Newer additions to the market include options-based ETFs designed to meet different income needs or manage risk, as well as actively managed ETFs focused on rapidly evolving themes.

### Using ETFs to Seek Regular Income

- The common sources of investment income span a spectrum from cash and savings vehicles to bonds, covered call strategies, and dividend-paying stocks, each carrying different levels of risk, yield, and return potential.

- Cash and savings vehicles are generally suited for short-term objectives such as meeting everyday living expenses, while longer-term or larger spending goals often lead investors toward investment vehicles with higher risk and return potential.

- Inflation is a particularly important concept for income investors, as the goal is not only to keep up with current spending rates but to preserve purchasing power over time.

- Traditional savings vehicles such as average savings accounts yield less than half of 1%, and CDs remain below the expected inflation rate of approximately 2.5% over the next five years, meaning cash holdings may lose value in real terms.

- SGOV, an iShares ETF holding Treasury bills, currently yields approximately 3.5% and is widely used as an alternative to traditional savings accounts for excess cash.

- Two primary risks associated with bond strategies are credit risk, which is the risk that a borrower does not repay, and interest rate risk, which reflects the potential regret of locking in a rate before rates move higher.

- SGOV limits both risks by investing exclusively in Treasury bills backed by the US government and by holding only bills maturing within three months, allowing the portfolio to reset relatively quickly as interest rates change.

### Enhanced Income Strategies Using Covered Call ETFs

- Covered call strategies generally own an underlying investment and sell call options against it, exchanging some upside potential for higher income today.

- TLTW is an iShares ETF that holds long-term Treasury bonds and writes call options on TLT to seek additional income, currently yielding around 10%.

- Covered call strategies applied to bond ETFs can be particularly attractive for income-focused investors because bonds generally have less upside potential than equities to begin with.

- IVBW follows a similar covered call approach but is applied to the S&P 500, yielding approximately 15%, though the upside tradeoff is more meaningful given equities' historical reliance on price appreciation.

- BALI holds a diversified basket of US stocks including companies like Nvidia and Apple, uses an options overlay to seek enhanced income, and targets a 7% annual yield floor while still seeking to participate in some equity market upside.

- Over the last 12 months, the S&P 500 returned approximately 18% while BALI returned approximately 17% with a yield of around 8%, illustrating its balance between income and growth participation.

### Dividend-Focused ETF Strategies

- Dividend-paying stocks may offer higher growth potential than other income strategies but carry greater risk, though established dividend payers have historically held up better during turbulent markets.

- Some dividend-focused strategies prioritize higher income today, while others focus on companies that have been growing their dividends, with the latter being more oriented toward total return and increasing income over time.

- DGRO, the iShares Core Dividend Growth ETF, screens for US companies that have grown their dividends for at least five consecutive years and applies additional quality filters such as sustainable payout ratios and positive earnings expectations, resulting in a portfolio of approximately 400 holdings.

- HDV, the iShares Core High Dividend ETF, screens for higher-quality US stocks that pay high dividends by evaluating financial strength and economic moat, ultimately selecting the 75 stocks with the highest dividend yields after those screens.

- Both DGRO and HDV distribute dividends to investors on a regular basis and incorporate quality screens as a shared feature.

- Focusing exclusively on dividend yield when evaluating dividend stocks or funds can be misleading, as a rising yield may reflect a falling stock price driven by financial stress rather than improving fundamentals, a situation referred to as a yield trap.

- Quality screens that focus on profitability and strong balance sheets help reduce the likelihood of selecting companies whose dividends may be at risk, and many dividend ETFs such as DGRO and HDV perform this analysis automatically.

### ETFs for Planning Around Known Future Spending Milestones

- When planning for known future expenses such as education costs, home purchases, or renovations, the investment challenge shifts from generating regular cash flow to targeting a specific amount of money at a specific future date.

- Term maturity ETFs, referred to by iShares as iBonds, are designed to mimic the lifecycle of buying and holding individual bonds while offering the diversification expected from an ETF structure.

- IBDW, the iShares 2031 Corporate iBond ETF, holds hundreds of investment-grade corporate bonds all maturing in 2031, pays monthly income, and returns the final net asset value per share to investors when the ETF delists at maturity.

- Unlike traditional bond funds, which fluctuate in price and lack a stated maturity, iBond ETFs provide a stated maturity year and an annual yield target, allowing investors to plan around a specific future date.

- IBDW currently yields approximately 4.88%, and a $100,000 investment earning 5% annually could grow by nearly $30,000 over a five-year period.

- iShares offers corporate iBond ETFs across maturities extending up to ten years into the future, along with Treasury iBond ETFs covering a similar range, and also provides iBond ETFs holding municipal bonds for tax-exempt income and high-yield bonds for higher income potential.

- Yields for all available iBond ETFs are updated daily and accessible on ishares.com, enabling investors to match specific ETFs or combinations of ETFs to specific future spending needs.

### ETFs for Retirement Planning

- Retirement is often the largest financial goal investors plan for, requiring tools that offer long-term growth potential, risk management, and income support across what may be a multi-decade period.

- Investing $250 per month into the S&P 500 over the last 40 years would have grown a $120,000 total contribution to approximately $1.8 million, illustrating the power of time and compounding.

- IVV, the iShares S&P 500 ETF, is commonly used by younger investors as a broad market foundation, with regular contributions that can be automated through platforms such as Fidelity to align with incoming paychecks.

- As investors approach retirement or begin relying on their portfolio for income, it generally makes sense to reduce risk and shift toward a more balanced allocation using low-cost core ETFs like IVV alongside bond market ETFs like AGG.

- Target date ETFs simplify the process of managing portfolio allocation over time by starting with a diversified portfolio weighted toward stocks and gradually shifting toward bonds and cash-flowing securities as the target retirement year approaches.

- Reaching the target date does not result in a 100% bond or cash allocation, as iShares target date ETFs maintain roughly 40% in stocks at that point to support diversification, provide growth potential, and help investors keep pace with inflation.

- iShares offers ten vintages of target date ETFs, ranging from ITDV designed for investors targeting retirement around 2030 to ITJ designed for those targeting 2070, with each fund ultimately merging into IRTR, the iShares LifePath Retirement ETF, upon reaching its stated year.

### Buffer ETFs for Equity Market Participation With Downside Protection

- Buffer ETFs are designed to help investors remain invested in the equity market while providing defined guardrails, offering downside protection in exchange for a cap on upside potential over a specified outcome period.

- Generally, the greater the downside protection offered by a buffer ETF, the lower the corresponding upside cap tends to be.

- iShares Max Buffer ETFs are designed to protect against 100% of losses if held over a full twelve-month outcome period before fees, using put options to create downside protection and selling call options to fund that protection, which creates the upside cap.

- MXJJ, the Max Buffer ETF that resets every June, offered a maximum upside of 7.3% over its most recent twelve-month outcome period.

- iShares also offers a suite of buffer ETFs that seek to protect against only the first 10% of losses, resulting in a higher upside cap of approximately 11.4% over the same period due to the reduced level of downside protection.

- Buffer ETFs are most suitable for investors who want to remain invested in equities and participate in some market upside while having a more defined tradeoff between downside protection and upside participation.

### Using Thematic ETFs to Pursue Long-Term Growth

- Broad equity ETFs such as IVV serve as a powerful starting point for investors seeking to participate in the long-term growth of the US equity market.

- ETFs also allow investors to target specific themes that are shaping markets today and may continue to drive growth over time, providing more precision than broad market exposure alone.

- Artificial intelligence is one of the most prominent current themes, and AIAI is an ETF that holds companies across the AI value chain, including Micron, Nvidia, and even private companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic that have not yet gone public.

- SOXX is an ETF focused specifically on the semiconductor chips enabling the AI buildout, offering more targeted exposure within the broader AI theme.

- Innovation is not limited to US companies, and international ETFs such as IXUS and EEM allow investors to access global players like Samsung, Alibaba, and Taiwan Semiconductor, with EEM focused on emerging markets and IXUS covering the broader international opportunity.

- Infrastructure is increasingly tied to the physical systems needed to support a digital and AI-driven economy, while defense is broadening into areas such as space and cybersecurity, both representing themes with dedicated ETF coverage.

- ETFs are particularly well-suited for thematic investing because identifying the right trend is often easier than selecting the right individual company, and thematic ETFs allow investors to gain exposure to a theme without relying on a single stock pick.

### Tax Efficiency of ETFs Compared to Mutual Funds

- ETFs have a structural advantage over mutual funds in that investors buying and selling ETFs largely transact with each other on exchanges, meaning fewer changes need to occur within the underlying fund itself.

- Less internal trading within an ETF means fewer securities are sold at a gain inside the fund, resulting in fewer taxable capital gain distributions to investors.

- Last year, 53% of mutual funds paid a capital gain distribution compared to only 7% of ETFs, demonstrating the structural tax efficiency advantage of ETFs in practice.

- ETFs made up approximately 35% of all managed fund assets yet accounted for a disproportionately small share of capital gains distributions made by the fund industry.

- Investors in ETFs may have greater control over when they choose to realize capital gains, since gains are generally not distributed until the investor decides to sell their shares, making ETFs particularly advantageous in taxable accounts.

### Tax Treatment of Treasury and Municipal Bond ETFs

- The tax treatment of income generated through an ETF mirrors the tax treatment of the underlying securities held within it.

- Treasury income flowing through a Treasury iBond ETF is taxed in the same manner as if the investor held the Treasury bond directly, which includes the potential exemption from state taxes applicable to individual Treasuries.

- Investors seeking tax-exempt income can achieve a similar outcome by owning a municipal bond ETF, as the tax-exempt nature of the underlying muni bonds is preserved through the ETF structure.

### Net Asset Value Versus Market Price in ETFs

- The net asset value of an ETF is calculated by dividing the total market value of the fund's holdings by the number of ETF shares outstanding, and for mutual funds this is the price at which transactions are struck at end of day.

- ETFs have an additional layer in the form of a market price, which is the price at which investors actually buy and sell shares throughout the trading day.

- For most ETFs, the net asset value and the market price are extremely similar or identical, as the structure of ETFs provides little reason for a meaningful divergence between the two.

- A notable exception can occur with ETFs holding international securities, such as Japanese stocks, where there is no overlap in trading hours between the underlying market and the US exchange on which the ETF trades.

- In such cases, the ETF's market price can adjust to reflect new information affecting the value of the underlying foreign securities before the net asset value is updated, resulting in a temporary divergence between the two figures.

- Investors should focus on the market price when transacting in ETFs, as that is the actual price at which shares are bought and sold.

### Target Date ETFs in Retirement Rather Than Only Leading Up to It

- Target date ETFs are designed not only to help investors reach retirement but to support them through what may be a multi-year or multi-decade retirement period.

- Holding only cash during retirement exposes investors to the risk that inflation erodes purchasing power more quickly than cash savings tools can keep pace with, whereas maintaining some equity exposure can help offset that risk.

- Maintaining diversification throughout retirement, including a smaller but meaningful allocation to equities and growth assets, is considered a sound approach to managing the portfolio during the retirement years.

- Sequencing risk refers to the risk that retirement outcomes differ significantly depending on whether markets are strong or weak at the time an investor begins drawing down their portfolio, a factor entirely outside the investor's control.

- Keeping some assets in growth-oriented investments during retirement helps mitigate sequencing risk by preserving the potential for the portfolio to recover and continue growing alongside the investor's needs.
