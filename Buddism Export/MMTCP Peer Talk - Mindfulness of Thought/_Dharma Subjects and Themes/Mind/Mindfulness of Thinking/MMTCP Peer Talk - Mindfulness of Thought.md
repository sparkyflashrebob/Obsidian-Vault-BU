MMTCP - MINDFULNESS OF THOUGHT

I’d like to thank Tressa for providing an overview of Mindfulness last month. Today, I will expand upon this - focusing on Mindfulness of Thought.

As Tressa reminded us, Mindfulness is an ancient practice that is part of a __set of teachings__ attributed to Siddhartha Gautama, who we know as the Buddha, who lived around 2500 years ago in the area that is now part of northern India.

Today Mindfulness has reached the West and is quickly spreading. There are many reasons for this. Mindfulness is thought of as a means of being better able to focus our attention in the present moment, as a way of reducing stress, and as a way to help us maintain a more emotionally balanced presence within ourselves. It is being taught and practiced in elementary schools, senior centers, prisons, and the workplace. This is especially important for our global society.  The pace of the world has picked up dramatically.  We are instantly connected to the rest of the world with the internet.  And we can all feel the weight of this collective momentum we are experiencing.

Mindfulness is also used as a core principle in treating those with more difficult mental afflictions. Today there is an alphabet of effective therapies that use Mindfulness as its foundation - from MBCT - Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy, to MBSR - for stress reduction, MBPM - for pain management, ACT - acceptance and commitment therapy, DBT for personality disorders, and others.

People are often drawn to Mindfulness because they recognize something's missing in their lives. Mindfulness is part of a series of different teachings used to cultivate spiritual awakening - connecting us with practices that foster greater kindness, compassion, joy, and balance that promote a greater sense of well-being and connectedness for both ourselves as well as the well-being of our greater society.

Currently, we are having an epidemic of thinking. We identify with our thinking as who we are and believe that our thoughts reflect the way the world really is. Because we believe this - that the world is the way we think of it, then those who do not believe as we do are wrong - that they are not seeing the world correctly.

In fact, you might say that we are in the midst of a “war on thought and truth.” To get our views and opinions and beliefs accepted as fact.  Society is faced with looking at what is considered to be true and looking at what was considered true from our past. I feel that this is and we good for our society to do so in the long run, but this will take some time to work through. We will experience growing pains.  It can make us uncomfortable and many of us would rather avoid it.

Thinking is built into our language, our culture, and our society. And it has been this way since human beings came into being.  To be with like-minded people brings us comfort and safety. We are taught to trust in our thinking, to be better thinkers.  Those who are thought to be better thinkers are thought to be better than others in many ways and can threaten our way of thinking due to our own self-judgment of worthiness.

There is an epidemic of thought and great effort is placed in the means to manipulate how we think. Incessant commercialization and advertising - Campaigning - use of fear tactics - purposely meant to evoke feelings in us and our natural desire for us to be happy.  Tempting us into taking actions that entice and promise us that we will be healthier and happier, to experience the good life, to get you to believe in their way of thinking.  And there is the backlash to all this.
America is based on a set of ideals - idealism - the ideal society - that it’s fair, and just, and equal. But it’s far from that in reality.  Each of us can see the results of this.  We are not at peace.  Happiness is fleeting.

Ajahn Chah said, "We human beings are in conflict in battle, at war with so many things with one another-- with our ideas, with our ideals, with our wishes, with our hopes. We're in conflict with when it's too hot, and when it's too cold, and how it tastes, and how it sounds, and how they are."  "Why not step out of the saddle, step off the battlefield? Stop the war? Find the place
of peace that's beyond and around, that's larger than all those conflicts. Why not live in peace?"

We have not thought to question thinking itself - to question our way of thinking and the results of our thinking. To look at the harm that our way of thinking causes. Not looking at how we talk to ourselves - how we talk to others….. Because we __identify__ with our of thinking. We see our thoughts as who we are. Instead of looking at the impact of our thoughts and way of thinking, actually prefer to entrench ourselves in it - to justify what we say to others.  Engaging in countless forms of biases - mostly unconscious but some not.  Never looking at our own biases, views, opinions, and belief systems.

Most, if not all of the events in our lives are out of our individual control. ** Things happen and we experience them, from within and without. **The battle is less __in__ what happens to us but more about how we respond. And most, if not all of this, depends upon how we think of ourselves and our life experience - individually and as a collective.

Viktor Frankl said that there are “forces beyond your control that can take away everything you possess except one thing, your freedom to choose how you will respond to the situation.”
Many do not realize that this freedom exists and choose not to exercise it.  Lost in the illusion of thinking.

But many of us are starting to wake up and are choosing to seek another way. Looking to awaken from this illusion of deluded thinking - to come into balance with the truth of the way life is.  In order to find this, __we have to look within__. This is the encouragement of our practice - in meditation and in life itself. To realize that contentment and freedom lies in each moment. This is the path that this practice of loving- awareness provides us.  This is a path that is available to each and every one of us- that is available in each thought and in every moment.

It doesn’t matter was how intellectual you are, your IQ, or your race, or gender, your financial status.  In fact, thinking often gets in the way -  and we can spend lots of money on therapy working to fix our thinking. But the issue is not thinking, but how we use thought.

The Buddha said that “Whatever we think and ponder about will become the inclination of the mind.”

“If a person speaks or acts with a harmful thought, trouble follows that person as the wheel follows the ox that draws the cart.  If a person speaks or acts with a harmonious thought, happiness follows that person as their own shadow, never leaving them.”

The good news is that through these practices - with loving-mindful-awareness,  we can look at our ways of thinking and experiencing life and use this as a path to our liberation.

There are a few ways Mindfulness of thought can help us.  The first, most basic, and most direct is to actually begin to observe our thoughts - to turn towards them. As we learn how to watch our thoughts we can begin to see patterns and ways of thinking within ourselves that we were unaware of previously.

Yet, if we identify with what we see, this can be very disturbing.  That is because human beings primarily create a sense of who they are mostly from identifying with thoughts, feelings, and emotions. They become “our thoughts”, “our feelings”, “our emotions”. We take ownership. We get lost in them.

We see our memories as our memories, instead of just memories.  We see beliefs as “our beliefs” instead of just beliefs.  Likewise with our views, and opinions, and other mental constructs.  Identifying them as who we are instead of as processes that are part of being human - of having a brain - to being a sensitive being with a mind that can conceive of anything.

This can be difficult because of our tendency of identifying with wanting to experience only pleasurable experiences and not experience unpleasurable experiences. Many people gauge their ability to meditate based on not being able to shut off the stream of thoughts they are having – not realizing that this is not the goal of practice.  Thinking occurs based on conditions.

One of the foundational teachings of the Buddha is summarized in the statement,

When this is, that is.
From the arising of this comes the arising of that.
When this isn't, that isn't.
From the cessation of this comes the cessation of that.

This is the teaching of the interdependence of all things.

So how does this play out?  Any reactive thoughts or emotions that arise cease when their causes and conditions are finished. We perceive life through our senses - by seeing, hearing, tasting, touching, and thinking itself.  As we sense the world, one of the first things that the brain does is recognize this information thru perception. Perception labels what we are experiencing.  This labeling comes in the form of thoughts. From there a whole range of other thought generation takes place.  This includes more thoughts, such as memories, but also emotions, feelings, and sensations that we also perceive.

I can say the word “orange” and can create thoughts in your head. This is an example of hearing the word “orange”.  Likewise, when we think of our mother - the thought is not your mother. Our thoughts are not the world.

But, through the practice, we being to see the thoughts as they arise and pass away - observing the patterns and ways of thinking can turn into insight.  Not only that, but each moment is different with a different set of conditions. We might meditate one moment and experience one train of thought then, even a few microseconds later, we can experience a whole new set of thoughts. They are here and then gone. Impermanent and changing.  New condition, new thoughts.  There is no such thing as a permanent thought.  Whatever things originate, all those things will cease.

We can begin to notice that most of our thoughts are reruns, and have specific ways of expressing themselves - habitual ways of thinking. This is the cause.  We have our “preferences.”

As we step back and observe our thinking, it can appear that the thoughts are thinking themselves - coming seemingly out of nowhere and going the same way - just to be replaced by another.  With these insights, we can give space to the thinking process. Thinking happens and, yet, we can be the knower of our thinking. Gaining an understanding that we are not our thoughts. This can be a powerful practice for us.

It is the ground and yet mindfulness is just part of the many practices we can use to wake up - To be able to rest in and be with the things as they are. When we are mindful then we can use other skillful ways as looking at our experience.  If we are not mindful then we are pulled around by our individual and societal conditioning - our habitual ways of being.  When we are mindful then other factors can come into play - we have other options.

One of them is to actually use our minds and the thinking process itself to investigate our direct experience in more detail.  Directing our thinking instead of not paying attention to how we are thinking. We can actually direct our minds and our thoughts to investigate our direct experience more deeply.
One of the ways this can be used in meditation is to use this power of perception to softy label what are aware of and what is arising in us in the moment.  This can help us to focus on and connect with what is happening in the moment. To a direct knowing that is beyond what we think about it. So being curious about how we experience reality becomes another factor in our awakening.

With mindfulness and investigation, we can look at the qualities of the mind itself. To know our own state of mind - know what mood we are in, or whether we are bright or dull. Seeing how our moods color the mind and our thinking patterns. To see how our state of mind conditions the type of thinking that arises.

We can use mindfulness and our curiosity to recognize what is present and learn how to counteract those aspects of thinking and being that hinder our peace, contentment and happiness - that are unhealthy for us.  We can bring awareness to habitual and unconscious thought patterns such as the push and pull of only wanting certain sense experiences, like wanting warm weather, or good food, or to be around our loved ones.  Or the opposite, our resistance to life experiences that we don’t like - like being around those that we don’t like or disagree with - or to thoughts you do not like 0- that are painful or difficult. Likewise with being depressed or unmotivated, or experiencing restlessness, or worry, or getting caught in negative perspectives and views, or being in fear. Or being caught in obsessive thinking, judging, or ways of avoiding being present - jumping from one thought to the next.  Not stopping to smell the roses, or breath in the fresh air, to open to the world of wonder that exists with the difficulties.

Through mindful loving awareness, we can stop feeding these habitual patterns of thinking and being and can come into a place of peace and contentment that is present in the now.  Come to this place of knowing.  Understanding that there is praise and blame, gain and loss, pleasure and pain, joy and sorrow. This is all part of life. Learning to stop trying to control life - but learning to act instead of reacting - acting from a place of wisdom.  To direct our energy towards the type of thinking that brings about more peace and contentment in our lives.

This is the gift of practice.  The incentive to practice.  This would be a most effective way of being. This is a cause for happiness. This can help the world to be a better place. This can lead to our benefit and to the benefit of others.  By changing our ways of thinking in this way, we change our view of the world, and when we do this the world itself changes.

Your happiness, contentment, and peace depends on your thoughts and actions and not in my, or anyone elses wishes for you.
