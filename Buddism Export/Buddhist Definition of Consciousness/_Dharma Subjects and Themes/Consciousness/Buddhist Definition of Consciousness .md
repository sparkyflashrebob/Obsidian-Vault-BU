In Buddhism, there are different types or levels of consciousness that are believed to exist. These include:

1. Sensory consciousness (Pali: cakkhu-viññāṇa, sota-viññāṇa, ghāna-viññāṇa, jivhā-viññāṇa, kāya-viññāṇa): This is the consciousness that arises when we experience something through our senses. It is often described as a "bare awareness" or a "simple apprehension" of an object.

2. Mental consciousness (Pali: mano-viññāṇa): This is the consciousness that is involved in thinking, imagining, and other mental activities. It is sometimes called the "discursive consciousness" because it involves concepts, ideas, and judgments.

3. Store consciousness (Pali: ālaya-viññāṇa): This is the consciousness that stores all the experiences, habits, and tendencies that we have accumulated over our lifetimes. It is sometimes compared to a "storehouse" or a "warehouse" of our past experiences.

4. Consciousness of dependent origination (Pali: paṭicca-samuppāda-viññāṇa): This is the consciousness that arises in dependence on other factors, such as our sense organs, the objects of our senses, and our mental activities. It is believed to be the basis for our sense of self or ego.

5. Pure consciousness (Pali: suddha-viññāṇa): This is a state of consciousness that is free from all mental activities and is believed to be the ultimate state of enlightenment or liberation.

It is important to note that in Buddhism, consciousness is not seen as a permanent or unchanging entity, but rather as a constantly changing and impermanent process. The ultimate goal of Buddhist practice is to develop insight into the true nature of consciousness and to transcend it altogether, in order to achieve liberation from suffering.
