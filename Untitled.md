# Right in Fact, Wrong in Dhamma: When Winning the Argument Costs Us Our Humanity

September 29, 2026

---

We live in a culture profoundly addicted to being right. 

From cable news panels and social media threads to dinner table debates and legislative chambers, our public life often resembles an unrelenting courtroom trial. But beneath the noisy rhetoric, this theater is driven by deep ulterior motives—primarily corporate greed, political ambition, and algorithms engineered to profit by feeding on other people’s fear and vulnerability. We scour the internet for data points, weaponize headlines, and convene an immediate "Judge and Jury" inside our heads. The goal is rarely mutual understanding or healing; the goal is to feed the machine of outrage, crush the opponent, win the verdict, and walk away feeling morally superior.

Yet, in our desperate rush to be right, a quiet, devastating tragedy occurs: we lose track of what is true, and we completely abandon what is beneficial.

Decades ago, in the forests of northeast Thailand, a young American monk named Sumedho found himself burning with righteous indignation. Another monk in the monastery had a loud, abrasive mouth, constantly upsetting people and violating social decorum. When their teacher, Ajahn Chah, went away for a few days, Sumedho seized his moment. At a formal assembly of the Sangha, he stood up with dates, witnesses, and facts in hand. He laid out an airtight case against the offending monk. His evidence was undeniable; he was completely, factually correct.

The public confrontation was so humiliating that the criticized monk packed his bowl and robes that night, left the monastery, and was never seen again.

When Ajahn Chah returned and learned what had happened, he called young Sumedho in. Instead of praising his sharp eye for rules and accuracy, the master delivered a gentle, piercing rebuke:

> *“You know, Tan Sumedho, you did something very harmful there. His mouth is bad, but his heart is good. How many monasteries do you think the fellow had to leave before he came here? This was the one place where he could stay and practice, because I made space for him. Now you have closed that door on him... You were right in fact, but wrong in Dhamma.”*

That single phrase cuts to the heart of the human dilemma: **You can be right in fact, and yet completely wrong in Dhamma.**

## The Filter of Speech: When Facts Become Weapons

In Buddhist ethics, truth is considered a non-negotiable baseline. In the *Abhaya Sutta*, the Buddha laid out an uncompromising filter for communication. But he made a distinction that modern society almost entirely ignores: **truth alone is not a license to speak or act.**

The Buddha explained that even if a statement is 100% factual and true, if it is *not beneficial*—if it does not lead to the reduction of suffering, if it merely wounds, inflames, or destroys without healing—**the wise person does not say it.**

Truth must pass through the crucible of compassion and timing:
1. *Is it true?* (The factual foundation)
2. *Is it beneficial?* (Does it alleviate suffering, or does it merely gratify our ego?)
3. *Is the time and container right?* (Can the heart of the listener actually receive it?)

When we sever factual accuracy from goodwill, our facts turn into clubs. We say whatever cruel, sharp, or devastating thing crosses our minds, and then we hide behind the cowardly defense: *“Hey, I’m just being honest. It’s the truth!”* 

Truth divorced from care is not honesty; it is hostility in disguise.

## The "Judge and Jury" and the Demonization of the Other

This spiritual dysfunction does not remain confined to personal relationships; it scales up into our national politics, shaping policies that inflict staggering real-world harm.

Consider the ongoing rhetoric surrounding immigration. We are repeatedly fed an engineered story designed to provoke primal fear: that the people crossing our borders are "animals," "criminals," and "an invading army." The societal Judge and Jury bangs its gavel, declaring millions of human beings to be the enemy before their stories are ever heard.

People cling to this narrative with ferocious certainty. Yet, if we step out of ideological silos and look with honest eyes, what is the living reality? 

The reality is that our entire society is quietly carried by the labor of immigrant hands. They are the mothers and fathers out in the fields at dawn harvesting our lettuce, strawberries, and wheat. They are packing our meat, cleaning our hospital rooms, washing our dishes, and framing our homes. They are human beings carrying an extraordinary burden of sacrifice simply to give their children a future free from gang violence or starvation.

Yet, under the banner of legalistic "rightness," we witness policies that tear toddlers from their mothers' arms, break long-standing community roots, and subject vulnerable people to cruel and unusual conditions. 

Those who enforce these measures may point to a statute book and claim they are "legally right." But from the vantage point of the heart, tearing a family apart is an atrocity. It is **right in legalistic code, but profoundly wrong in Dhamma.**

## The Rohinī River: Corporate Balance Sheets vs. Priceless Life

Over twenty-five hundred years ago, a brutal drought struck northern India. The river Rohinī began to dry up, leaving only a trickle of water between two neighboring clans—the Sakyans and the Koliyans. Farmers on both banks began to argue over who had the rightful claim to irrigate their crops. The dispute escalated from insults to threats, until royal armies mobilized on opposing riverbanks, armed with bows, spears, and chariots, poised for mutual slaughter.

The Buddha saw the impending catastrophe, walked out into the middle of the riverbed, and placed himself directly between the battle lines. He asked the assembled kings:

> *“Tell me, great kings: What is the market value of water?”*  
> *“Very little, Blessed One,”* they answered.  
> *“And what is the value of human blood, of human life?”*  
> *“It is beyond measure, Blessed One. It is priceless.”*  
> *“Would you destroy that which is priceless for the sake of that which has so little value?”*

Ashamed by the clarity of the question, the warriors lowered their spears.

Look at our modern world through the lens of the Rohinī River. Today, water crises threaten entire continents, yet corporations pump fresh aquifers dry to sell bottled plastic or cool massive server farms. We watch corporate conglomerates and billionaires sacrifice the basic health, clean water, and livable wages of communities on the altar of quarterly balance sheets. 

Our economic experts argue endlessly about costs, profits, and property rights. They have a mountain of spreadsheets proving they are "fiduciarily right." But they have forgotten the fundamental question of the Buddha: **Would you sacrifice that which is priceless—the health, dignity, and survival of living beings—for that which has no ultimate value?**

An economy that demands the sacrifice of humanity to serve profit has completely lost its moral compass. A healthy society is built on collective stewardship for human flourishing, not on grinding the many to enrich the few.

## The Tribal Ledger of Pain: Gaza and Selective Grief

Nowhere is the poison of "being right" more catastrophic than in the horrors of war.

When we look at the devastation in Gaza and Palestine, the dominant political narratives immediately demand that we choose a camp. We are instructed to tally an ideological scoreboard—to grieve passionately for the innocent lives lost on one side, while coldly dismissing or rationalizing the slaughter of children, journalists, and families on the other side as "unfortunate collateral damage."

As Mahatma Gandhi famously warned, **"An eye for an eye leaves the whole world blind."**

When we reduce human tragedy to a ledger of retribution, vengeance masquerades as justice. But the Buddha reminded us in the *Dhammapada*: *“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By love alone is hatred appeased. This is an eternal law.”*

Boundless compassion (*Karuṇā*) does not carry a passport. It does not look at an ethnic background, a flag, or a religious creed before deciding whether a tear is worth shedding. A mother weeping over a lifeless child in Gaza is enduring the exact same unfathomable agony as a mother weeping over a child in Tel Aviv, Kyiv, or anywhere else on Earth. Every single life extinguished is a universe destroyed. 

If our political convictions require us to numb our hearts to the slaughter of another human being just so our "side" can maintain its moral righteousness, our convictions are poisonous.

## The Inverted Vessel: Shameless Lies and the Mirror of Action

Why has it become so easy for societies to drift into cruelty? 

The answer lies in our casual tolerance of deceit. In modern public life, lying has ceased to be a scandal; it has become an accepted strategy. Political actors fabricate blatant falsehoods about minority communities, elections, and world events with complete impunity—all to win an election or fuel an algorithmic outrage cycle.

When the Buddha’s seven-year-old son, Rāhula, told a deliberate lie as a childhood joke, the Buddha washed his feet using a water dipper. He left a tiny puddle of dirty water in the bottom and pointed to it:
*“Rāhula, do you see this little bit of leftover, dirty water? That is how little spiritual virtue is left in someone who feels no shame at telling a deliberate lie.”*

Then the Buddha threw the water away, turned the vessel upside down, and said:
*“Even so hollow and upside down is the life of someone who feels no shame at a lie. When someone feels no shame at a falsehood, there is no harm they are incapable of doing.”*

To protect his son from this descent, the Buddha gave him a simple, lifelong practice: **The Mirror of Action.**
> *“Rāhula, what is a mirror for?”*  
> *“For reflection, venerable sir.”*  
> *“In the exact same way, before you speak or act, while you speak or act, and after you speak or act, you must reflect: **Does this decision cause harm to myself, harm to others, or harm to both?** If it leads to harm, you must not do it.”*

Notice the Buddha did not tell Rāhula to ask: *“Will this win the debate? Will this make me look powerful? Does this satisfy my ego?”*  
He said: **Does it cause harm?**

## Refusing the Gift of Hatred

When we see the magnitude of societal injustice, fear and fury naturally arise. But how do we engage without becoming the very bitterness we are fighting?

In the *Akkosa Sutta*, a furious Brahmin approached the Buddha and showered him with vicious, personal curses. The Buddha waited patiently until the storm passed, and then asked:
*“When guests visit your house and you offer them food, if they do not accept the food, to whom does it belong?”*  
The Brahmin replied: *“It remains with us; it belongs to us.”*  
The Buddha smiled: *“In the same way, Brahmin, the abuse, insults, and hatred you offer me—I do not accept them. They remain entirely yours. Whoever insults back is eating at the same table with you. But I am not sharing your meal.”*

This is the great teaching for our time. The outrage machine relies on us accepting its poison and returning fire. When we retaliate with matching cruelty, we are pulling up a chair and eating at the table of hatred.

Refusing the gift does not mean being passive. It means standing up for immigrant families, speaking out against corporate plunder, and demanding an end to imperial war with a mind rooted in clarity, courage, and universal care.

## The Courage to Empty the Boat

We can spend our entire lives clutching our rigid opinions, banging the gavel of our internal Judge and Jury, and insisting that the world acknowledge how right we are. But when we come to the end of our days, no one will care how many internet arguments we won or how tightly we guarded our pride.

The only questions that will echo in the quiet of the heart are the ones that truly matter:
- *Did we alleviate suffering, or did we add our own fear to an already burning room?*
- *Did we recognize the priceless value of the living beings sharing this fragile planet with us?*
- *Did we have the courage to put down the heavy stone of being "right," so our hands could be free to help?*

It is time to put away the courtroom gavel. It is time to look into the mirror of reflection, refuse the toxic gifts of our age, and begin planting the causes of healing.
